# Feedback: Chapter 28 — Quantitative Bias Analysis

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part6/28-qba.qmd`

## Overall impression

I am a health researcher who has run a few MAICs and read a couple of NMA papers, and I picked up this chapter hoping to learn what "QBA" means in practice. The chapter is clearly written by a mathematician for mathematicians: the prose is elegant, every bound is proved, and there is a genuine worked example at the end. But for someone like me, it lands as a wall of symbols with the ITC motivation bolted on at the front and then largely abandoned for thirty pages. I finished the E-value section genuinely unsure how I would compute one for my own trial, and the NORTA section reads like a probability lecture rather than a practical HTA tool. The single worked example (Section 6) is the only place I felt grounded, and it comes after five sections of abstract derivation. I think the chapter is recoverable for a reader like me, but it needs an interleaved structure (motivation, formula, example, repeat) and a great deal more domain framing.

## What was unclear

- **`@sec-qba-intro` (lines 5-59).** The opening tells me "every method up to now has removed bias by measuring the variable that causes it." As a noob, I do not have the prior chapters' structure in my head. Which methods? A one-line table (MAIC = reweight, STC = outcome model, ML-NMR = integrate) would have saved me from flipping back. The phrase "conditional constancy of *absolute* effects and balance of every imbalanced *prognostic* variable, not merely the effect modifiers" (@thm-unanchored-paic-consistency) is the single most important sentence for the chapter and it is dropped in as a cross-reference with no plain-language restatement. I had to stop and ask: what is the difference between a prognostic variable and an effect modifier again? That distinction is load-bearing for the whole chapter and it is not re-explained here.

- **`@sec-qba-intro` line 39-45, the NORTA preview.** "The NORTA (normal-to-anything) construction, which imputes the unreported joint distribution of the comparator's covariates by gluing the reported margins to a dependence structure borrowed from the IPD through a Gaussian copula." I read this sentence five times. As a health researcher I do not know what a "margin" is in this sense, what "gluing" means, or why a Gaussian copula is the right glue. This is the first time the reader meets "margin" used as a distribution term rather than a percentage, and it is undefined.

- **`@def-quantitative-bias-analysis` (lines 69-88).** The definition writes $\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}=\theta\cdot B(\mathbf{b})$ (multiplicative) or $\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}=\theta + B(\mathbf{b})$ (additive). I got the multiplicative case from the confounding factor later, but the additive case is never illustrated. What is a concrete example of an additive bias function? A risk difference with mismeasurement? It would help me to see one sentence pairing each of the three "corners of the bias triangle" with its $B$ shape before the E-value machinery starts.

- **`@lem-qba-confounding-factor` (lines 95-127).** The proof is compact but I lost the thread at line 115: "$r_e=q_e(0)\big[(1-p_e)+p_e\,\mathrm{RR}_{UD}\big]=q_e(0)\big[1+p_e(\mathrm{RR}_{UD}-1)\big]$, using $q_e(1)=\mathrm{RR}_{UD}\,q_e(0)$." Where did $q_e(1)=\mathrm{RR}_{UD}\,q_e(0)$ come from? It is the constant-risk-ratio assumption from the lemma statement, but the connection is not spelled out for a beginner. One more line: "by the definition of $\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$ as the within-arm $U$-risk ratio, $q_e(1)/q_e(0)=\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$" would close the gap.

- **`@lem-qba-sharp-bound` proof, lines 192-197.** The vertex argument: "Fix $f_1,f_0$ and vary a single coordinate $t=p_0(u)\in[1,R]$, holding the others fixed. Writing $a,b>0$ for the partial sums over the other coordinates, $B(t)=\big(a+f_1(u)\,t\big)\big/\big(b+f_0(u)\,t\big)$, whose derivative ... has a sign independent of $t$. Thus $B$ is monotone in each coordinate, so coordinate ascent drives every $p_0(u)$ to an endpoint." I followed the derivative but not the conclusion. "Monotone in each coordinate" does not immediately imply "the maximum is at a vertex" for me; a separately convex or concave function could have an interior max. The point is that a ratio of affine functions is monotone, so the max over a box is at a vertex. That last clause is what I needed spelled out.

- **`@lem-qba-sharp-bound` proof, lines 210-214.** The two-regime optimization. "If $Sb\le 1$, set $a=Sb$; then $B=\big(1+(R-1)Sb\big)\big/\big(1+(R-1)b\big)=:g(b)$, and $g'(b)\propto (R-1)S\big(1+(R-1)b\big)-\big(1+(R-1)Sb\big)(R-1)=(R-1)(S-1)\ge 0$". The algebra skips the cancellation. For a weak reader it would help to write the numerator of $g'(b)$ explicitly as $(R-1)[S(1+(R-1)b)-(1+(R-1)Sb)] = (R-1)(S-1)$, because the $S(R-1)b$ and $-(R-1)Sb$ terms cancel. As written, "it simplifies to $(R-1)(S-1)$" looks like magic.

- **`@thm-evalue-derivation` Part 2 (lines 265-273).** "The smaller root is below $1$ for $\rho>1$ (at $\rho=2$ it equals $2-\sqrt2\approx0.586$), hence infeasible since $v\ge1$." Why is $v\ge1$ a constraint at all? Because $v=\mathrm{RR}_{EU}=\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$ is a risk ratio of a confounder association, and risk ratios are at least $1$ by definition (@eq-qba-sensitivity-params says "both at least $1$"). That linkage is assumed, not stated, at the moment it is used. A one-line reminder would save a beginner from a confused minute.

- **`@def-itc-evalue` (lines 327-347).** The three-case formula for $E_{\mathrm{ITC}}$ appears with no preamble deriving the boundaries. Why does the formula switch at $\mathrm{BF}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$? I had to wait until `@prp-itc-evalue` Part 2 to understand that the denominator goes nonpositive there. The definition would be far less intimidating if a single sentence preceded it: "Because the bias factor with $\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$ fixed is bounded above by $\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$, the equation $\mathrm{BF}_U=\mathrm{BF}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ has a finite solution only when $\mathrm{BF}_{\mathrm{obs}}<\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$; this split gives the three cases below."

- **`@eq-qba-unanchored-estimand` (line 318-320).** The g-computation contrast is written with nested $g$ and $g^{-1}$ and an integral over $dF_0(\mathbf{x},u)$. I am a health researcher; this is the single most opaque expression in the chapter. Even a one-line gloss in words ("predict each comparator patient's outcome under the index treatment using the IPD model, average those predictions across the comparator population, transform back to the effect scale, and subtract the comparator's own reported risk") would make the rest of the section readable.

- **`@prp-qba-tipping-monotone` proof (lines 575-596).** "Realize the comparator covariates by the common-$\mathbf{Z}$ coupling of @eq-qba-norta: draw $\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}_d(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega})$ once, set the reported coordinates $X_{R,j}=F_{R,j}^{-1}(\Phi(Z_j))$ (independent of $m$) and the unreported coordinate $U(m)=F_{U,m}^{-1}(\Phi(Z_d))$." The coupling idea is the crux of the proof and it is introduced in a subordinate clause. I needed a sentence: "The trick is to use the *same* normal draws for every value of $m$, changing only the quantile function applied to the last coordinate. This makes $U(m)$ and $U(m')$ pointwise ordered by the same $\mathbf{Z}$, which is what monotonicity needs."

## What wasn't elaborated enough

- **The E-value's "explain away" target.** Throughout `@sec-qba-evalue`, "explain away" means "reduce the true risk ratio to the null," i.e. $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{true}}\le1$. For a protective effect the null is $\mathrm{RR}=1$; for a harmful effect the null is also $\mathrm{RR}=1$; for a risk difference the null is $0$. The chapter commits to the risk-ratio scale early (line 53-55) but never pauses to tell a beginner that the E-value is *scale-specific*: you cannot plug in a log odds ratio directly, you must convert to a risk ratio first. This is a notorious source of misuse in the applied literature, and the chapter, which is otherwise careful about caveats, does not flag it. A remark like "the E-value is defined on the risk-ratio scale; for an odds-ratio estimate from a rare-outcome trial the odds ratio approximates the risk ratio, but for a common outcome one must convert, and for a hazard ratio one uses the approximate conversion of @vanderweele2017evalue" would prevent a real-world error.

- **The relationship between the symmetric E-value and the ITC E-value.** `@prp-itc-evalue` Part 3 says they coincide when $\mathrm{RR}_{UD}=E$, and the worked example checks this. But the *conceptual* point, that the ITC E-value is always at least as large as the symmetric E-value (because it exploits extra information), is not stated as a proposition. I had to infer it. A corollary "$E_{\mathrm{ITC}}\ge E$ whenever both are defined, with equality iff $\mathrm{RR}_{UD}=E$" would make the gain legible.

- **Selection bias and misclassification.** The "bias triangle" is named three times (lines 30, 86, 797) but only the confounding corner is developed. The remark at line 129-138 waves at "the selection odds ratio divides out of the crude odds ratio; the classification correction inverts the sensitivity-specificity map cell by cell." For a chapter titled "Quantitative Bias Analysis," a reader like me expects at least a half-page on each of the other two corners with their $B$ functions, even if the proofs are cited to Lash. As it stands, two of the three named threats are gestured at and dropped. Why name a triangle and then draw only one vertex?

- **The discrete-margin caveat for NORTA.** `@lem-qba-spearman-matching` remark (lines 504-519) admits the Spearman matching is "approximate" for binary $U$ and that the copula is non-unique. But the worked example uses a *continuous normal* $U$ (line 710), so the caveat is never operationalized. Since the chapter itself says "the unreported $U$ is often binary" (line 510), the example is the easy case and the hard case is left to a remark. A short paragraph showing how the procedure degrades for a binary $U$ (e.g. what changes in the worked example if $U$ is Bernoulli) would close the gap between the theory and the use case the chapter itself flags as common.

- **The probabilistic-bias-analysis prior choice.** `@def-probabilistic-bias-analysis` says $\pi$ "encodes external evidence" but says nothing about *how* an analyst chooses it. In the worked example (line 773-774) the prior is just asserted as $\mathcal{N}(2.0,0.3^2)$. Where does $0.3$ come from? A health researcher faced with their own trial has no idea how to set this. One paragraph on elicitation (e.g. "a common default is to center the prior at the IPD value and set the standard deviation to a fraction of the IPD standard deviation; alternatively a beta prior on a prevalence can be elicited from a clinician's plausible range") would make the method usable.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

- **Why prove the sharp bound at all?** `@lem-qba-sharp-bound` is the technical heart of the chapter and it takes 60 lines of proof. The motivation given (lines 142-146) is one sentence: "To answer it we need a bound that holds for *any* unmeasured confounder." But *why* does the bound need to be sharp? A beginner does not see why sharpness matters for practice. The answer, which I think is: "a non-sharp bound gives a conservative E-value that overstates robustness if the bound is too low, or understates it if too high; sharpness is what makes the E-value the *exact* threshold rather than a guess," is never said. The chapter proves sharpness and then never tells the reader why they should care that it is sharp rather than merely valid.

- **Why bias analysis instead of just a bigger model?** A natural noob question is: "If I am worried about an unmeasured covariate, why not just fit a model with a sensitivity parameter?" The chapter never contrasts QBA with the alternative of Bayesian model expansion. One paragraph at the start of `@sec-qba-framework` explaining that QBA is the right tool when the covariate is *unreported by the comparator* (so no model can include it), whereas full Bayesian expansion is the right tool when you have the data, would clarify the boundary.

- **The HTA decision context.** Nowhere does the chapter mention the actual institutional setting where QBA gets used: a health technology assessment dossier, a NICE committee, an FDA advisory panel. The phrase "decision threshold" appears at line 538 but is abstract. A health researcher reader wants to know: QBA is what you do when the committee asks "how confident are you that this survives an unmeasured confounder?" The closest the chapter comes is the remark at line 397-409, which is good but comes after the math. Moving a concrete HTA framing to the very front of the introduction would anchor everything.

- **Why four instruments, in that order?** The introduction (lines 32-45) lists E-value, ITC E-value, NORTA, tipping-point, in increasing sophistication. But the chapter actually presents E-value, ITC E-value, NORTA, tipping, probabilistic. The probabilistic upgrade is mentioned in the intro (line 43-45) but not as a numbered instrument. The "increasing sophistication" framing is also not self-explanatory: the E-value is the simplest *answer*, but NORTA is a *tool* that feeds the tipping analysis. The reader does not get a map of which instrument answers which question. A small table ("Question: how robust is the point estimate? -> E-value, ITC E-value. Question: what comparator value flips it? -> tipping. Question: how do I propagate uncertainty about that value? -> probabilistic. Question: how do I build the comparator population to feed any of these? -> NORTA") would transform the chapter's usability.

## Missing examples and intuition

- **No running example before Section 6.** The chapter has exactly one worked example, at the very end (lines 694-790). Five sections of pure derivation precede it. For a noob this is backwards. Each of the four instruments deserves a mini-example immediately after its definition, using the *same* toy trial (the $B$ vs $A$ log-risk-ratio trial from Section 6) so the reader sees the numbers accumulate. As written, I had to hold the abstract E-value formula in my head for 400 lines before seeing $E=3.443$ computed.

- **No intuition for the E-value formula shape.** `@eq-qba-evalue`, $E=\rho+\sqrt{\rho(\rho-1)}$, is a strange-looking expression. Why a square root? Why $\rho(\rho-1)$ and not $\rho^2$? The derivation derives it but gives no feel for it. A sentence or a small plot: "for $\rho$ near $1$, $E\approx1+\sqrt{\rho-1}$, so the E-value rises like the square root of the excess risk ratio, slowly at first; for large $\rho$, $E\approx2\rho$," would give a beginner a mental picture.

- **No diagram of the bias-factor surface.** `@eq-qba-bf` is a function of two variables. A contour plot of $\mathrm{BF}_U(\mathrm{RR}_{EU},\mathrm{RR}_{UD})$ with the diagonal and the $\mathrm{BF}_U=\rho$ level set marked would make the E-value's "diagonal intersection" construction instantly legible. The chapter is text-only where a figure would do the work of a paragraph.

- **No clinical scenario for the unreported covariate.** The worked example says "$u$ the one unreported covariate (a prognostic effect modifier, say a baseline biomarker)" (line 705-706). Which biomarker? In what disease? A health researcher learns by anchoring to a real variable. "Say, baseline FEV1 in a COPD trial, or baseline PASI in psoriasis" would make the whole chapter feel like it is about my work. The disease-agnostic framing reads as deliberately generic in a way that obscures intuition.

- **No example of the infinite certificate in words.** The $E_{\mathrm{ITC}}=+\infty$ case (worked example line 758-760) is the chapter's most distinctive practical output, and it is announced with one line and a colon. A health researcher wants to know what to *write in a dossier*: "The unreported covariate's outcome association, estimated from the IPD as RR_UD=1.65, is smaller than the bias factor 2.01 that would be required to nullify the result. Since no across-arm imbalance can produce a bias factor exceeding RR_UD, the comparison is robust to this covariate regardless of the comparator distribution." That sentence is the deliverable; the chapter buries it.

## Notation and jargon problems

- **$E$ as the E-value versus $\mathbb{E}$.** Line 58-59 explicitly disambiguates, which is good, but in the proofs the bare $E$ for exposure (line 97, "$E\in\{0,1\}$") also appears. So $E$ is simultaneously the exposure variable and the E-value, distinguished only by context and font. For a beginner this is a real hazard. Consider $\mathrm{Exp}$ or $A$ for the exposure, or rename the E-value $\mathcal{E}$.

- **$\mathrm{RR}_{EU}$ and $\mathrm{RR}_{UD}$ subscripts.** $EU$ reads as "exposure to unmeasured confounder" and $UD$ as "unmeasured confounder to disease," but this is never spelled out at first use (line 156-158). A beginner has to reverse-engineer the subscript meaning. One line at @eq-qba-sensitivity-params: "the subscript $XY$ denotes the risk ratio of $Y$ across levels of $X$" would fix it.

- **"Bias factor" $B$ versus "bias function" $B(\mathbf{b})$.** `@def-quantitative-bias-analysis` uses $B(\mathbf{b})$ for the general bias function, and `@lem-qba-confounding-factor` uses $B$ (no argument) for the specific confounding bias factor. Same letter, related but different objects. A reader like me conflates them. Renaming the general one (e.g. $\mathcal{B}$) or at least flagging the overload would help.

- **"Crude" versus "adjusted" versus "observed" versus "true."** The chapter uses $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{crude}}$, $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{adj}}$, $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{obs}}$, $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{true}}$ across different sections. Lines 102-106 use crude/adjusted; lines 259-260 use obs/true. Are crude and observed the same? Are adjusted and true the same? The mapping is implicit. A small glossary block in `@sec-qba-framework` would prevent the confusion that I, and I suspect every beginner, hit.

- **"Galois inequality for generalized inverses" (line 453).** This is jargon from order theory dropped without definition. The property $\{F^{-1}(u)\le x\}=\{u\le F(x)\}$ is elementary and could be stated as "the defining property of the generalized inverse" without naming Galois. The name will send a beginner to Wikipedia and away from the chapter.

- **"Effect-on-the-exposed standardization" (line 164).** The proof of `@lem-qba-sharp-bound` says "the causal risk ratio for the effect on the exposed." This is standardization to the exposed covariate distribution, a causal-inference term of art. For a reader who skipped Part II it is opaque. A parenthetical "(i.e. standardize the unexposed risks to the exposed arm's covariate distribution)" would make it self-contained.

## Pacing issues

- **The introduction is a wall.** Lines 5-59 are 55 lines of unbroken prose covering motivation, four-instrument preview, notation, and a disclaimer about novelty. For a noob this is too much before the first definition. Breaking it into three short subsections (Why QBA; The four instruments; Notation for this chapter) would give the reader footholds.

- **`@sec-qba-evalue` is the longest section and has no breathing example.** It runs from line 140 to line 300, about 160 lines, with two theorems, two proofs, and two remarks, and zero numerical illustration until Section 6 some 400 lines later. This is the single worst pacing problem for a beginner. Inserting even a one-line numerical check after `@thm-evalue-derivation` (e.g. "for $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{obs}}=2$, $E=2+\sqrt2\approx3.41$") would let the reader verify the formula on the spot.

- **NORTA and Spearman matching back-to-back.** `@sec-qba-norta` (lines 411-525) introduces the Gaussian copula, Sklar's theorem, NORTA, and Spearman matching in one block. Each of those is a chapter-worthy topic. The reader gets no example of NORTA until the worked example, which uses the *independence* copula (line 709-710), the trivial case. So the only worked NORTA example sidesteps the entire difficulty. This is a serious gap: the reader who wants to see NORTA actually glue a non-trivial dependence structure finds nothing.

- **The worked example arrives too late.** Section 6 (line 694) is excellent and is the chapter's strongest pedagogy, but by the time I reached it I had nearly given up. Even moving the first two paragraphs of the example (the setup, lines 696-710) to right after the introduction, and then referring back to it as each instrument is introduced, would restructure the chapter from "definitions then example" to "example then definitions," which is how beginners learn.

- **The Notes section (lines 792-831) is dense and unsorted.** It packs provenance for every instrument into four paragraphs. For a noob this is where I would look for "where do I read more," but it is written as a credit ledger. A short "Further reading" bullet list (Lash for QBA; VanderWeele for E-value; Cario and Nelson for NORTA; Nelsen for copulas) would be more usable.

## What worked well

- **The worked example (Section 6) is genuinely hand-checkable.** I verified $K=0.300$, the tipping point $m^\dagger=0.600$, and the E-value $3.443$ with a calculator. The numbers are consistent across all four instruments and the cross-check at line 769-771 (recovering the symmetric E-value) is a beautiful touch. This is the model for the rest of the chapter.

- **The caveats are honest and travel with the number.** The remark at lines 396-409 listing the three caveats of the ITC E-value (sampling uncertainty not propagated, single-covariate, ratio-scale only) is exactly the kind of responsible framing a health researcher needs. The remark at lines 504-519 on the discrete and definiteness caveats for NORTA is similarly careful.

- **The connection to `@thm-sema-insufficient` is well-motivated.** Lines 19-22 and the remark at line 402 tie the chapter back to the insufficiency of the shared-effect-modifier assumption. This gives the whole QBA enterprise a point: it is the quantitative answer to a qualitative gap established earlier. Good.

- **The exercise set is well-designed.** `@exr-qba-evalue-compute` is a clean basic check; `@exr-qba-protective` makes the symmetry explicit; `@exr-qba-itc-evalue-star` reuses the worked example with a stronger covariate; `@exr-qba-norta-margins-star` confronts the discrete caveat directly. The progression from compute to prove is right.

- **The probabilistic-bias-analysis convergence theorem (`@thm-qba-pba-convergence`) is appropriately rigorous.** Grounding a procedure that "is not merely a heuristic" with Glivenko-Cantelli is the right move and is cleanly done.

## Suggestions

1. **Restructure around the worked example.** Introduce the $B$ vs $A$ trial setup immediately after the introduction, then present each instrument as "here is the question, here is the formula, here is the number for our trial." This converts the chapter from a proof sequence into a worked analysis, which is how QBA is actually used in an HTA dossier.

2. **Add a one-page "QBA in one screen" summary.** A table with one row per instrument (E-value, ITC E-value, NORTA, tipping point, probabilistic), columns for question answered, inputs, output, scale, key limitation. This is what a health researcher pins above their desk.

3. **Disambiguate $E$ (exposure) from $E$ (E-value).** Rename one of them. Also add the small glossary for crude/adjusted/observed/true and for $EU$/$UD$ subscripts.

4. **Expand the bias triangle to two pages.** Give selection bias and misclassification each a short subsection with their $B$ and $h$, even if proofs are cited. A chapter named "Quantitative Bias Analysis" that develops only one of three named threats feels incomplete.

5. **Add a figure: the bias-factor contour and the diagonal.** One TikZ or matplotlib contour of $\mathrm{BF}_U(x,y)$ with the level set $\mathrm{BF}_U=\rho$ and the diagonal marked. This single figure would replace a paragraph of explanation of why the E-value is the diagonal intersection.

6. **Add a NORTA example with non-trivial dependence.** The worked example uses the independence copula. Add a second mini-example, even five lines, where the comparator $U$ is correlated with $x_R$ via a borrowed Spearman correlation, and show the adjusted effect changing. Otherwise NORTA is proved but never seen to do its job.

7. **State and prove the corollary $E_{\mathrm{ITC}}\ge E$.** It is the headline practical claim of the chapter (the ITC E-value is sharper because it uses more data) and it is currently left for the reader to infer.

8. **Flag the scale-specificity of the E-value in a remark.** Odds ratios, hazard ratios, risk differences: tell the reader explicitly what to convert and how, because this is the most common applied misuse.

9. **Soften the jargon.** Replace "Galois inequality" with "the defining property of the generalized inverse"; expand "effect-on-the-exposed standardization" with a parenthetical; spell out the $EU$/$UD$ subscripts at first use.

10. **Move the HTA framing to the front.** The first paragraph should name the decision context (a reimbursement dossier, an advisory committee) so the reader knows from line 1 that this is a practical tool, not a probability exercise.
