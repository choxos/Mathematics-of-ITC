# Feedback: Chapter 03 — Linear Algebra III

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part1/03-linear-algebra-iii.qmd`

## Overall impression

I can tell this is a serious, careful chapter and that a lot of work went into it. The theorem-proof structure is clean, the worked example at the end is genuinely helpful, and the bridge to covariance matrices at @sec-la3-covariance is the one place I felt the ITC connection click. But as a health researcher with rusty math, I spent most of this chapter confused or swimming. The pace is brutal: we go from "what is an eigenvalue" to the Courant-Fischer min-max theorem in a single chapter, with almost no breathing room, no pictures, and very few concrete health-research anchors. The intro (@sec-la3-intro) promises that "every quantity that controls the precision of an indirect treatment comparison is, at bottom, an eigenvalue or a singular value," but after that line the ITC motivation mostly vanishes until the very last section. I was grinding through pure algebra for pages with no reminder of why I should care. The worked example (@sec-la3-example) is good but comes too late; I needed micro-examples scattered throughout.

## What was unclear

**The jump to complex vector spaces (@sec-la3-eigen, lines 46-50).** The chapter opens by saying "a real matrix can fail to have real eigenvalues," then immediately moves us into $\mathbb{C}^n$ with a conjugate-linear inner product $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle = \mathbf{x}^*\mathbf{y}$. As a weak-math reader I have never seen $\mathbf{x}^* = \bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top$ before and the notation hit me cold. What is the conjugate transpose, why do I need it here, and why is the inner product "conjugate-linear"? A one-paragraph aside explaining that complex inner products need conjugation to make $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$ real and nonnegative would have saved me. Right now it reads like a formality I am supposed to already know.

**@def-eigenvalue (lines 52-68).** The eigenspace is defined as $E_\lambda = \mathcal{N}(A-\lambda I)$, and then geometric multiplicity is $\dim E_\lambda \ge 1$. But the sentence "its dimension $\dim E_\lambda \ge 1$ is the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$" is buried inside the definition. I had to read it three times to realize geometric multiplicity is a separate named quantity, not just a fact about $E_\lambda$. Pulling it out as its own sentence or even its own definition would help.

**@lem-geo-le-alg (lines 113-140).** The proof constructs a basis, extends it, assembles $S$, and writes $S^{-1}AS$ as block upper triangular "for some blocks $B$ and $C$." I do not understand why $S^{-1}AS$ has that block form. The text says "Because $A\mathbf{v}_i=\lambda_0\mathbf{v}_i$ for $i\le g$, the matrix $S^{-1}AS$ is block upper triangular" but it does not actually show me the multiplication. For a beginner this is a big skip: I need to see why the lower-left block is zero. One line of "the first $g$ columns of $AS$ are $\lambda_0\mathbf{v}_i$, matching the first $g$ columns of $S(\lambda_0 I_g)$, so the lower-left block of $S^{-1}AS$ is zero" would close the gap.

**@lem-trace-det-eigenvalues proof (lines 272-294).** This is the hardest proof in the chapter for me. The Leibniz expansion over permutations $S_n$, "fixed points of $\pi$," "no permutation has exactly $n-1$ fixed points" — I followed the logic line by line but I never built intuition. Why is the coefficient of $t^{n-1}$ related to fixed points at all? A sentence explaining "the term indexed by a permutation $\pi$ contributes a factor of $t$ exactly for each index $i$ with $\pi(i)=i$, so the degree in $t$ equals the number of fixed points of $\pi$" is there at lines 282-284, but I still could not see why only the identity contributes to $t^{n-1}$ until I drew it out myself. The claim "No permutation has exactly $n-1$ fixed points" deserves a parenthetical: "(fixing $n-1$ symbols pins the last one too, since a permutation is a bijection)." Without that I had to stop and prove it to myself.

**@thm-spectral-theorem proof (lines 380-438).** The induction is clear in outline but several steps lost me. At line 398-400, "By @thm-gram-schmidt choose an orthonormal basis of $W$ and assemble it as the columns of a matrix $Q_1$" — I needed to be told explicitly that $W$ has dimension $n-1$ (it is stated at line 388 but I had lost it by line 398). The block matrix computation at lines 414-431 is dense; the vanishing of the off-diagonal blocks is the key step and it is shown, but I had to reread four times. The remark at lines 440-447 about $P_i = \mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top$ being the orthogonal projection is the single most illuminating sentence in the chapter, and it is buried after the proof. It should be a displayed remark or even its own short proposition, because it is the bridge from "abstract decomposition" to "this is why projections work."

**@thm-sylvester-criterion proof (lines 576-609).** The contradiction argument with $U = \mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{q}_1,\mathbf{q}_2\}$ and $V = \{\mathbf{x}: x_n = 0\}$ is clever but I found it disorienting. Why $x_n = 0$ specifically? The choice of $V$ feels arbitrary and I worried it only works because of how we labeled coordinates. A sentence saying "we may take $V$ to be any $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace on which $A$ is positive definite; the coordinate slice $x_n=0$ is one such because $A_{n-1}\succ 0$" would have reassured me the argument is coordinate-free in spirit.

**@prp-pseudoinverse-penrose uniqueness proof (lines 919-934).** The chain of equalities at lines 922-926 is a wall of symbols. Each individual step is labeled (P3), (P1), etc., but I could not follow the flow. "Using (P3) for $G$, then (P1) for $H$ in the form $A=AHA$, then (P3) for both" — I had to write it out by hand to see which matrix is which. A slower, two-line-at-a-time layout with each justification on its own line would help beginners like me.

## What wasn't elaborated enough

**Why the spectral theorem matters before it is proved.** @sec-la3-spectral (line 301) starts with "Symmetric matrices are the best-behaved matrices in all of linear algebra" and lists three facts to prove, but never says what the payoff is for a statistician. I know from the intro that covariance matrices are symmetric, but I needed a sentence here like "the spectral theorem is why every covariance matrix can be diagonalized, why PCA works, and why the matrix square root exists." The motivation is back-loaded to @sec-la3-covariance; it should be front-loaded too.

**The remark on functions of a matrix (lines 449-459).** This is a crucial idea — $f(A) = Q\,f(\Lambda)\,Q^\top$ — and it gets a remark box. For a health researcher this is the operational core: it is how you compute a square root, an inverse, a log, anything. I wanted a full paragraph with a small worked instance, maybe "if $A = Q\mathrm{diag}(4,9)Q^\top$ then $A^{1/2} = Q\mathrm{diag}(2,3)Q^\top$" before the abstract statement. As is, I read it three times and still was not sure I could apply it.

**Rayleigh quotient and PCA (lines 656-710).** The text says the variational viewpoint is "the entry point to principal component analysis and to the conditioning bounds used throughout statistics" (lines 659-661), but PCA is never actually defined or connected. For a health researcher PCA is probably the one eigenvalue thing I have heard of. A two-sentence aside "PCA finds the direction $\mathbf{x}$ maximizing $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{x}$ subject to $\|\mathbf{x}\|=1$; by @thm-rayleigh the maximizer is the top eigenvector of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$" would connect the theorem to something I know.

**Conditioning and the condition number.** Lines 765-772 mention $\|A\|_2 = \max_i|\lambda_i|$ and Weyl's inequality, then say "both are pursued in the exercises." But conditioning of $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ was name-checked in the intro (lines 24-29) as the reason the SVD matters for least squares. I expected at least a sentence defining the condition number $\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_r$ and saying "when $\kappa$ is large, small perturbations in the data cause large swings in $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$, which is why collinearity inflates variance." That is the health-research payoff and it is missing from the body.

**The Mahalanobis distance (lines 1018-1023).** The bridge remark defines $\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}$ as the Mahalanobis distance and says Part V uses it, but it never says what it measures. One sentence: "it is the standardized Euclidean distance that accounts for correlation and unequal variances, so two covariate profiles that differ by one raw unit are far apart in directions where the study has little variation and close in directions where it has a lot" would make it concrete.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

- **@sec-la3-eigen generally.** I never learned *why* I care about eigenvalues before the definition. A motivating paragraph: "When $A$ models how a system evolves, eigenvectors are the directions that keep their shape and eigenvalues are how much they stretch. In statistics the 'system' is a covariance or information matrix, and its eigenvectors are the independent axes of variation." Without that, the definition of eigenvalue at @def-eigenvalue lands as pure algebra.
- **Geometric vs algebraic multiplicity (@lem-geo-le-alg).** Why do I care that these can differ? The defective example at lines 224-234 helps, but it comes later. At the lemma itself I wanted a line: "when geometric is strictly less than algebraic, the matrix is not diagonalizable, which is the pathology the spectral theorem will rule out for symmetric matrices."
- **@sec-la3-trace-det.** Why do I care that trace and determinant are symmetric functions of eigenvalues? The section proves it beautifully but never says. For a health researcher: "the trace of a covariance matrix is total variance, and the determinant is a generalized variance (the volume of the confidence ellipsoid); both are products/sums of eigenvalues, which is why PCA 'explains' variance." That connection is absent.
- **Sylvester's criterion (@thm-sylvester-criterion).** Why prove a second, arithmetic test when the eigenvalue test already exists? Because computing eigenvalues is hard and computing determinants of small submatrices is easy. That one sentence of motivation is missing.
- **Courant-Fischer (@thm-courant-fischer).** The text says it is for "interior eigenvalues" but never says why interior eigenvalues matter. A line: "interior eigenvalues control the stability of the middle principal components and underlie the eigenvalue interlacing used in dimension reduction" would justify the effort.

## Notation and jargon problems

- **$\mathbf{x}^* = \bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top$ (line 48).** Introduced without definition in notation.qmd (which uses $\mathbf{x}^\top$ only). A beginner stumbling on the conjugate transpose cold is a real barrier. Add it to notation.qmd or define it inline.
- **"Similar matrices" (@def-diagonalizable, line 180).** The term "similar" is introduced and immediately "diagonalizable" is defined in terms of it. I was not sure whether similarity is an equivalence relation (it is, but the text does not say). One parenthetical: "(similarity is an equivalence relation; similar matrices share rank, determinant, trace, and spectrum)" would orient me.
- **"Defective" (line 232).** Introduced in a remark with no definition box. It is used later at line 377 ("never defective"). Worth a formal definition or at least bolding on first use.
- **$A\succeq B$ notation (line 504).** The Loewner order $A\succeq B$ is defined only at line 504, deep in the definiteness definition. But the notation table in notation.qmd lists only $A\succeq 0$ and $A\succ 0$, not the binary $A\succeq B$. Either add it to notation.qmd or flag it more prominently here.
- **"Leading principal minors" (line 563).** The term is introduced in prose ("the determinants of the top-left square submatrices") but never given a definition box. For a beginner the phrase "principal minor" vs "leading principal minor" vs "minor" is a minefield; the remark at lines 611-617 mentions "all principal minors" but the distinction from "leading" is never formally laid out.
- **$\mathcal{N}$, $\mathcal{C}$ (lines 38, 786, etc.).** These appear throughout and are in notation.qmd, but a beginner reading this chapter in isolation will not flip back. A one-line reminder at first use ("$\mathcal{N}(A)$ is the null space, $\mathcal{C}(A)$ the column space") in the body would help.
- **"$A$-invariant" (line 390).** The term is defined inline ("if $\mathbf{x}\in W$ then ... so $A\mathbf{x}\in W$") but the label "invariant subspace" is used as if I should recognize it. Bold it or pull it into a margin note.
- **Frobenius norm $\|A\|_F$ (line 1194, exercise).** Appears only in a starred exercise with no definition in the body or notation.qmd. If it is fair game in exercises it should be defined somewhere reachable.

## Pacing issues

- **Far too much theory before the first example.** Lines 1-1025 are essentially pure abstraction. The worked example at @sec-la3-example is excellent but it is the last section before Notes. For a beginner this is a wall. I would strongly recommend inserting a running $2\times2$ example threaded through the chapter: the same matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ could illustrate eigenvalues, then diagonalization, then the spectral theorem, then quadratic forms, then Rayleigh, then the square root, so each abstract result has an immediate concrete check.
- **The spectral theorem proof is very long for one sitting.** @thm-spectral-theorem (lines 365-438) is the centerpiece and the proof is roughly 60 lines of dense induction. No intermediate checkpoint or "what we have shown so far" sentence. Breaking the proof into named parts (base case; finding the first eigenvector; the invariant complement; the inductive assembly) with bold subheaders would help a beginner track where they are.
- **Sylvester's criterion proof is also long and dense (lines 576-609).** The contradiction argument with $U$ and $V$ is a lot to hold in your head. A short plan-of-proof before it starts ("we induct on $n$; the forward direction is easy; for the converse we assume all minors positive, get $A_{n-1}\succ 0$ by induction, and rule out a negative eigenvalue by a dimension-count contradiction") would help.
- **SVD and pseudoinverse back to back.** @sec-la3-svd (lines 775-869) and @sec-la3-pseudoinverse (lines 871-993) are both long and dense, with no example between them. By the time I reached the Penrose equations I was saturated. The rank-deficient example at lines 1078-1101 should be split, with the SVD part moved next to the SVD theorem and the pseudoinverse part next to the pseudoinverse.
- **Exercises ramp up sharply.** The first exercise (@exr-diagonalize-symmetric) is doable; by @exr-ata-aat-spectrum (line 1187) and @exr-weyl-inequality (line 1197) we are at graduate-math-major level with no ramp. A few intermediate exercises bridging "compute an SVD by hand" to "prove Weyl's inequality" would help.

## What worked well

- **The intro (@sec-la3-intro) is excellent.** The three bullets about covariance, Gram matrices, and whitening are the clearest "why this matters" in the chapter. I just wish that energy had been sustained throughout.
- **@lem-eigvec-independent (lines 145-173).** The induction proof is clean, well paced, and I could follow every step. This is the model for how the other proofs should read.
- **The worked example @sec-la3-example (lines 1026-1101).** Once I got here I understood large parts of the chapter retroactively. The hand-checking of $Q\Lambda Q^\top = A$, the trace/det verification, and the pseudoinverse Penrose check are all exactly what a beginner needs.
- **The bridge remark @sec-la3-covariance (lines 1000-1024).** This is the best single paragraph in the chapter for a health researcher. It ties spectral theorem, square root, whitening, and Mahalanobis distance together and points forward to PCA and Part V. I would have wanted this kind of integration earlier and more often.
- **The remark at lines 440-447** interpreting $P_i = \mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top$ as the orthogonal projection onto the eigenline. Once I read this, the spectral decomposition stopped being symbols and became geometry.
- **The notes section (@sec-la3-notes, lines 1103-1126)** is honest about what is proved where and why the proof route was chosen. The note that this layer has no Coq counterpart is helpful for orienting expectations.

## Suggestions

1. **Thread a running $2\times2$ example** (e.g., $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$) through every section, not just at the end. Each theorem gets a one-line numeric check immediately after the proof.
2. **Add at least two figures:** the unit-circle-to-ellipse picture for the SVD, and the eigen-axes of the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=1$. These carry more intuition than pages of prose.
3. **Front-load the ITC motivation.** Repeat a one-line health-research anchor at the start of each major section (eigenvalues, spectral theorem, quadratic forms, SVD, pseudoinverse), not just in the intro and the covariance bridge.
4. **Slow down the Leibniz-expansion proof of @lem-trace-det-eigenvalues.** Add the parenthetical about "no permutation has exactly $n-1$ fixed points," and show the $n=2$ or $n=3$ case explicitly first so the reader can see the pattern before the general argument.
5. **Define the conjugate transpose and the complex inner product before using them** (line 48). Either a short "complex refresher" box at the start of @sec-la3-eigen or a forward pointer to an appendix.
6. **Promote the projection interpretation of the spectral decomposition** (lines 440-447) to a named remark or proposition. It is the conceptual key for everything downstream.
7. **Add a conditioning-number paragraph** in @sec-la3-svd or @sec-la3-pseudoinverse, connecting $\sigma_1/\sigma_r$ to collinearity and variance inflation. This is the single most relevant fact for a health researcher fitting regressions.
8. **Split the final worked example** so the SVD piece sits near @thm-svd and the pseudoinverse piece near @def-pseudoinverse, rather than both at the end.
9. **Formally define "defective," "principal minor," "leading principal minor," and the Loewner order** in definition boxes, and add $\mathbf{x}^*$, $\succeq$ (binary), and $\|\cdot\|_F$ to notation.qmd.
10. **Add intermediate exercises** between the computational ones and the starred theoretical ones, for example "verify the Penrose equations for a specific $2\times2$ rank-one matrix" before asking for the general Weyl proof.
