# Feedback: Chapter 07 — Generalized Linear Models

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part1/07-glm.qmd`

## Overall impression

I am a health researcher who has run a few logistic regressions in R and Stata, read some forest plots, and knows what an odds ratio is in practice. I picked this chapter because I want to understand the math behind the models I fit. Honestly, this chapter made me feel like I walked into the wrong classroom. The opening paragraphs hooked me with concrete clinical examples (counts, adverse events, survival), and the non-collapsibility section genuinely changed how I think about odds ratios. But the long middle stretch, from the exponential dispersion family through IRLS and the asymptotic normality proof, is a wall of algebra with almost no handholds for someone at my level. I survived it, but only by trusting the proofs rather than understanding them. The chapter is clearly written for someone who already finished a measure-theory-adjacent probability course, not for the "naive researcher to mastery" goal stated in the project's own design.

## What was unclear

- **@def-exponential-family (lines 73-97).** This is the first real definition and it hits me cold. "We fix a reference measure $\nu$ on the real line" means nothing to me. I have never used a "reference measure" or integrated "against $\nu$." The parenthetical "for discrete responses the integral is a sum" helps a little, but I do not understand why the measure-theoretic framing is needed at all when every concrete example in @exm-edf-members is a plain density or pmf. This is the single biggest conceptual wall of the chapter and it comes at line 68.
- **@eq-edf-density (lines 79-82).** I see the formula $\exp\{(y\theta - b(\theta))/a(\phi) + c(y,\phi)\}$ but I have no intuition for what the three pieces "do." A one-line gloss would help: "$y\theta$ couples the data to the parameter, $b(\theta)$ normalizes it, $c(y,\phi)$ handles terms that don't depend on $\theta$." As written I had to reverse-engineer that from the proof of @thm-glm-mean-variance.
- **@thm-glm-mean-variance proof (lines 138-181).** The proof is correct but too terse for me. The step at line 144-145, rewriting $M_Y(t)$ by folding $e^{ty}$ into the exponent and completing the natural parameter, is the key trick and it is shown as one equality. I had to sit with it for ten minutes. An intermediate line showing $ty + y\theta/a(\phi) = y(\theta + t a(\phi))/a(\phi)$ explicitly would have saved me. Likewise the chain "$\theta'=\theta+t a(\phi)$" at line 141 is introduced without motivating why that specific substitution is the right one; I understand retrospectively it is chosen to absorb $e^{ty}$, but at first read it looks magic.
- **@thm-glm-mean-variance, second claim (lines 176-180).** "By the intermediate value theorem its image $\mathcal M$ is an open interval." I had to recall that the continuous image of an open interval under a strictly monotone function is an open interval. This is stated as if obvious. For a weak-math reader it deserves a clause of justification.
- **@eq-edf-normalization (lines 99-107).** "Equivalently, $b(\theta)=a(\phi)\log\int\dots$". I could not immediately see why @eq-edf-normalization follows from the density integrating to one. The algebra is one line but it is skipped.
- **@sec-links, @def-link-function (lines 250-263).** "Twice continuously differentiable and strictly monotone, hence a bijection from $\mathcal M$ onto an open interval $g(\mathcal M)$." I follow "strictly monotone = invertible," but why $g(\mathcal M)$ must be open, and why we need $C^2$ at the definition stage, is asserted not motivated. The next sentence "Twice differentiability is what the estimation theory of @sec-glm-estimation needs" is forward-referenced but I do not get the intuition now.
- **@prp-canonical-eta-theta (lines 279-290).** The statement "$\theta_i=\eta_i$" is compact but I did not appreciate why it matters until much later (@lem-canonical-hessian). A sentence saying "this is why the canonical link makes the math clean: the natural parameter and the linear predictor become the same object" would help a beginner.
- **@thm-glm-score proof (lines 425-458).** The "four links of the chain" framing is good, but the jump from @eq-glm-loglik to the four displayed derivatives at lines 429-440 packs a lot into equalities. In particular "$d\theta_i/d\mu_i = 1/b''(\theta_i) = 1/V(\mu_i)$, the second because $\mu_i=b'(\theta_i)$ gives $d\mu_i/d\theta_i=b''(\theta_i)$ and the inverse function has reciprocal derivative" is one sentence doing three steps. I lost the thread here.
- **@prp-glm-information proof (lines 510-531).** Uses the information equality (@thm-information-equality) without restating it. A weak reader who read Chapter 5 weeks ago will not remember that $\mathcal I = \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf U)$. One clause reminding me what the information equality says would keep the proof self-contained enough to read.
- **@thm-irls proof (lines 564-598).** The cancellation at lines 586-591, where $\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}$ appears, cancels, and reappears, is the crux and it is shown in two lines. I had to rewrite it on paper. The line "the two $\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}$ terms cancel" is true but I did not see it immediately because one is inside the inverse-times-product and the other is free.
- **@def-saturated-model (lines 716-723).** "It attains the largest log-likelihood achievable by any model in the family for the given data, because each term $y_i\theta_i - b(\theta_i)$ is maximized over $\theta_i$ at $b'(\theta_i)=y_i$." I follow the claim but the justification "maximized at $b'(\theta_i)=y_i$" skips the derivative step: $d/d\theta_i[y_i\theta_i - b(\theta_i)] = y_i - b'(\theta_i) = 0$. One parenthetical would close it.
- **@prp-deviance-nonneg proof (lines 752-765).** The "Bregman divergence" is introduced inside the proof with no prior definition. I had never heard the term. The Taylor remainder argument is fine, but the naming and the $D_b(u,v)$ notation feel like an Easter egg dropped into a proof rather than a defined object. Either define Bregman divergence in its own boxed environment or drop the name and just use the Taylor argument.
- **@sec-noncollapsibility (lines 841-849).** The preamble says "In a linear model the coefficient of a treatment indicator is both the effect within every covariate stratum and the effect on the population average, because expectation is linear." I think I believe this, but it is asserted rather than shown with a one-line display. Given that this is the motivational contrast for the whole section, a one-line derivation $\mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)] - \mathbb E_X[\mu_0(X)] = \mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)-\mu_0(X)] = \delta$ up front would make the "linear is special" point land before we see the logit break it.
- **@def-non-collapsibility (lines 860-875).** The definition is technically precise but the quantifier structure ("for every distribution of $X$ and every pair of stratum means consistent with it") is heavy. I had to read it three times. A plain-language gloss immediately after: "collapsible means: if the conditional effect is the same in every stratum, then the pooled effect equals that same number, no matter how the strata are sized" would help a lot.
- **Hazard ratio paragraph (lines 966-977).** "the population average of an exponential is not the exponential of the average." This is the intuitive heart of the section and it is buried in one sentence. I want this unpacked: a small display showing $\mathbb E_X[e^{-\int h}] \neq e^{-\int \mathbb E_X[h]}$ and a reference to Jensen. The whole hazard-ratio non-collapsibility claim is left as a forward reference (acknowledged at line 1097-1099), which is honest, but for the beginner the section promises more than it delivers on the HR.

## What wasn't elaborated enough

- **The cumulant generating function and why it is the right tool (@thm-glm-mean-variance, lines 113-181).** The chapter says "the proof uses the cumulant generating function, which is exactly what the cumulant function $b$ controls; this is the reason for the name." That is a sentence. For a beginner, the fact that $K_Y(t)$'s derivatives at zero give cumulants (= mean, variance, ...) is the load-bearing idea and it is dispatched in a few lines (162-172). I would like a short paragraph before the proof explaining the strategy: "we will show $K_Y(t) = [b(\theta + t a(\phi)) - b(\theta)]/a(\phi)$, and because cumulants are derivatives of $K$ at zero, the mean and variance fall out as $b'$ and $a(\phi)b''$." Right now the strategy is revealed only in retrospect.
- **The exponential dispersion family as a unifying abstraction (lines 66-111).** The chapter asserts it "contains the normal, Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, and gamma distributions as special cases" (lines 22-24) and then verifies this in @exm-edf-members. What is missing is the motivation for why this particular family is the right abstraction: why not just handle each distribution separately? The answer (it gives one proof of mean/variance, one score equation, one IRLS algorithm for all of them) is implicit. A paragraph stating this upfront would give the reader a reason to care before the algebra begins.
- **IRLS as "a sequence of weighted linear regressions" (@thm-irls, lines 543-610).** The remark at lines 600-610 is good and is the closest the chapter comes to intuition. But the theorem statement and proof are dense. I would like a small pictorial or schematic description before the theorem: "Step 1: form a synthetic response $z$ by nudging the current linear predictor by the residual. Step 2: weight each point by how informative it is. Step 3: run weighted least squares. Repeat." The remark does this but only after the formal proof.
- **Deviance as a generalization of RSS (@sec-deviance, lines 710-714).** The opening two sentences promise the analogy, and @prp-deviance-nonneg confirms it for the normal case, but the conceptual bridge from "residual sum of squares" to "twice a log-likelihood gap against a saturated model" is not built. A beginner coming from Chapter 6's RSS needs a sentence explaining why the log-likelihood gap is the natural generalization: because for Gaussian log-likelihood, the gap is exactly (proportional to) the RSS.
- **Asymptotic normality and its use (@cor-glm-asymptotic-normality, lines 663-697).** The proof defers almost entirely to @thm-mle-asymptotic-normality. That is fine structurally, but a beginner needs a sentence in the remark or corollary body saying what this is *for*: this is the theorem that justifies the standard errors and confidence intervals reported by `glm()`. The remark at 699-708 about delta-method SEs for ORs is good but the foundational "this is why your software prints a SE" is unsaid.
- **The "analysis of deviance" workflow (@thm-deviance-test, lines 795-828).** The theorem is clear; the remark at 830-839 is helpful. But the connection to the $F$-test and chi-square test the reader may know from introductory biostatistics is only sketched. A sentence "this is the GLM analogue of the ANOVA $F$-test you learned for linear models" would anchor it.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

- **Why the exponential dispersion family at all?** The introduction (lines 18-27) motivates GLMs well, but the specific choice of the EDF as the random component is presented as given. A beginner asks: why this family and not some other? The answer (it is exactly the class for which mean and variance are clean functions of one natural parameter, making ML estimation tractable) is never stated as motivation, only revealed after the fact.
- **Why prove @thm-glm-mean-variance in full?** The mean and variance formulas are things I can look up. The chapter proves them via the cumulant generating function. What I am missing is a sentence telling me why the proof matters: because the same cumulant-trick structure is reused for the score, the information, and the asymptotic theory, so seeing it once pays off three times.
- **Why the canonical link?** @def-canonical-link (lines 269-277) defines it and @prp-canonical-eta-theta and @lem-canonical-hessian give its properties, but the beginner motivation "it makes the math and the algorithm simplest, and for the families we use it also gives the interpretable effect measures (OR, RR)" is scattered. A consolidating sentence at the definition would help.
- **Why IRLS?** The remark at 600-610 says "fitting a logistic or Poisson regression costs no more than a handful of linear regressions, and why GLM software is built on a linear-algebra core." Good, but this is the *computational* motivation. The *pedagogical* motivation for the reader is stronger: IRLS is the bridge showing that everything the reader learned in Chapter 6 (hat matrix, leverage, normal equations) transfers to GLMs. That bridge is mentioned but undersold.
- **Why non-collapsibility *here*?** The introduction (lines 36-42) says this is the most consequential reason. But within @sec-noncollapsibility itself, the connection to ITCs is concentrated in the closing remark (979-992) and is dense. For a beginner, a one-paragraph "what this means for your systematic review" near the top of the section, before the formal definition, would motivate the formalism. Right now the ITC payoff is deferred to the very end.
- **The hazard ratio forward reference.** The chapter says the HR non-collapsibility proof is deferred (lines 968-977, 1097-1099). For a health researcher, the HR is arguably the most important effect measure in the book, and leaving it unproven in the chapter that promises to prove "everything" is a real gap in felt completeness, even if the deferral is honest.

## Missing examples and intuition

- **No worked example for the EDF decomposition.** @exm-edf-members verifies five distributions, which is good, but each is a one-paragraph algebra rearrangement. I would have liked one of them (say Bernoulli) walked through slowly with each piece of @eq-edf-density labeled: "here is $y\theta$, here is $b(\theta)$, here is $a(\phi)$, here is $c(y,\phi)$." The current format throws all five at once.
- **No numerical example for the score equations or information.** The worked example at @sec-glm-worked (lines 994-1072) is excellent and is the chapter's saving grace for a beginner. But it only covers logistic regression, and only the saturated two-group case. There is no worked example showing the score equation being assembled for a non-canonical link, or showing Fisher information weights being computed numerically. The probit IRLS is left to @exr-glm-irls-probit, a starred exercise.
- **No visual / geometric intuition for IRLS.** The remark at 600-610 is verbal. A figure showing one IRLS step (the synthetic $z$ points, the weighted least-squares line, the updated fit) would make @thm-irls dramatically more accessible. The chapter has zero figures.
- **No example for deviance beyond Poisson/binomial.** @exm-deviance-forms covers the two discrete families. The normal case is proved in @prp-deviance-nonneg but not illustrated with numbers. A one-line numeric check that the Gaussian deviance equals RSS would close the loop for Chapter 6 readers.
- **No clinical / trial example for non-collapsibility.** @exm-noncollapsible-or is a clean two-stratum numerical example, which is good. But it is abstract: "$X\in\{0,1\}$" with no clinical meaning. A one-sentence framing ("imagine $X$ is age group, and these are response probabilities under two drugs") would help the health researcher feel the example in their bones. The chapter is otherwise good about clinical framing in the intro but drops it in the formal sections.
- **No example of the "attenuation toward the null" phenomenon.** The remark at 945-946 states the marginal OR is attenuated toward 1. A beginner benefits from seeing two or three numerical cases (different baseline risks, different stratum weights) to feel how the attenuation varies. The starred exercise @exr-glm-noncollapse asks the reader to construct one; an inline example would be better for learning.

## Notation and jargon problems

- **$\nu$ (reference measure).** Used from line 68 onward with only a brief gloss. A weak reader will not know what "Lebesgue measure" versus "counting measure" means operationally. The remark at 54-64 on reused symbols does *not* mention $\nu$. It should.
- **$\theta$ vs $\boldsymbol\theta$ vs $\theta_{ijk}$.** The remark at 54-64 handles this, and it is well done. No complaint here; this is a model for the rest of the chapter.
- **$\phi$ vs $\Phi$ vs $\Phi'$.** The remark at 54-64 addresses it. Good. Though for a beginner, the sentence "the symbol $\phi$ is never overloaded inside this chapter" is reassuring but I still had to double check while reading the probit section (@sec-other-links).
- **$a(\phi)=\phi/w$ vs $a_i(\phi)=\phi/w_i$.** The switch between the generic $a(\phi)$ in @def-exponential-family and the indexed $a_i(\phi)$ in @def-glm (line 310) is natural but made without an explicit transition. I stumbled on it.
- **"Cumulant function" vs "cumulant generating function".** $b(\theta)$ is the cumulant function; $K_Y(t)$ is the cumulant generating function. They are related but different objects. The chapter uses both and the names are close enough that I conflated them on first read. The sentence at 116-117 ("the cumulant generating function, which is exactly what the cumulant function $b$ controls") is the only disambiguation and it is subtle.
- **"Prior weight" $w$.** The term "prior weight" (line 77) sounds Bayesian to a health researcher. It is not; it is a known frequency/exposure weight. A parenthetical "despite the name, this is not a Bayesian prior; it is a known multiplicity such as a binomial denominator $n_i$" would prevent confusion.
- **"Mean space" $\mathcal M$.** Introduced at line 129 without much fanfare. It recurs in @def-link-function. I would like it stated once, plainly: "$\mathcal M$ is just the set of possible means for this distribution; for Bernoulli it is $(0,1)$, for Poisson it is $(0,\infty)$."
- **Variance function $V(\mu)$.** Defined at line 132. The name suggests it is a function of $\mu$, which it is, but the chain $V(\mu) = b''(\theta(\mu))$ is the definition and it took me a moment to see that this is well-defined because $\theta(\mu)$ is a function. Worth a clause.
- **"Scaled deviance" $D^\ast$ vs "deviance" $D$ (lines 725-743).** The factor-of-$\phi$ relationship is stated but I found myself re-reading to keep $D$ and $D^\ast$ straight. The naming is standard but the chapter could add a one-line mnemonic.
- **Bregman divergence.** Introduced mid-proof at line 757 with no forward warning. Either define it in its own callout or strip the name.

## Pacing issues

- **The opening is fast but fair.** Lines 1-52 set up motivation and dependencies well. The dependency list at 44-52 is heavy for a beginner (it references eight Chapter 4/5/6 results by name) but it is honest. Pacing here is acceptable.
- **@sec-edf (lines 66-244) is the steepest section.** We go from the abstract EDF definition, through a full CGF proof of mean/variance, through five worked examples, in roughly 180 lines. For a weak reader this is a lot of new abstraction with no breathing room. I would split it: definition, *then* a concrete example (Bernoulli) *before* the theorem, *then* the general proof, *then* the rest of the examples. As is, the reader meets the hardest proof (lines 138-181) before any concrete grounding beyond the intro.
- **@sec-links (lines 245-385) is well paced.** Link definition, canonical link, the three concrete models, probit/cloglog. This section flows.
- **@sec-glm-estimation (lines 387-708) is relentless.** Score equations, information, Fisher scoring, IRLS, canonical Hessian, separation, asymptotic normality. Six major results with full proofs in one section, no worked example interleaved (the worked example is a separate section at the end). I strongly recommend moving @sec-glm-worked or a smaller numeric interlude *inside* this section, after @thm-irls, so the reader sees the machinery move before they meet @lem-canonical-hessian and the asymptotic theory.
- **@sec-deviance (lines 710-839) is well paced.** Clear definitions, clean proofs, good remark.
- **@sec-noncollapsibility (lines 841-992) is the chapter's highlight and is well paced** for the odds-ratio part. The hazard-ratio subsection (966-977) feels rushed by comparison, which is a pacing asymmetry given that for health researchers the HR may matter more than the OR.
- **@sec-glm-worked (lines 994-1072) is excellent but comes too late.** By the time I reached it I was algebraically exhausted. Moving an earlier, smaller numeric example into @sec-glm-estimation would restructure the chapter's energy better.
- **The exercises (lines 1101-1179) are good and well pitched**, with a mix of routine and starred problems. No pacing complaint.

## What worked well

The clinical framing (counts, adverse events, survival), the three-reason statement of why GLMs matter for ITCs, and the explicit seeding of non-collapsibility are exactly the kind of motivation I needed. More of this voice elsewhere would fix most of my complaints.
- **The non-collapsibility example @exm-noncollapsible-or (lines 910-947)** is the single most illuminating thing in the chapter. Every number is checkable by hand, the contrast with @prp-collapsibility-rd-rr is sharp, and the closing remark (949-964) tying it to Jensen's inequality is the kind of "why" explanation I want everywhere.
- **The remark on reused symbols (lines 54-64)** is a model for the whole book. Pre-empting the $\theta$/$\boldsymbol\theta$/​$\theta_{ijk}$ and $\phi$/$\Phi$ clashes saved me real confusion.
- **The worked example @sec-glm-worked (lines 994-1072)** is genuinely illuminating: closed-form MLE, one full IRLS step with numbers, and a deviance test by hand. This is the chapter at its pedagogical best.
- **The "why the variance is tied to the mean" remark (lines 183-192)** is short, clear, and gives the structural intuition behind @eq-glm-mean-variance. More remarks like this throughout would help.
- **The "IRLS in words" remark (lines 600-610)** is the only place IRLS becomes intuitive. It is well written.
- **The "consequences threaded through the book" remark (lines 979-992)** successfully connects this chapter to Chapters 11, 17, 19, and the NMA/ML-NMR framework. It is dense but it does its job.
- **The exercises**, especially @exr-glm-irls-probit, @exr-glm-noncollapse, and @exr-glm-cloglog, are well designed and would consolidate a careful reader's understanding.
- **The machine-checked tag at @prp-collapsibility-rd-rr (line 885)** is used appropriately and points at a real Coq lemma; it does not disrupt the flow.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a concrete Bernoulli example *before* @thm-glm-mean-variance.** Walk through @eq-edf-density for $Y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)$ labeling each term, then state the theorem. The current order (abstract definition, hard proof, then five examples) is backwards for a beginner.
2. **Soften the measure-theoretic opening of @sec-edf.** Either drop "reference measure $\nu$" in favor of "density or mass function" with a one-line footnote acknowledging the unified treatment, or add a short paragraph explaining why the unified measure framing is worth the abstraction. Right now it costs the beginner more than it buys.
3. **Add a strategy paragraph before the proof of @thm-glm-mean-variance.** State the plan: compute $K_Y(t)$, then read off mean and variance as $K'(0)$ and $K''(0)$. Right now the strategy is implicit.
4. **Insert a small numeric IRLS interlude inside @sec-glm-estimation**, right after @thm-irls, even if it duplicates part of @sec-glm-worked. Seeing the algorithm move once, immediately, cements the theorem.
5. **Add at least one figure**: an IRLS step for logistic regression (synthetic $z$ vs $x$, weighted least-squares line, updated fitted curve). The chapter has zero figures and the subject is geometric.
6. **Give the non-collapsibility section a clinical frame up front.** Before @def-non-collapsibility, a paragraph: "Suppose a trial reports an OR of 3.5 in both younger and older patients, yet when you pool the data the OR is 3.14. This is not a subgroup interaction and it is not confounding; it is arithmetic." Then the formal definition.
7. **Define or remove "Bregman divergence" in @prp-deviance-nonneg.** If kept, give it a short callout; if removed, the Taylor argument stands alone fine.
8. **Expand the hazard-ratio subsection (lines 966-977)** with one display showing $\mathbb E[e^{-\int h}] \neq e^{-\int \mathbb E[h]}$ and an explicit Jensen reference, even if the full proof is deferred. Currently it is the chapter's thinnest passage on the topic most relevant to health researchers.
9. **Add a "what this is for" sentence to @cor-glm-asymptotic-normality**: that this theorem is what makes `glm()` print standard errors and CIs. The technical content is there; the practical anchor is not.
10. **Reconsider the placement of @sec-glm-worked.** Either move it earlier (after @thm-irls) or split it into two interludes. Ending the formal development with a worked example is good, but the formal development itself is currently example-free for too long.
11. **Disambiguate "cumulant function" $b$ vs "cumulant generating function" $K_Y$** in a short remark near @def-exponential-family or at the start of the @thm-glm-mean-variance proof. The near-homonym is a real trap for a weak reader.
12. **Add $\nu$ to the reused-symbols remark (lines 54-64).** It is the one reused/loaded symbol not covered there, and it is the first one a beginner trips on.
