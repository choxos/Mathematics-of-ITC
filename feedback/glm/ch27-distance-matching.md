# Feedback: Chapter 27 — Distance Matching

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part6/27-distance-matching.qmd`

## Overall impression

I came to this chapter expecting to learn how to pick a comparator study when I have a single-arm trial or a disconnected network, because that is a problem I actually face in my HTA work. The chapter does eventually answer that question, and the worked example at the end (@exm-distance-comparator-ranking) was the moment I finally understood what all the abstract distances were for. But getting there was a slog. The first four sections read like a real-analysis text: dense definitions, terse proofs, and a pile of distances introduced one after another with no real-world anchor. The bias-bound section (@sec-distance-bias) is the heart of why any of this matters, and it is well argued, but it leans hard on machinery from Chapter 17 (@thm-unanchored-paic-consistency) and on Wasserstein / coupling theory that I do not think a weak-math reader can follow without more hand-holding. The caveat section (@sec-distance-caveat) is excellent and honest, and it is the part I will actually cite. Overall: rigorous and correct, but it forgets the naive researcher for long stretches, especially in sections 3 through 6.

## What was unclear

1. **The "affine contrast" assumption appears without enough setup (@sec-distance-bias-linear, line 483).** @thm-distance-linear-bias-bound supposes $\tau(\mathbf{x})=\alpha+\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{x}$. As a health researcher I know what a linear predictor is, but I had to stop and ask: is this the same as "no effect modification on the linear predictor scale"? Is this the log-odds, the log-hazard, the risk difference? The chapter says "the conditional contrast" but never pins down for a concrete outcome (say a binary outcome with a logit link) what affine-in-covariates means in practice and when it is plausible. A one-paragraph bridge from "affine contrast" to "this holds when your outcome model is a linear regression on the link scale with no interactions" would have saved me a lot of effort.

2. **The Wasserstein distance is dropped on the reader with no definition beyond the infimum over couplings (@thm-lipschitz-bias-bound, lines 588-596).** I have basic calculus; I do not know what a "coupling" of two probability measures is, what $\Pi(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})$ means intuitively, or why an infimum over joint laws is the right object. The proof (lines 599-617) uses "let $\gamma$ be any coupling, that is, a joint law of a pair whose first marginal is $\mathcal{P}_j$" as if that explains it. For a Part VI chapter aimed at readers who have survived Parts I to V, a two-sentence intuition ("a coupling is a way of pairing up patients from the two populations; the Wasserstein distance is the cheapest average distance you have to move people to turn one distribution into the other") would make the whole section legible.

3. **"Conditional constancy" is invoked as the working assumption (line 462-464) but not re-explained.** The text says "we work under conditional constancy, so that the conditional contrast ... is the same function of the covariates in the index and comparator populations." I gathered this is @def-conditional-constancy-absolute from earlier chapters, but in a Part VI extension chapter I would have liked a one-line restatement, because everything in @sec-distance-bias hinges on it and I was not 100% sure whether "constancy of absolute effects" is the same as "the conditional contrast is the same function."

4. **The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality remark (lines 619-626) is incomprehensible to me.** It says the bound "is the elementary, coupling side of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality $W_\rho(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}_\rho}\le1}|\mathbb{E}_\mathcal{P}f-\mathbb{E}_\mathcal{Q}f|$." I do not know what $\mathrm{Lip}_\rho$ denotes as a norm, I have never seen this duality, and the remark does not tell me why I should care about it if the coupling proof is already self-contained. This reads like a note for the author, not for the reader. Either motivate it ("this duality shows the Wasserstein distance is itself the worst-case bias over all Lipschitz contrasts, which is why it is the natural object") or cut it.

5. **The "coherent rescaling" definition (@thm-distance-scale-invariance, lines 378-382) confused me.** The theorem says "apply the coherent rescaling that maps every summary vector $\mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ and rescales the auxiliary inputs in step." I had to read it three times to understand that the point is: if you record age in months, you also record the standard deviation in months, so the ratio is unchanged. Calling it "coherent" without first giving the concrete example (age in years vs months, with SD in years vs months) made the formal statement land cold. Put the example first.

6. **The $\|\mathbf{v}\|_A$ notation (line 62) is introduced once and then used heavily.** I eventually got it from the notation.qmd entry, but in-chapter the jump from "$\sqrt{\mathbf{v}^\top A\mathbf{v}}$" to "$\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_j\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}$" as if it is obvious was a small stumble. A reminder the first time it appears in @eq-distance-mahalanobis that this is just the quadratic-form norm defined in the intro would help.

## What wasn't elaborated enough

1. **The link between distance minimization and the downstream MAIC/STC/ML-UMR is asserted but not shown.** Line 83 says the winner "is fed into an unanchored MAIC, STC, or ML-UMR," and the "what a good choice buys" remark (lines 100-108) lists less reweighting, higher ESS, nearer-interior support. But there is no worked pipeline: pick study $D$ by Mahalanobis, then run a MAIC on it, then show the weights, then show the ESS, then show the estimate. The chapter stops at "feed it in." For a methods user, a paragraph or a small schematic showing the comparator selection as Step 0 of the Part IV pipeline would make the chapter feel connected rather than freestanding.

2. **The proof of @thm-mahalanobis-whitening (lines 197-226) is correct but very compressed on the existence of the square root.** Lines 198-204 cite @thm-cov-psd and the spectral construction in one breath. A weaker reader will not remember why a positive-definite matrix has a spectral decomposition with positive eigenvalues, or why that gives a square root. Given the book's stated goal of taking a naive reader to mastery, a one-sentence recall ("by the spectral theorem of Chapter 2, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)$ and every $\lambda_i>0$ because $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ0$, so $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}=Q\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_i})Q^\top$") would have made the existence argument self-contained here.

3. **The metric-properties proof (@prp-distance-metric-properties, lines 328-364) packs a lot into one block.** Part 2 proves the triangle inequality for $q=1,2,\infty$ directly and then says "for general $q\in[1,\infty)$ the inequality is the named Minkowski inequality." For a weak-math reader, the jump from the three explicit cases to "and for general $q$ it is Minkowski" is a gap: it is exactly the case the reader cannot verify. Either prove Minkowski from Hölder in a short lemma, or explicitly say "we cite Minkowski's inequality; its proof from Hölder is Exercise @exr-distance-minkowski-metric" so the reader knows where to look.

4. **The non-identification proof Part 1 (@prp-distance-diagnostic-not-validity, lines 690-703) is the centerpiece caveat and deserves more space.** The counterexample $\mathcal{P}_0=\delta_0$ versus $\mathcal{P}_j=\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_{+1}$ with $\tau(x)=x^2$ is beautiful and short, but the parenthetical at lines 700-703 that checks the Lipschitz bound and the Wasserstein distance is doing a lot of work in one sentence ("the Wasserstein distance is $W_\rho=1$, the optimal coupling moving each $\pm1$ to $0$"). For the reader who is shaky on Wasserstein, spelling out that optimal coupling in two lines, and explicitly stating $|B_j|=1 \le L\cdot W_\rho = 2$, would turn a parenthetical into a teaching moment.

5. **The Coq connection section (@sec-distance-coq, lines 898-913) is thin and slightly defensive.** It says the chapter is "not part of the companion Coq development" and then argues the ingredients are already mechanized elsewhere. That is fine, but the section reads like an apology. It would be more useful to either (a) give the one-line Coq statement that would certify @thm-distance-linear-bias-bound if it were formalized, or (b) say plainly that distance matching is a design heuristic and does not need formal certification, so its absence from `theories/` is intentional rather than a backlog item.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

1. **The biggest missing motivation is at the very top.** The introduction (@sec-distance-intro) explains that disconnected networks and single-arm studies force a comparator choice, and that is good. But it never says *how common* this is or *what is at stake*. As an HTA researcher I want to know: is this the oncology single-arm-trial problem? Is this the situation where a new drug gets accelerated approval on a single-arm Phase II and I have to compare it to a standard-of-care drawn from a Phase III? Naming a concrete disease area (say, a single-arm trial of a novel CAR-T therapy versus external lymphoma registries) in the first paragraph would instantly tell me this chapter is for me.

2. **Why entropy balancing is not in this chapter, despite the title's framing.** The orchestrator brief and the file name "distance matching" suggest entropy balancing belongs here (the feedback prompt explicitly mentions it), but the chapter never mentions entropy balancing, the KL divergence, or Hainmueller's method. As a reader who knows entropy balancing is the dominant individual-level reweighting tool and who expected to see it contrasted with aggregate distance matching, I am confused about where it went. Even one paragraph saying "entropy balancing operates at the individual level and is the subject of Chapter 18 / 27-adjacent material; this chapter is strictly aggregate-level covariate-summary matching, which is a design step upstream of any reweighting" would locate the chapter correctly in the reader's mental map.

3. **Why distance matching versus just running MAIC on every candidate.** A natural question I had by section 2: if I have four candidate comparators, why not just run four MAICs and pick the one with the best ESS or the most stable weights? The chapter hints at this in the "what a good choice buys" remark and in @exr-distance-ess-tradeoff, but never directly argues "because MAIC on a badly matched comparator can fail to converge, can produce extreme weights, and gives you no pre-registration handle; distance matching is a cheap, pre-analysis, reproducible screen." That argument is the practical reason the method exists, and it is missing.

4. **Why prove the scale-invariance theorem at all (lines 376-413)?** The remark at lines 447-455 gives the decision-theoretic moral, which is good, but the *motivation* could be sharper: a regulator will ask "would your comparator choice change if the sponsor had reported age in months?" and the theorem is the answer. Framing it as a regulatory-robustness result rather than a pure-algebra result would make the reader care before the proof.

5. **Why the Lipschitz-Wasserstein bound matters if we then immediately show it is not achievable by mean distances.** Section @sec-distance-bias-nonlinear proves a bound (@thm-lipschitz-bias-bound) and then @prp-distance-wasserstein-lower-bound shows mean distance does not upper-bound Wasserstein, so the bound is not usable with the distances we actually have. A weak reader will ask "then why did we just prove it?" The answer, that it tells us *what* would be needed (a full-distribution distance) and therefore *why* mean-based matching is only a diagnostic, is present but buried at the end of the remark at line 628-630. Lead with the punchline: "this section proves that the linear bound has no nonlinear analogue at the level of means, which is the deepest reason distance matching is a diagnostic and not a theorem."

## Missing examples and intuition

1. **There is exactly one worked example in the whole chapter (@exm-distance-comparator-ranking), and it is excellent but comes very late (line 751).** Sections 3, 4, 5, and 6 each introduce a new distance or theorem with no mini-example. A one-line numerical illustration after @def-euclidean-distance ("if the index has mean age 60 and Study A has mean age 66, with SD 10, the standardized age gap is 0.6 SD") would have made each definition concrete as it landed.

2. **The Mahalanobis intuition (remark at lines 228-238) is good but abstract.** It says a high-variance direction is "cheap to differ in" and a discordant move against a correlation is "expensive." I wanted a picture or a concrete pair of candidates. The worked example eventually supplies one (Study D moving age and biomarker together, lines 833-836), but that insight is 600 lines after the definition. A small inline example immediately after @thm-mahalanobis-whitening would have fixed the intuition before the reader hits the bias section.

3. **No clinical / drug / trial scenario anywhere until the worked example, and even there it is only "a single-arm index study" with covariates age, sex, biomarker.** What disease? What drug? What is the comparator? Is the outcome overall survival, response rate, PFS? The chapter is in Part VI (applications), and the brief explicitly asks for concrete drug/trial scenarios. A two-sentence framing in the introduction ("consider a single-arm Phase II of a novel oncology agent reporting only mean age, sex proportion, and mean baseline hemoglobin; the external comparators are four Phase III trials of competing regimens reporting the same three summaries") would transform the chapter from abstract to applied.

4. **The Gower distance (@def-distance-variants, lines 283-292) has no example at all.** It is defined, mentioned as handling mixed continuous and binary covariates, and never illustrated. Since it is the one distance that explicitly handles the mixed type common in real trial baselines, a two-line example with one continuous and one binary covariate would help.

5. **The unmeasured-imbalance counterexample (@prp-distance-diagnostic-not-validity Part 2, lines 705-718) is abstract.** It says "let an unobserved prognostic variable $W$ have mean 0 in the index and 1 in the comparator." As a health researcher I read this and thought: okay, but what is $W$ in practice? Disease severity? Frailty? A comorbidity not recorded in the aggregate report? Naming a concrete unmeasured prognostic factor (say, performance status not reported in the AgD) would make the caveat land much harder, because that is exactly the kind of variable that differs between a single-arm Phase II and an external Phase III.

## Notation and jargon problems

1. **$\boldsymbol{\delta}_j$ is defined as $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_0$ (line 60), but in the notation.qmd the symbol $\delta_{j,\,t_1 t_k}$ means a study-$j$ treatment contrast (line 118).** Two different uses of $\delta$ in the same book is a collision. The chapter's $\boldsymbol{\delta}_j$ is a covariate gap; the notation file's $\delta$ is a treatment-effect contrast. A reader cross-referencing will be confused. Consider $\mathbf{g}_j$ or $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_j$ for the gap.

2. **"$\ell$-th entry" / covariate index $\ell\in\{1,\dots,p\}$ (line 61)** is fine, but the proofs sometimes write $\delta_{j\ell}$ and sometimes $\delta_\ell$ (e.g., line 822: "$d_M^2=\tfrac{1}{75}\delta_1^2+4\delta_2^2+\tfrac{1}{12}\delta_3^2-\tfrac{1}{30}\delta_1\delta_3$" drops the $j$). In a multi-candidate setting the missing $j$ made me double-check which study was being evaluated.

3. **"$\succ0$" and "$\succeq0$"** (used throughout, e.g., line 161, 178) are in notation.qmd but a weak-math reader will not remember positive-definite versus positive-semidefinite from Part I by the time they reach Part VI. A one-line reminder at the first use in @def-mahalanobis-distance would help.

4. **"Whitening" (line 173, 228) is jargon from signal processing / multivariate stats.** The chapter uses it as if it is self-explanatory. The remark at 228-238 explains it, but the term itself is introduced at line 157 ("decorrelates and standardizes ... simultaneously") without saying "this combined operation is called whitening." Define the term at first use.

5. **"SEMA" and "SPFA"** appear in notation.qmd (line 146) but the chapter uses "shared prognostic factor assumption (@def-spfa)" and "shared effect modifier assumption" inline, which is good. But @def-spfa, @def-conditional-constancy-absolute, @def-effect-modifier, @def-prognostic-variable are all forward/backward references the reader must chase. In a Part VI chapter, one-line glosses in parentheses the first time each appears would reduce the cross-reference load.

6. **"Bristol Compass blog" (line 20, 921)** is cited twice as the practical source but is not in the bibliography visible here; the notes say "bibliographic entries ... are proposed in the chapter work log." For the reader this is a dangling reference. Either include the URL in a footnote or mark it clearly as forthcoming.

## Pacing issues

1. **The introduction (lines 5-54) is very long and very dense for an opening.** It packs the motivation, the menu of distances, the scale-invariance question, the linear-bias result, the Lipschitz-Wasserstein result, the central caveat, the worked example preview, and the catalogue correspondence into one 50-line paragraph block. As a weak reader I was exhausted before section 2. Splitting it into a short "what and why" paragraph and a "what this chapter proves" paragraph, or moving the catalogue correspondence (lines 52-54) to the notes, would give the reader a breath.

2. **Sections 3, 4, and 5 (@sec-distance-mahalanobis, @sec-distance-variants, @sec-distance-scale) are a parade of definitions and proofs with no narrative arc.** Each introduces a distance, proves it is a metric or is scale-invariant, and moves on. The reader cannot tell which one matters most. A one-line "if you read only one distance, read standardized Euclidean" or "the rest of this section is reference material" signpost would help the weak reader prioritize.

3. **The bias section (@sec-distance-bias) ramps up very fast.** It goes from the transport-bias definition (line 466) to the Cauchy-Schwarz bound (line 481) to the Wasserstein bound (line 581) to the lower-bound proposition (line 632) in under 200 lines, with two full proofs. This is the conceptual core and it deserved more room, more intuition, and at least one inline numerical check before the big worked example at the end.

4. **The worked example (@exm-distance-comparator-ranking, lines 758-884) is the right pace and the right length.** It is the best-paced section in the chapter. The lesson is that the chapter would be easier if earlier sections matched this density of concrete arithmetic.

5. **The exercises (@sec-distance-exercises) are good but several are pitched well above a weak-math reader.** @exr-distance-minkowski-metric asks the reader to prove Minkowski from Hölder; @exr-distance-affine-invariance is starred; @exr-distance-secondmoment is starred. There is no easy first exercise that just asks the reader to compute a distance by hand on a tiny example. Adding one "compute $d_{SE}$ and $d_M$ for two candidates with two covariates" exercise at the top would give the weak reader a foothold.

## What worked well

1. **@exm-distance-comparator-ranking is genuinely excellent.** Four studies, four distances, four different winners, the exact bias computed, and the reversal demonstrated with numbers. This is the clearest part of the chapter and the part that taught me the most. The equality-case check for Study B and Study D (lines 868-883) is a lovely touch that makes the Cauchy-Schwarz equality conditions tangible.

2. **The central caveat (@sec-distance-caveat, @prp-distance-diagnostic-not-validity) is honest and well-stated.** The two-part structure (nonlinearity + unmeasured imbalance) cleanly delimits what the method can and cannot do, and the "what aggregate-level matching is, and is not, good for" remark (lines 721-733) is the kind of crisp scope statement I can quote in a methods section. This is the chapter at its best.

3. **The "what a good choice buys, and what it does not" remark (lines 100-108)** is a model of honest motivation: smaller gap means less reweighting, higher ESS, nearer-interior support, but no validity guarantee. More of this voice earlier would help.

4. **The scale-dependence reversal proof (@prp-distance-scale-dependence, lines 429-445)** is short, concrete, and persuasive. The perturbation argument at the end (lines 441-443) turning a tie into a strict reversal is exactly the kind of arithmetic that convinces a weak reader.

5. **The connection to the unanchored consistency theorem (remark at lines 560-573)** is the right kind of cross-reference: it says precisely which hypothesis of @thm-unanchored-paic-consistency the affine specialization corresponds to, and it states the payoff ("comparator selection bounds the very bias the consistency theorem assumes away"). This is how Part VI should connect to Parts IV and V.

6. **The closing pointer to Chapter 28 for the unmeasured-confounding part (lines 946-949)** is well placed and gives the reader a clear next step.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a concrete clinical framing in the introduction.** Name a disease, a drug class, a single-arm trial, and a set of external comparators. Carry that same scenario through to the worked example so the example reads as a continuation, not a fresh setup.

2. **Add a one-paragraph "where entropy balancing and individual-level reweighting live" note** near the introduction or in the notes, so readers expecting entropy balancing (the title-adjacent topic) know this chapter is strictly aggregate-level and where to find the individual-level material.

3. **Front-load intuition for Wasserstein and couplings** in @sec-distance-bias-nonlinear. Two sentences of "a coupling pairs patients from the two populations; the Wasserstein distance is the minimum average distance over all such pairings" before the formal infimum would make the section reachable.

4. **Restate "conditional constancy" and "affine contrast" in plain terms** at the start of @sec-distance-bias, with a concrete outcome example (binary outcome, logit link, linear predictor with no interactions) so the health reader knows exactly when the linear bound applies.

5. **Add a tiny inline numerical example after each distance definition** (one line of arithmetic each), not just the big one at the end.

6. **Resolve the $\boldsymbol{\delta}_j$ versus $\delta_{j,t_1t_k}$ notation collision** with notation.qmd, or at minimum flag it in a footnote.

7. **Split the introduction** into a short motivation and a separate "roadmap of what we prove" paragraph, and move the catalogue correspondence to the notes section to lighten the opening.

8. **Add an easy first exercise** that asks the reader to compute two distances by hand on a two-covariate, two-candidate example, before the harder proof-based exercises.

9. **Expand the unmeasured-imbalance counterexample (Part 2)** with a named prognostic variable (e.g., performance status unreported in AgD) so the caveat is immediately recognizable to a health researcher.

10. **Either justify the Kantorovich-Rubinstein remark (lines 619-626) with intuition or remove it;** in its current form it is aimed at the author, not the reader.
