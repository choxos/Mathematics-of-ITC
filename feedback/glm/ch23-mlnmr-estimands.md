# Feedback: Chapter 23 — ML-NMR Estimands

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part5/23-mlnmr-estimands.qmd`

## Overall impression

I came in hoping to understand what an ML-NMR *estimand* actually is, because in my health-policy day job "estimand" is the word regulators (FDA, NICE, IQWiG) hammer on: you must say *what* you are estimating, *for whom*, and *on what scale* before anyone trusts a number. This chapter does eventually tell me that, and the worked example (@exm-mlnmr-new-target) is genuinely the part where I felt "okay, I get it now." But getting there was rough. The introduction reads like it was written for someone who already finished the book; the notation lands all at once in lines 54 to 59 with zero unpacking; and the central theorem (@thm-target-population-integral) drops four lettered parts on me (a, b, c, d) before I have any intuitive picture of why "integrate then contrast" versus "contrast then integrate" is even a real distinction a clinician should care about. The identifiability sections are technically impressive but I lost the clinical thread almost completely; I could not tell you after one read why a health researcher should ever care about SEMA failing beyond a vague "your answer is wrong." The chapter clearly knows its subject deeply; it does not yet remember what it is like not to.

## What was unclear

**The four-lettered theorem arrives too compressed (@thm-target-population-integral, lines 183 to 223).** Part (a) asserts both estimands are "the ML-NMR realization of the transport g-formula," but I do not have a crisp mental picture of the transport g-formula from Chapter 17 anymore by this point in the book; the cross-reference (@thm-target-population-estimand) is a name, not a recap. A two-sentence "here is what the transport g-formula said in words, and here is the one piece ML-NMR plugs in" before the theorem would have saved me re-opening Chapter 17. As written, part (a) is a wall of cross-references.

**The order-of-operations distinction (@def-mlnmr-target-contrast, lines 162 to 178) is stated but not motivated before it matters.** The chapter tells me @eq-mlnmr-est-marg integrates then contrasts and @eq-mlnmr-est-cond contrasts then integrates, and that this "distinguishes them." But it does not say *why a health researcher should care* until the collapsibility dichotomy in part (c), several paragraphs later. I read the definition assuming the two were interchangeable bookkeeping; the punchline that they are different quantities with different data requirements comes too late.

**Part (c) proof is too terse for me (lines 246 to 263).** The "non-collapsible case" paragraph invokes @thm-aggregation-bias "applied to the strictly convex or strictly concave g^{-1}" and asserts the two arm discrepancies "do not cancel in general." I had to stop and reason: wait, why is g^{-1} necessarily strictly convex or strictly concave? For logit, expit is sigmoid, which is convex then concave; it is not globally convex. The sentence glosses over the sign-change and the fact that Jensen on two arms does not simply add. I would have wanted either a cleaner invocation (e.g., strict Jensen on each arm separately, then a note on why the biases differ in magnitude because the baselines differ) or an explicit pointer to where the sign convexity is addressed.

**`$\bar x_{*}$` dropping the prognostic effects `$\boldsymbol\beta_1$` (line 207) felt like magic.** Part (b) concludes the conditional contrast "depends on P* only through the covariate mean, and not at all on the baseline μ_* or on the prognostic effects β_1." The proof (lines 239 to 244) says "neither μ_* nor β_1 appears, because they canceled in @eq-mlnmr-est-condcontrast." True, but a one-line intuition would help: the prognostic effects shift both arms equally, so they cancel in any *contrast*. I had to reverse-engineer that; a parenthetical "(the prognostic effects shift both arms by the same amount, so they leave every contrast unchanged)" would have made it click.

**The "$s_{jk}:=\mu_j+\gamma_k$" notation in @thm-mlnmr-identifiability-large (line 582) was introduced silently.** The chapter suddenly writes `s_{jk}` without it appearing in notation.qmd or being defined inline. I had to infer it is the combined intercept. Defining it explicitly ("write $s_{jk}:=\mu_j+\gamma_k$ for the per-cell intercept") the first time it appears would save a reread.

**Line 506 to 508 (the `dγ_B/dβ_2` derivative) is dense and unexplained.** The proposition @prp-mlnmr-sema-necessary tells me the contrast difference is stationary at `$\bar x_{*}=q$`, and the proof cites a derivative `$d\gamma_B/d\beta_2=-q\,\dot g_1/[(1-q)\dot g_0+q\dot g_1]$` where `$\dot g_0,\dot g_1>0$` are "derivatives of g^{-1} at the two strata." I do not know at which strata these derivatives are evaluated (at the C-stratum linear predictor? the B-stratum?). The notation `$\dot g_0,\dot g_1$` is introduced in the middle of a derivation with no definition of the evaluation points. This is the densest line in the chapter and the one I most wanted expanded.

## What wasn't elaborated enough

**The monotonicity lemma @lem-mlnmr-aggregate-monotone (lines 302 to 337) is the engine of the entire identifiability argument, and its proof flies by.** The finiteness argument (lines 317 to 321) handles the "otherwise the one-sided monotone bound together with finiteness of Θ(c_0) controls the integral on the relevant side" with a hand-wave that I could not parse on first reading; I needed the explicit domination spelled out. Given that *every* identifiability result in the chapter rests on this lemma, a more careful finiteness argument (or at least an acknowledgment that the case where the limits ℓ, u are not finite, e.g. log link with range (0,∞), needs separate domination) would repay itself.

**The exchangeable-EM case (part 3 of @thm-mlnmr-identifiability-large, lines 556 to 562 and 610 to 635) is the most hand-waved proof in the chapter.** It says the posterior "integrates to one (the likelihood is bounded and the prior is proper, so their product has finite integral)" but never argues the likelihood is bounded on a noncompact parameter space, and never addresses that the AgD-only interaction is *not* likelihood-identified, only prior-identified. A reader at my level reads this as "we waved at Bayesian identifiability." The contrast with the crisp rank arguments of parts 1 and 2 is stark; the honest admission that part 3 is weaker is welcome but the proof itself does not convey the limitation, only the surrounding remark does.

**The generality theorem's MAIC reduction (part (c), lines 728 to 750) never tells me the practical upshot.** The proof shows the two estimators share a limit under a *linear* (affine-mean) surface, and that "on a nonlinear surface the two limits can differ." This is the clinically crucial point (it tells me when ML-NMR and MAIC disagree) but it is buried at the end of a dense proof paragraph. A one-line remark up front, e.g. "the two agree exactly when the conditional mean is affine in x; departures from affinity are where ML-NMR's explicit integration beats MAIC's moment matching" would have surfaced the actionable insight.

**The "correct specification on the covariate support of P*" hypothesis (@thm-target-population-integral, line 189) is mentioned once and never returned to.** For a health researcher this is the assumption that scares me: it says my parametric model has to hold outside the observed covariate region if I extrapolate. The remark at lines 283 to 292 gestures at this ("the honesty cost is...") but never connects it to a concrete clinical scenario (e.g., extrapolating to a frailer target population than any trial enrolled). This is the single assumption a decision-maker will challenge, and it deserves a fuller treatment than a passing remark.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

**The whole "which population?" framing (lines 21 to 32) never lands as a clinical decision.** The introduction says we may report the relative effect "in the population of either trial, in a third decision-relevant population, or in any population whose covariate distribution we can describe." That is true and important, but it is abstract. A health researcher lives this question concretely: NICE asks "what is the incremental benefit of drug B versus A in the *UK NHS* population, which is older and sicker than either pivotal trial?" The chapter should open with that. Right now the motivating scenario is structural ("a fitted model is a surface; an indirect comparison is a single number") rather than decisional. I never felt the policy stakes.

**Why prove the no-SEMA proposition @prp-mlnmr-sema-necessary at all?** The chapter proves that without SEMA the two-study contrast is identified only in the comparator population. The clinical translation, buried in the remark at lines 513 to 523, is: *if you cannot defend that the effect modifier acts the same way for both drugs, your indirect comparison cannot be transported anywhere except the trial you already have.* That is a profound practical limitation and it is the reason SEMA is "defended by pharmacological argument and sensitivity analysis, not extracted from a two-study network" (line 521 to 522). The proposition deserves a worked clinical example: imagine two oncology drugs where the effect modifier is a biomarker; if the biomarker-treatment interaction genuinely differs across drugs, show me concretely that the indirect estimate in a target population is unmoored. Without it, the proposition reads as pure geometry.

**The "superpower and its price" remark (lines 269 to 281) is the best motivator in the chapter and it comes too late.** It says the point of ML-NMR (report in any population) and its price (you must name the target and supply baseline risk on a non-collapsible scale). This belongs near the top of @sec-mlnmr-est-estimand, not as a post-proof remark. I would have engaged with the theorem far better had I known the stakes first.

**The collapsibility dichotomy (part (c)) is the heart of the chapter and is never connected to the FDA/EMA estimand-strategy debate.** Regulators explicitly distinguish marginal (population-average) and conditional (subgroup-specific) treatment effects, and the choice has real consequences (conditional log-odds ratios are transportable across populations with different baseline risks; marginal odds ratios are not). This chapter proves exactly that fact (@thm-target-population-integral(c)) but never names it as the same debate regulators are having. A footnote or a remark saying "this is the math behind why regulators care about marginal vs conditional effect measures" would tie the math to a stake a health researcher recognizes.

## Missing examples and intuition

**No running clinical scenario.** The chapter has exactly one numeric example (@exm-mlnmr-new-target) and it is abstract (treatments A, B, C; a binary effect modifier X; no disease, no outcome). For a beginner this is fine for checking arithmetic but it fails to build intuition. Compare to what would help: "Drug A is a new oral anticoagulant, drug B is warfarin, drug C is standard care; the effect modifier is age (older patients bleed more on anticoagulants); the IPD trial enrolled younger fitter patients, the AgD trial on B-vs-C enrolled older frailer ones; NICE wants the effect in the UK population." Even one paragraph setting up this frame before the math would make every subsequent result land.

**The identifiability sections have zero example.** @thm-mlnmr-identifiability-2study, @prp-mlnmr-sema-necessary, and @thm-mlnmr-identifiability-large are theorem-proof-remark, theorem-proposition-proof-remark, theorem-proof-remark. No example illustrates "here is a small network, here is what the data look like, here is what we can and cannot recover." A tiny worked example after @prp-mlnmr-sema-necessary showing two parameter pairs that produce identical AgD data but different target contrasts (the chapter even constructs them analytically in lines 487 to 510) would convert an abstract "manifold" into a tangible object. The analytic construction is right there; it just needs numbers attached.

**No diagram.** For a chapter organized around "collapse a surface to a number by averaging over a population," a picture would help enormously: a 2D plot of the conditional mean surface in x, the two arm curves, the target density f_* overlaid, and the marginal means marked as integrals. The anchored triangle is also a natural place for a small graph diagram (it is referenced as "canonical anchored triangle" repeatedly, lines 358, 689). The chapter is entirely visual-content-free.

**The "baseline cancels on a collapsible scale" intuition (line 141, 250) deserves a picture.** On the identity scale the arm surface shifts vertically by the baseline, so subtracting arms removes it; on a logit scale the sigmoid bends the shift, so subtracting does not remove it. This is exactly the kind of thing a beginner sees instantly with a figure and slowly with algebra.

## Notation and jargon problems

**`$f_{*}$` abbreviation (line 56).** The chapter introduces `$f_{\mathcal P^*}$` abbreviated to `$f_{*}$` in line 56 but then uses `$f_{*}$` and the un-abbreviated `$f_{\mathcal P^*}$` somewhat interchangeably thereafter (e.g., line 153 uses `$f_{*}$`, line 215 uses `$f_{*}$`, the theorem statement line 188 uses `$\mathcal P^*$` consistently). Mild but noticeable.

**`$\bar\theta_t(\mathcal P^*)$` versus `$\mu_t^{*}(x)$` (lines 151 to 175).** The bar-over-theta for the *marginal* (population-averaged) mean and the superscript-star-subscript for the *conditional* mean at x differ subtly and the distinction is load-bearing. A beginner will confuse `$\bar\theta_t$` (a number) with `$\mu_t^*(x)$` (a function of x). The notation.qmd entry for `$\mu_t(x)$` (line 101) is the causal version; the ML-NMR `$\mu_t^{\mathcal P}(x)$` is not separately listed in notation.qmd. Adding it would help.

**"Affine" vs "linear" used interchangeably (lines 199, 239, 739).** The chapter calls the conditional contrast "affine in x" (line 239) and elsewhere "linear" (e.g., "affine-mean (linear-surface, first-moment) conditions," line 692). For a beginner these are different words meaning different things (affine includes an intercept; linear in the strict sense does not). Pick one and stick to it, or clarify that "linear surface" here means affine.

**`$s_0:=\mu_{BC}$`, `$s_1:=\mu_{BC}+\beta_1$` (line 488 to 489).** These abbreviations appear once, in the densest proof, and are never used again. They added cognitive load without saving much. Spelling them out would be clearer given they are used in exactly one equation.

**"Probability limit" (lines 51, 736, 743) is statistical jargon a weak-math health researcher will not recognize.** The term means "the value an estimator converges to as sample size grows," but it is introduced without definition. A parenthetical the first time it appears would help.

**`$\operatorname{expit}$` (lines 819 to 821) appears without definition in the example.** It is the inverse-logit, but notation.qmd does not list it. A beginner meeting it for the first time needs one line: "expit(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z}), the inverse-logit."

## Pacing issues

**The introduction (lines 5 to 59) front-loads three questions and the notation in one sweep.** Lines 21 to 52 are three rich paragraphs answering "which quantity," "is it identified," "how does it relate to earlier methods," each naming multiple cross-referenced theorems. For a beginner this is a wall. A gentler sequencing would be: state the three questions briefly, defer the cross-references to the body, and let each section earn its motivation. As written I felt I needed to have read the chapter already to read the introduction.

**The jump from @lem-mlnmr-surface-transport (lines 97 to 132) straight to @thm-target-population-integral (lines 183 to 223) is steep.** The lemma establishes conditional-contrast-is-population-free and absolute-arm-needs-baseline; the theorem immediately leverages both into a four-part identification-and-integral result. There is no intervening intuition-building. A short example between the two, showing on the identity scale how the cancellation works and on the logit scale how it fails, would bridge the gap.

**@sec-mlnmr-est-monotone (the monotonicity lemma, lines 295 to 349) interrupts the identifiability narrative.** The chapter puts a technical lemma between the estimand section and the two-study identifiability section. The lemma's own remark (lines 339 to 348) previews its role ("this gap is precisely the obstruction that SEMA removes"), which is good, but the placement still feels like a detour. Either fold the lemma into the two-study section as a named tool, or motivate up front why it must precede the identifiability proofs.

**The generality theorem (@thm-mlnmr-generalizes-nma, lines 671 to 702) with its four parts and long proof (lines 704 to 769) lands as a climax but at the point of exhaustion.** By line 700 the reader has absorbed two identifiability theorems and a proposition; the generality theorem's four sub-results are each substantial (the MAIC reduction alone is lines 728 to 750) and the proof runs unbroken. Breaking it into four labeled mini-sections or interleaving remarks would ease the load.

## What worked well

**The worked example @exm-mlnmr-new-target (lines 798 to 889) is excellent and is the chapter's clearest teaching moment.** Concrete numbers, two target populations, the anchored identity verified numerically, the non-collapsibility gap exhibited as a number (0.022502), the conditional-vs-marginal contrast drawn sharply. This is the section I would hand to a colleague. The follow-up remark varying the baseline (lines 891 to 901) doubles down on the same pedagogy and is just as good.

**The "where each hypothesis is spent" remark (lines 653 to 662) is a model of clarity.** It names each assumption and tells me exactly which block of the proof uses it and what breaks if it is removed. Every theorem in the chapter deserves this treatment.

**The honest handling of the exchangeable-EM limitation (lines 644 to 650, 654 to 661).** The remark openly states that part 3 is Bayesian-only, that the frequentist rank argument has no analogue for AgD-only interactions, and that "this is a genuine limitation, not an oversight." For a textbook this is rare and valuable.

**The "superpower and its price" remark (lines 269 to 281)** is the most important single paragraph in the chapter for a health researcher: it states in plain terms what ML-NMR buys and what it costs. Its only flaw is placement.

**The exercises (lines 958 to 1019) are well-calibrated**, ranging from a hand-computation (@exr-mlnmr-third-target) to a closed-form derivation (@exr-mlnmr-sema-gap) to a conceptual STC-reduction proof (@exr-mlnmr-stc-reduction). They genuinely probe understanding rather than symbol manipulation.

## Suggestions

1. **Open with a clinical scenario.** Replace the abstract introduction with one paragraph featuring real drug classes (e.g., a new anticoagulant vs warfarin vs standard care, an age effect modifier, an IPD trial and an AgD trial, a UK NHS target). Carry this scenario as a recurring thread into @exm-mlnmr-new-target and at least one identifiability example.
2. **Move the "superpower and its price" remark (lines 269 to 281) to the start of @sec-mlnmr-est-estimand,** before @def-mlnmr-target-contrast. State the stakes before the definitions.
3. **Add a figure:** a 2D plot of two arm conditional means vs x with the target density overlay and the marginal means marked; and a small graph diagram of the anchored triangle at its first mention (line 358).
4. **Expand part (c) of @thm-target-population-integral's proof** to address the strict-convexity/strict-concavity subtlety explicitly, or split the non-collapsible case into "each arm separately biased by Jensen" plus "the biases differ because the baselines differ."
5. **Add a numeric example to @prp-mlnmr-sema-necessary.** The analytic construction of two observationally-identical parameter pairs (lines 487 to 510) is begging for numbers; attach the same coefficients as @exm-mlnmr-new-target and show the two pairs give the same AgD data but different target contrasts at, say, `$\bar x_{*}=0.2$` versus the comparator mean `$q=0.6$`.
6. **Connect the collapsibility dichotomy to the regulatory estimand debate** in a remark: name it as the same marginal-vs-conditional choice that ICH-E9(R1) and the FDA estimand guidance discuss. This ties the pure math to a stake health researchers already know.
7. **Define `$s_{jk}:=\mu_j+\gamma_k$` explicitly** at line 582, and spell out the evaluation points of `$\dot g_0,\dot g_1$` at lines 506 to 508.
8. **Expand the correct-specification-on-target-support remark (lines 283 to 292)** with a concrete extrapolation example (frailer target than any trial) and a pointer to how one would do a sensitivity analysis there.
9. **Slow down the introduction:** state the three questions, defer the theorem cross-references, and move the notation dump (lines 54 to 59) to a boxed "Notation in this chapter" callout so it does not interrupt the motivating prose.
10. **Add `$\mu_t^{\mathcal P}(x)$`, `$\operatorname{expit}$`, and "probability limit" to notation.qmd** or define them inline on first use.
