# Feedback: Chapter 08 — Hierarchical and Bayesian Modeling

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part1/08-hierarchical-bayes.qmd`

## Overall impression

I came in hoping to understand why hierarchical models matter for meta-analysis and ITCs, since that is the whole point of my work. The chapter is clearly written by someone who knows the math deeply, and the opening paragraph (lines 5-16) actually does a great job motivating the "between extremes" framing of no-pooling versus complete-pooling. That hooked me. But very quickly, around the exchangeability section, I lost the thread and never fully got it back. The chapter front-loads a lot of measure-theoretic probability (sigma-algebras, dominating measures, Radon-Nikodym, Tonelli, Fubini, disintegration) that is way above my head, and it does so before giving me a single concrete meta-analysis example I can hold onto. By the time the worked example at @sec-hb-worked (line 1143) finally arrived, I had already struggled through five dense sections with no numerical anchor. The pacing is back-loaded: all the intuition comes at the end when it should have been threaded through the whole chapter. I genuinely believe a reader with my background would give up around @thm-de-finetti or @thm-bayes. The chapter reads like it was written for someone who already knows Bayesian statistics and just wants the proofs filled in, not for a naive researcher being taken to mastery.

## What was unclear

- **@def-exchangeability (line 45) and the notation $\stackrel{d}{=}$**: The definition uses equality in distribution with a permutation, which is clean, but I had to puzzle out what $\stackrel{d}{=}$ means. It is not in the notation table I was given (I checked notation.qmd). For a beginner this is the very first wall. A one-line gloss "the two random vectors have the same joint distribution" does appear right after, but the symbol itself is never defined in the notation reference.

- **@lem-iid-mixture-exchangeable (line 64) and its proof**: The proof writes the joint density "with respect to $\nu^{\otimes n}$" and integrates "against $\Pi$." I know what an integral is from calculus, but "product measure $\nu^{\otimes n}$," "dominating measure," and "integrating against a distribution $\Pi$" (as opposed to a density) are not explained. Line 73 jumps straight to this measure-theoretic language. I could follow the commutativity-of-multiplication idea, but the scaffolding around it was opaque.

- **@thm-de-finetti (line 90)**: This is the single hardest moment in the chapter for me. The theorem statement is dense: "unique probability measure $\Pi$ on $[0,1]$" with an integral over $\theta^s(1-\theta)^{n-s}$. I understand the words individually but the *meaning* (why is this saying "exchangeable implies conditionally iid given a latent parameter"?) took me a very long time to extract. The proof (line 103) then says the converse is proved by showing empirical frequencies "form a bounded martingale and converge almost surely." Martingales have not been introduced or cross-referenced. I have no idea what a martingale is. This is a huge gap.

- **@thm-bayes proof (lines 227-253)**: The proof is written in full measure-theoretic generality with "Radon-Nikodym theorem," "Tonelli's theorem," "Fubini's theorem," "disintegration property," "$\mu \otimes \nu$," "$\nu \otimes \mu$-integrable." I know Bayes' theorem as $P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B)$ from a basic stats course. The leap from that to "verify the defining disintegration property" with a double integral over bounded measurable $h$ and a set $B$ is enormous. The chapter said in the preamble (line 209) that the content is "interpretive and computational, not analytical," but then the proof is exactly the analytical part. This mismatch confused me about what I am supposed to take away.

- **@thm-james-stein proof (lines 542-576)**: The line "apply Stein's lemma in coordinate $i$, conditioning on the other coordinates and treating $Y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_i, \sigma^2)$, with $h(\cdot) = g_i(\mathbf Y)$ as a function of $Y_i$" (line 551) is a step I cannot follow. Stein's lemma was stated for a one-dimensional $Z$ and a function $h: \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$. Here $g_i(\mathbf Y)$ depends on all of $\mathbf Y$, so in what sense is it "a function of $Y_i$"? The conditioning trick is not spelled out. Then the divergence $\nabla\cdot\mathbf g$ computation (lines 557-563) goes by very fast; I could not reproduce the $\sum_i y_i^2 = \|\mathbf y\|^2$ substitution that collapses the expression to $-(J-2)^2\sigma^2/\|\mathbf y\|^2$. The noncentral chi-square density argument at the end (line 572-574) for finiteness of $\mathbb{E}[1/\|\mathbf Y\|^2]$ is stated but not derived; "behaves like $r^{J/2-1}$ near $0$" is asserted without showing where it comes from.

- **@def-reml (line 609) and error contrasts**: "Error contrast" is defined as $\mathbf a^\top \mathbf y$ with $\mathbf X^\top \mathbf a = \mathbf 0$, i.e. $\mathbf a \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp$. I had to sit with this. The *idea* (project out the mean, look only at residuals) is intuitive, but the formulation in terms of the orthogonal complement of the column space is notation-heavy and the connection to "this is why the denominator becomes $n-1$" only becomes concrete at @thm-reml-one-sample, much later. A sentence bridging the abstract definition to "this is the residual space" would have helped me enormously.

- **@prp-hmc-invariance proof (lines 1053-1069)**: "The leapfrog proposal is a deterministic, volume-preserving involution after the momentum flip; for such a proposal the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance ratio reduces to..." Why does volume-preservation plus involution reduce the acceptance ratio to a pure density ratio? I follow the algebra once stated, but the *logic* (that the proposal Jacobian is 1 and forward/reverse proposal densities are equal) is asserted, not derived from @thm-mh-stationarity. The link between the general MH acceptance probability and this special form is the one place I most wanted a worked bridge.

## What wasn't elaborated enough

- **The de Finetti converse (line 107-112)**: The chapter explicitly cites rather than proves the substantive half, which is reasonable, but the sketch ("empirical frequencies form a bounded martingale and converge almost surely to a limit $\Theta$") is too thin to give me any intuition. A few sentences on *why* the empirical frequency is the right object, and a picture of "the latent parameter is the long-run frequency," would make the theorem feel less like a black box. Right now I am told it is a representation theorem and asked to take it on faith.

- **Random effects vs fixed effects (@def-random-effects, line 172)**: The distinction is defined formally but the *practical* health-researcher meaning is underdeveloped. In my world "fixed effect" and "random effect" are the two meta-analysis models I pick between in RevMan or R. The chapter says fixed effects = "unrelated unknown constants, each estimated only from its own unit" and random effects = "exchangeable sample from a common population distribution." That is correct but I wanted a concrete pair of scenarios: "if you believe all trials estimate the same underlying drug effect, use fixed; if you believe each trial has its own true effect drawn from a population of trial effects, use random." Lines 185-202 partly do this via the $\tau^2\to\infty$ and $\tau^2\to 0$ limits, which I liked, but the motivating example is buried in formal limits.

- **Empirical Bayes vs full Bayes (line 741-752)**: The remark says EB "ignores the uncertainty in $(\hat\mu, \hat\tau^2)$, so the empirical-Bayes posterior variance understates the true posterior variance." This is a critical practical point but it is stated in one sentence. How badly? When does it matter (small $J$)? Is there a correction? As a practitioner I want to know when EB will mislead me. The funnel / non-centered parameterization aside (line 1082-1086) is similarly underdeveloped: I am told the funnel "defeats a single step size" but not what a funnel *looks* like or why it causes divergences.

- **The DerSimonian-Laird estimator (@eq-dersimonian-laird, line 733)**: The formula appears with $w_j = 1/s_j^2$, $Q$, and the truncation at 0, but *why* this particular form is the method-of-moments estimator is not shown. Where does $Q - (J-1)$ come from? What is $Q$ (the Cochran heterogeneity statistic)? For a health researcher this is the single most used estimator in the chapter and it gets one paragraph with no derivation. At minimum a line saying "$Q$ has expectation $J-1$ under the null of no heterogeneity, so $Q-(J-1)$ estimates the excess heterogeneity scaled by..." would make it click.

- **NUTS (line 1072-1086)**: NUTS is described in one dense remark. "Building the leapfrog trajectory forward and backward in time, doubling its length until it begins to double back on itself" is hand-wavy. Since NUTS is *the* sampler used in Stan and therefore in essentially every Bayesian ITC result later, this deserves more. A small diagram or a paragraph on the doubling tree and the U-turn criterion would help.

- **Marginal likelihood sensitivity / Lindley's paradox (line 1131-1140)**: Mentioned in one remark. I have no intuition for why a diffuse prior drives the marginal likelihood to zero. A two-line derivation or even a pointer to an example would make this less of a name-drop.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

- **de Finetti's theorem (@thm-de-finetti)**: The chapter says (line 84-88) that de Finetti shows the hierarchical structure is "not a modeling choice but a representation theorem." That is a strong claim, but *why does that matter to me as a health researcher?* If the representation theorem says exchangeable = conditionally iid given a latent parameter, what do I gain from knowing this is forced rather than chosen? The motivation is missing: it justifies treating study effects as drawn from a population, which is the entire conceptual basis of random-effects meta-analysis. Say that explicitly. Right now the theorem feels like pure math for its own sake.

- **Stein's lemma and James-Stein (@lem-stein, @thm-james-stein)**: The remark at line 578-589 does connect JS to shrinkage and to empirical Bayes, which is good. But *why prove it in a book about ITCs?* The chapter says (line 495) "it is the rigorous justification for pooling when one is reluctant to commit to a prior." That is the right motivation but it is one sentence at the start. A reader like me needs that point hammered: "if you are uncomfortable putting a prior on the between-study variance, James-Stein tells you that even with *no* prior, shrinking toward a common mean beats treating studies as unrelated, provided you have three or more. This is the frequentist license to pool." The $\tau^2\to\infty$ / no-pooling discussion earlier would be strengthened by a forward reference to JS.

- **REML (@sec-reml-eb)**: Why is REML the default in meta-analysis software? The chapter says so at line 700-702 but the *why* is only shown via the one-sample bias example (line 601-607). For the hierarchical meta-analysis model specifically, I never saw the REML objective applied to my actual problem. The general restricted log-likelihood (line 694-699) is given but never instantiated on the normal-normal model of @eq-normal-normal-model. The bridge from "REML fixes a bias in variance estimation" to "REML is what you should use to estimate $\tau^2$ in your meta-analysis" is implied but not made explicit.

- **MCMC / HMC in the ITC context**: The chapter says HMC "is the sampler inside Stan and the engine of the ML-NMR software of Part V" (line 971-972) and that the posterior is non-conjugate so "estimation proceeds by the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo of @sec-hmc" (line 1242-1244). Good. But during the long MCMC and HMC developments I had no ITC touchstone. A single running example threaded through the theory, e.g. "here is a 3-study log-odds-ratio network, here is why the posterior is not conjugate, here is what HMC does to it," would keep me oriented.

- **The posterior predictive distribution (@sec-predictive)**: The remark (line 782-790) mentions "In population-adjustment methods (Part V) the target-population marginal effect is itself a posterior predictive quantity." That is exactly the ITC hook I wanted, but it is one sentence. Expand it: why is a target-population effect a *predictive* quantity? Because we are predicting what a new study in a new population would show. That connection is the whole reason this section exists for my purposes, and it is almost invisible.

## Pacing issues

- The chapter is extremely front-loaded with abstraction. Sections @sec-exchangeability and @sec-bayes together (lines 38-264) contain de Finetti's theorem, the measure-theoretic Bayes proof, and the Radon-Nikodym/Tonelli/Fubini apparatus, all before a single concrete numerical example. This is the hardest ~220 lines of the chapter and it is where a beginner is most likely to quit.
- The worked examples (@sec-hb-worked, line 1143) come *after* MCMC, HMC, and Bayes factors. This is backwards. I needed the three-study example alongside @thm-shrinkage-posterior-mean, not 700 lines later. The bridge section (@sec-hb-bridge, line 1228) then comes after the examples, which is fine, but the examples themselves should be interleaved with the theory.
- @sec-hmc (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) is the densest single section for a beginner. It introduces Hamiltonians, momentum, the leapfrog integrator, volume preservation, reversibility, and the Metropolis correction in rapid succession (lines 966-1087). There is no breathing room and no toy example. I would split this or at least insert a one-dimensional numeric trace.
- The conjugacy section (@sec-conjugacy, line 266) is comparatively well paced, but the jump from univariate normal-normal to the multivariate corollary (@cor-conjugate-normal-mv, line 329) is abrupt. A sentence on "why we need the multivariate version" (it underlies the network of contrasts in Part III) would orient me.
- Exercises (lines 1292-1383) are good but some are very hard relative to the chapter's stated beginner audience. @exr-james-stein-sure (line 1357) asks me to prove Stein's unbiased risk estimate, which is arguably harder than the proof already in the chapter. The starred exercises are fine but I would have liked more medium-difficulty practice on the core shrinkage and conjugacy mechanics.

## What worked well

- The opening paragraph (lines 5-16) is excellent motivation: no-pooling vs complete-pooling vs partial-pooling, stated in plain English with an ITC framing. This is the clearest passage in the chapter and should be a model for the rest.
- @thm-shrinkage-posterior-mean (line 437) and its proof: once I got past the conjugacy machinery, the shrinkage formula and the three-regimes remark (line 481-490) clicked. The "$B_j$ increases in $s_j^2$" intuition (line 487) is exactly the kind of practitioner-facing insight I want everywhere.
- The three-regimes summary equation (lines 195-199) tying no/complete/partial pooling to $\tau^2 = \infty/0/\text{middle}$ is a beautiful organizing device.
- @exm-three-study-shrinkage (line 1149) is a genuinely good worked example once I reached it: shrinkage with given hyperparameters, a conjugacy cross-check, empirical Bayes via DerSimonian-Laird, and one MH step, all on the same numbers. If examples like this were threaded through the chapter it would be transformed.
- The bridge to NMA (@sec-hb-bridge, line 1228) is the strongest ITC connection in the chapter. It explicitly maps $\theta_j \to \delta_{j,1k}$, $\mu \to d_{1k}$, and names the consistency equations and the non-collapsibility of the logit. This is the payoff paragraph and it is well done.
- The notes-and-references section (line 1249) is thorough about catalogue identifiers (L.1-L.5) and honestly flags that several attributions are not yet in the bibliography. The transparency about Coq scope (line 1284-1290) is also welcome.

## Suggestions

1. **Interleave the worked example with the theory.** Pull @exm-three-study-shrinkage apart and place a shrinkage calculation right after @thm-shrinkage-posterior-mean, a conjugacy check right after @thm-conjugate-normal, an EB calculation right after @eq-dersimonian-laird, and an MH step right after @thm-mh-stationarity. Keep @sec-hb-worked as a consolidated "putting it together" section but do not make it the first place numbers appear.
2. **Add an intuition paragraph before @thm-de-finetti** stating, in plain English: "if you cannot distinguish the studies before seeing data, then there must be a latent common parameter they all depend on; the rest is bookkeeping." Then state the theorem. Then give the binary example with $n=3$ and a concrete mixing distribution (e.g. point mass at 0.3 and 0.7) so the integral is computable by hand.
3. **Define or defer the measure theory.** Either add a short "Measure-theoretic primer" callout box defining dominating measure, Radon-Nikodym, Tonelli/Fubini, disintegration, and martingale, or explicitly defer the @thm-bayes proof to an appendix and present the density-ratio form as the working result. As written, the proof is inaccessible to the stated audience and the remark (line 255-264) already says the interpretive content is what matters, so the heavy proof undercuts the stated goal.
4. **Add ITC touchstones throughout**, not only at the bridge. After exchangeability: "the trials in an indirect comparison of A-vs-B are exchangeable if we have no prior reason to favor one trial's effect over another." After conjugacy: "this is the update a Bayesian NMA uses for each study-level contrast." After REML: "this is how `metafor` and Stan estimate $\tau^2$ by default." After HMC: "this is what runs when you call `stan_glmer` on a network model."
5. **Expand the DerSimonian-Laird derivation.** Show why $Q-(J-1)$ and the specific denominator arise from the method of moments. This estimator is arguably the single most important formula for a health researcher doing meta-analysis and it deserves a derivation, not just a display.
6. **Add figures**: a shrinkage plot ($\hat\theta_j$ vs $y_j$ with the $y=x$ line and the $\mu$ line), a funnel-geometry sketch for the centered vs non-centered parameterization, an HMC leapfrog trajectory in 1D or 2D, and a Markov chain trace. The chapter is 1383 lines of pure math with zero figures; for a beginner audience this is a major gap.
7. **Unify the parameter notation.** Reconcile $\boldsymbol\phi$ (hierarchical) and $\boldsymbol\xi$ (Bayesian) explicitly, or use one symbol consistently. Add $\stackrel{d}{=}$, "kernel," $\delta_x$, $\boldsymbol\vartheta$, and "noncentral chi-square" to notation.qmd or define them inline on first use.
8. **Soften the James-Stein section.** The result is important but the proof is among the hardest in the chapter. Consider presenting the risk identity as the main theorem and relegating the full Stein-lemma derivation to an exercise or appendix, with the intuition (estimating the shrinkage factor from the ensemble is exactly borrowing strength) foregrounded in prose.
9. **Add a posterior-predictive ITC example.** Show, even schematically, how a target-population marginal effect is a posterior predictive draw. One sentence in a remark (line 788) is not enough for the load this concept bears in Part V.
