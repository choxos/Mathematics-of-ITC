# Feedback: Chapter 18 — MAIC

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part4/18-maic.qmd`

## Overall impression

I can tell this is a serious, carefully written chapter and that a lot of work went into it. But honestly, as a health researcher with basic calculus and a weak math foundation, I was lost for large stretches. The first paragraph jumps straight into "the marginal $B$-versus-$C$ effect in the $BC$ trial" and references five theorems and four definitions across earlier chapters before I even understand what MAIC *does* mechanically. The worked example at the end (Section [A fully worked two-covariate example](#sec-maic-example)) was the only thing that let me breathe; everything before it felt like it was written for someone who already knew MAIC. I kept wanting a concrete trial: which drug is $A$, which is $B$, what is $C$, what covariate are we talking about? The chapter almost never says. The math is clearly correct and thorough, but the *teaching* scaffolding for a naive reader is thin until the very end.

I had to go back to Chapter 14/16 mentally. A one-sentence plain reminder ("an effect modifier is a patient characteristic that changes how much a treatment helps, e.g. disease severity") would anchor me.
- **Introduction, line 20-31:** "One trial, which we call $AC$, compared an active treatment $A$ against a common comparator $C$." Why are trials named by the pair of arms? I found "$AC$" and "$BC$" as trial names confusing because later "$A$-versus-$C$" also uses the same letters. A concrete example (e.g. "$AC$ trial = drug A vs placebo C") would have disambiguated instantly.
- **Section [The two-trial setup](#sec-maic-setup), lines 92-94:** "Randomization within the index trial means $T\perp(Y(A),Y(C))\mid\mathbf{X}$ holds with the conditioning vacuous." I have no idea what "the conditioning vacuous" means as a phrase. This is dense jargon dropped without unpacking.
- **[Method-of-moments covariate balance](#sec-maic-moment), lines 129-134:** The feature map $\boldsymbol{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}^q$ and "taking $\phi$-components equal to the squared and cross-product covariates $x_jx_k$ balances the second moments, hence the covariance matrix; taking spline or indicator bases balances the corresponding functionals." I got lost here. What is a "feature map" intuitively? Why does squaring covariates balance the covariance? A two-line numeric illustration would help enormously.
- **[The entropy-balancing objective](#sec-maic-entropy), lines 197-200:** "$\sum_i p_i\log p_i=\tfrac1n\sum_i w_i\log w_i-\log n=\tfrac1n H(\mathbf{w})-\log n$". This algebra is fine but I had to read it three times. Why is $p_i=w_i/n$? Because of the normalization $\sum_i w_i=n$. That link is stated but I lost the thread because the $+\log n$ and $-\log n$ cancellations were not spelled out step by step.
- **Theorem [Exponential form of the entropy-balancing weights](#thm-maic-exponential-weights), lines 250-262:** The jump from the Lagrangian stationarity to "$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is any minimizer of the convex log-partition function $Q$" felt abrupt. I had the Lagrangian, I had the stationarity condition, and suddenly we are minimizing a different function $Q$. A bridging sentence ("instead of solving the primal over $\mathbf{w}$, we solve the dual over $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, which is equivalent by duality") would help; the dual reduction paragraph at lines 311-336 does this but only after the fact.
- **Proof of [thm-maic-exponential-weights](#thm-maic-exponential-weights), lines 322-329:** The Hessian computation $\nabla^2 Q=\operatorname{Cov}_{p(\boldsymbol{\beta})}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})$ is shown but I had trouble following the index gymnastics in lines 330-333. "since $\partial_\ell p_i=p_i(\Delta_{i\ell}-\sum_j p_j\Delta_{j\ell})$" — where does this come from? It is the derivative of a softmax; a parenthetical "(this is the softmax derivative)" would orient me.
- **[Existence and uniqueness via convex duality](#sec-maic-existence), lines 374-378:** "relative interior" and "affine hull" are defined in one sentence each. I still do not have an intuitive picture. A tiny 2D sketch described in words (a triangle: interior points feasible with positive weights, edge points need zero weights, outside points infeasible) would make this section click. The remark at lines 474-486 does this partially but only after the heavy proof.
- **Proof of [thm-maic-existence-uniqueness](#thm-maic-existence-uniqueness), Part 2 sufficiency, lines 429-459:** This is the hardest proof in the chapter for me. The coercivity argument with "$\varepsilon\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{S}$, so $\varepsilon\mathbf{u}=\sum_i q_i\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i$" and then "for every unit $\mathbf{u}\in V$ there is an index $i$ with $\mathbf{u}^\top\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i\ge\varepsilon$" is a clever argument but I could not follow why the uniform-in-$\mathbf{u}$ bound follows. Why does the same $\varepsilon$ work for every direction? I think it is because the relative ball of radius $\varepsilon$ is contained in $\mathcal{S}$, but the proof does not say that explicitly.
- **[MAIC weights are logistic inverse-probability weights](#sec-maic-ipw), lines 528-536:** The Bayes derivation of the density ratio is compact. "Writing $P(\mathbf{x}\mid S=s)=P(S=s\mid\mathbf{x})\,P(\mathbf{x})/P(S=s)$" — this is Bayes applied to densities, but the notation $P(\mathbf{x})$ for a marginal density of a continuous vector confused me vs the probability measure notation elsewhere. A clearer density-vs-probability distinction would help.
- **Theorem [Consistency of anchored MAIC](#thm-anchored-maic-consistency), assumption (ii), lines 601-605:** "the effect modifiers and their interaction coefficients are shared across $A$ and $B$ relative to $C$, so that $\hat d_{BC}^{\mathrm{AgD}}$ is consistent for the $\mathcal{P}_{BC}$ marginal contrast." I do not understand what "interaction coefficients are shared across $A$ and $B$ relative to $C$" means operationally. Is this the SEMA assumption? - **Theorem [Consistency of unanchored MAIC](#thm-unanchored-maic-consistency), assumption (i), lines 716-718:** "the conditional mean function $\mu_t(\mathbf{x})$ is the same in its source population and in $\mathcal{P}^{*}$." Why is this "essentially untestable" (line 796)? The remark says so but does not explain *why* two trials cannot test it. I wanted a sentence: "because we only see $\mu_t$ in the source trial, never in the target, so we cannot compare."
- **[Standard errors: the sandwich](#sec-maic-sandwich), lines 992-1004:** The stacked estimating function with three blocks is introduced very compactly. I did not immediately see why the balance block uses $e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i$ (no $\alpha$). A sentence ("$\alpha$ drops out of the balance equation because it multiplies $\sum_i\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i$ which is fixed by the target, so only $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a free parameter here") would clarify.

I gathered it means "smooth enough for the functional delta method" but a one-line definition or a pointer to Chapter 5 would help. The Fréchet vs Hadamard distinction (line 1111) is also undefined.

## What wasn't elaborated enough

- **Why entropy / KL specifically (lines 203-212):** The remark lists four reasons entropy is canonical, but each reason is a forward reference to a theorem. As a beginner I wanted: "entropy penalizes large weights superlinearly, so a single patient cannot dominate; squared error penalizes linearly and lets one patient dominate." A visual or numeric contrast of $w\log w$ vs $(w-1)^2$ would make the choice vivid.
- **The link-scale dependence of which moments to balance (lines 142-155):** This is a crucial practical point for health researchers and it is buried in a remark. The statement "On a nonlinear link such as the logit or the log hazard, the conditional contrast involves $g^{-1}(\eta(\mathbf{x}))$, whose population average depends on higher moments" is the heart of the matter but is one sentence. I needed a worked mini-example: "with a logit link and a binary covariate, matching only the mean can leave the marginal log-odds biased by X" with numbers.
- **Non-collapsibility interaction (lines 674-680):** "For non-collapsible measures ... the subtraction in Step 4 is licensed only under the scale-alignment conditions catalogued by the general anchored PAIC theorem, which we invoke." This is a huge practical issue (log odds ratio, log hazard ratio are the most common scales in HTA!) and it is handled by saying "we invoke Chapter 17." For a chapter on MAIC, used mostly on log-HR and log-OR scales, this is too light. At least a paragraph on *what* the scale-alignment condition requires concretely would help.
- **The unanchored case's strength of assumptions (lines 795-803):** The remark says unanchored is "a last resort" but does not quantify or give an example of how it fails. A concrete scenario ("comparing a cancer drug A vs B with no common comparator; if age is prognostic and unbalanced and unmeasured, the absolute mean is biased by Z") would land the warning.
- **Effective sample size interpretation (lines 906-911):** "$\mathrm{ESS}=n/2$ doubles the variance, $\mathrm{ESS}=n/10$ tenfolds it, equivalent to discarding ninety percent of the data." This is the most useful sentence in the section. I wanted more like it: what does ESS=30 out of n=400 feel like in a real HTA submission? What confidence interval widening does that imply?
- **The logistic-IPW equivalence, "method of moments vs maximum likelihood" (lines 560-572):** This distinction is the key conceptual bridge between MAIC and standard causal inference, and it gets one remark. I would have liked a small diagram or table: "Standard IPW: fit logistic by ML on labeled data. MAIC: fit logistic by moment-matching on unlabeled comparator. Same target density ratio, different fitting criterion."

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

- **Why reweight at all?** The introduction (lines 27-31) says "we can move our analysis into the $BC$ population: reweight the $AC$ IPD so that its weighted covariate moments equal those reported by $BC$." But I never got a plain-language *why* until the worked example (lines 1243-1265). The motivation should appear earlier and concretely: "If drug A works better in sicker patients and the BC trial had sicker patients, the naive comparison is unfair to A; reweighting makes the AC trial's patient mix look like BC's so the comparison is apples to apples."
- **Why entropy, why not just any weights?** Lines 33-36 say "Infinitely many weight vectors achieve covariate balance; MAIC selects the one that distorts the sample as little as possible." Good. But *why* is "least distortion" the right principle? The health-researcher answer ("we want to stay as close to the original trial as possible so we do not overfit to a few unusual patients; that keeps the standard error honest") is implicit, not stated.
- **Why prove existence/uniqueness so carefully (Section [Existence and uniqueness](#sec-maic-existence))?** As a practitioner I would just run the optimizer and see if it converges. The motivation ("so we know in advance whether the method is even defined, and we can diagnose overlap failure *before* fitting, from the covariate ranges") is only stated at the end (lines 474-486). Stating it up front would give me a reason to read the hard proof.
- **Why the sandwich and not the naive variance?** The motivation is in the remark at lines 1061-1072 but only after the theorem. I would have liked the motivating sentence first: "if you refit the weights on the same data you use to estimate the effect, pretending the weights are fixed understates your uncertainty; the sandwich fixes that."
- **Why does the no-extrapolation result matter for HTA?** Lines 916-921 state the theorem but the *practical* punchline ("if the comparator trial included older patients than any in your IPD, MAIC literally cannot tell you what A does in those older patients; you need a model, which is STC/ML-NMR") is only in the closing remark (lines 970-980). Lead with it.
- **Why is the anchored vs unanchored distinction so important?** Lines 60-68 sketch it but the *consequence* (unanchored needs you to balance prognostic variables too, which is usually impossible, so it is rarely defensible) is the single most important practical takeaway for a health researcher, and it is scattered across several remarks rather than foregrounded.

## Missing examples and intuition

- **No running concrete drug/trial scenario.** The whole chapter uses abstract $A,B,C$ and $\mathcal{P}_{AC},\mathcal{P}_{BC}$. Even one persistent example threaded through ("Drug A = new oncology immunotherapy; B = older checkpoint inhibitor; C = chemotherapy; covariates = age, ECOG score, prior therapy") would make every theorem tangible. The worked example (Section [A fully worked two-covariate example](#sec-maic-example)) uses generic "severity score" with $x_1, x_2$; it is good but disconnected from any real trial framing.
- **No picture of the convex hull / overlap idea.** The geometry of feasibility (Section [Existence and uniqueness](#sec-maic-existence)) cries out for a figure: index covariates as dots, the convex hull shaded, the target mean as a star inside/on/outside. This is the single biggest pedagogical gap for a visual learner like me.
- **No numeric illustration of the exponential form before deriving it.** Before Theorem [thm-maic-exponential-weights](#thm-maic-exponential-weights), a tiny 3-patient, 1-covariate numeric example of "here are three patients, here is the target mean, here are the weights that balance it, notice they are $\exp(\alpha+\beta x)$" would have made the theorem feel like it was explaining something I had already touched.
- **No illustration of ESS collapse.** A small table: "uniform weights ESS=n; weights $(3,1,0)$ ESS=...; weights $(10,1,1,1,...)$ ESS=..." would make the ESS bound and the variance inflation visceral before the abstract proofs.
- **No example of the no-extrapolation failure.** The non-identification theorem (Section [MAIC cannot extrapolate](#sec-maic-noextrap)) is abstract. A one-sentence concrete instance: "suppose the BC trial included patients with age 75-85, but your AC trial only enrolled up to age 70; MAIC cannot say anything about A's effect at age 80, no matter how clever the weights" would make the theorem stick.
- **The logistic-IPW equivalence (Section [MAIC weights are logistic inverse-probability weights](#sec-maic-ipw)) has no numeric bridge.** A worked 4-patient example showing the MAIC weights numerically equal to inverse-odds weights from a fitted logistic model would make [thm-maic-logistic-ipw-equiv](#thm-maic-logistic-ipw-equiv) feel real instead of symbolic.

## Pacing issues

- **The introduction is a wall.** Lines 5-83 are four paragraphs of dense summary referencing ~15 cross-references before any concrete construction. For a naive reader this is exhausting. Splitting into "the problem" (short, concrete) then "the method" (short, concrete) then "the map of the chapter" would be gentler.
- **Section [The entropy-balancing objective](#sec-maic-entropy) to [The exponential weights via Lagrangian and KKT](#sec-maic-weights):** three sections of optimization theory (entropy, KKT, duality) with no numeric or visual rest. A beginner is swimming in symbols for ~150 lines. Inserting a small worked example after the exponential form is derived (before the existence/uniqueness deep dive) would give a breathing point.
- **Section [Existence and uniqueness via convex duality](#sec-maic-existence):** the proof (lines 406-471) is the densest in the chapter and comes before any of the applied sections (consistency, ESS, variance). Reading order felt backwards: I would rather see *that* MAIC works (consistency, the example) and *what* it costs (ESS, variance) before the hardest pure-existence argument. The existence theorem could be stated and its proof deferred to an appendix or later in the chapter.
- **Two consistency theorems back to back (Sections [Consistency of anchored MAIC](#sec-maic-anchored) and [Consistency of unanchored MAIC](#sec-maic-unanchored))** without a numeric interlude between them. The unanchored proof reuses "the affine-mean argument of Step 2" (line 757) which I had to flip back for. A short bridging example contrasting the covariate sets needed would pace it better.
- **The worked example comes last (Section [A fully worked two-covariate example](#sec-maic-example)).** For a beginner this is the single most illuminating section, and it sits at line 1142 of 1401. Moving a *mini* version of it to right after the setup (Section [The two-trial setup](#sec-maic-setup)) and keeping the full version at the end would dramatically improve the learning curve.
- **Exercises ([Exercises](#sec-maic-exercises)) jump in difficulty.** Exercises 1-3 are doable; Exercise 4 (majorization, Schur-convexity) and 5-6 (starred, general derivations and sandwich algebra) require math far above the stated prerequisite. There is no middle band.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a running concrete trial scenario** (e.g. an oncology drug comparison with named covariates: age, ECOG, prior-therapy) and reference it at every major theorem, so each abstract result has a concrete anchor.
2. **Move a 5-line numeric mini-example to right after [The two-trial setup](#sec-maic-setup):** three patients, one covariate, target mean, the balance equation, the exponential weights. Give the reader something to hold before the entropy derivation.
3. **Add a figure for the convex-hull / overlap geometry** in [Existence and uniqueness](#sec-maic-existence): index covariates as points, hull shaded, target inside / on boundary / outside, with the three cases labeled feasible-positive / feasible-with-zero-weights / infeasible.
4. **Foreground the practical "why" for each major theorem:** one plain-language sentence of motivation before each theorem statement, not only in the remark after.
5. **Define jargon on first use or give a one-line gloss in a parenthetical:** "vacuous conditioning", "coercive", "Hadamard-differentiable", "self-normalized", "balancing score", "g-formula", "majorizes". Otherwise beginners stall.
6. **Expand the link-scale / non-collapsibility discussion (lines 142-155 and 674-680)** with a concrete logit or log-HR mini-example showing *why* first-moment matching is insufficient and *what* scale alignment requires. This is the most practically important gap for HTA users who work almost exclusively on log-OR / log-HR scales.
7. **Consider reordering:** introduce ESS and variance inflation *before* the hard existence/uniqueness proof, so the reader sees the cost of reweighting while motivation is fresh, then tackle the hardest pure-math result with that context.
8. **Add a "practitioner checklist" box** near the end: (a) confirm overlap by plotting covariate ranges; (b) balance every imbalanced effect modifier; (c) prefer anchored whenever a common comparator exists; (d) report ESS and flag ESS/n < 0.5; (e) use sandwich or full-bootstrap, not naive SE; (f) for non-collapsible scales, check the scale-alignment conditions. This would turn the chapter's scattered practical advice into one actionable artifact.
9. **Bridge the Lagrangian-to-dual gap in [thm-maic-exponential-weights](#thm-maic-exponential-weights):** a sentence before Eq. @eq-maic-dual saying "we now show the primal solution is obtained by solving an equivalent dual problem over $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ alone, which is what software actually minimizes" would orient the reader before the dual reduction paragraph.
10. **Soften the introduction:** split the four-paragraph front matter into a short concrete "the problem" and a separate "roadmap," and cut the density of early cross-references in the very first paragraph by deferring some to later.
