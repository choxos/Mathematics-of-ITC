# Feedback: Chapter 01 — Linear Algebra I

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `chapters/part1/01-linear-algebra-i.qmd`

## Overall impression
As a health researcher with shaky math, I survived this chapter but only because I have seen regression and "dummy variable trap" in applied stats courses; the abstract axiomatic development (eight vector-space axioms, Steinitz exchange, rank-nullity proof) felt like being dropped into a pure-math course with no on-ramp. The biggest barrier was the gap between the motivating ITC material in the intro (lines 5 to 38) and the sudden leap into axiom-by-axiom proofs of facts I could not connect to anything clinical. The chapter is clearly rigorous and well-organized, but it is written for someone who already believes that "subspace" and "null space" are worth caring about; I needed many more bridges back to trials, treatments, and estimands than were offered.

## What was unclear
- **@def-vector-space (lines 46 to 68):** the eight axioms are dumped with no warmup. I do not understand *why* these eight and not others, and axiom (VS8) "distributivity over scalar addition" $(a+b)\mathbf{v}=a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{v}$ confused me because I have never thought of "adding two scalars" as something a vector should respond to. A one-line intuition per axiom would have helped.
- **@lem-vector-space-basics proof (lines 114 to 135):** the proof of part (1) chains axiom citations like "using (VS3) for $\mathbf{0}'$ and then (VS1) and (VS3) for $\mathbf{0}$" with no expanded line. I got lost trying to follow which $\mathbf{0}$ is which in $\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}'=\mathbf{0}'$. I need this spelled out term by term.
- **@lem-dependence (lines 245 to 271):** the phrase "there is an index $i\in\{2,\dots,k\}$" and the "largest index with $c_i\neq 0$" argument lost me. Why largest? What if the dependence relation has the nonzero coefficient in the middle? I did not follow why picking the *largest* index is legitimate rather than arbitrary.
- **@thm-steinitz-exchange proof (lines 313 to 347):** this is the hardest proof in the chapter for a beginner and it is presented as a single dense induction with reindexing "so that $j_0=k+1$." I do not understand the reindexing move; can I really relabel the $\mathbf{w}$'s mid-proof without breaking the spanning hypothesis? A worked micro-example (say $m=2$, $n=3$) before or alongside the proof would have made the replacement mechanism visible.
- **@thm-row-rank-column-rank proof (lines 706 to 726):** the rank factorization $A=CF$ is introduced inside the proof with no prior definition of "rank factorization." When the text later refers to "@eq-rank-factorization" I had to scroll back to discover $F$ was defined only here. I also did not follow why "$F^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$ has only $r$ columns, so $\dim\mathcal{C}(F^\top)\le r$" is enough; a beginner does not immediately see that "number of columns" bounds "dimension of column space."
- **@thm-four-subspaces proof, orthogonality (lines 763 to 777):** the step "orthogonality is preserved under linear combinations: if $\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$ for all $i$ then $\mathbf{x}^\top\sum_i c_i\mathbf{a}^{(i)}=\sum_i c_i\,\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{a}^{(i)}=0$" is the load-bearing move and is given in a parenthetical. I needed this as a short lemma with its own statement, not a parenthetical aside.
- **@thm-invertibility-characterization proof (lines 953 to 1000):** the "closing the loop" paragraph (lines 991 to 994) where the text says "these implications close the loop" by tracing cycles is hard to follow without a diagram of the implication graph. A small diagram or a table of which arrow proves which implication would clarify the structure.

## What wasn't elaborated enough
- **The "dummy variable trap" example (lines 18 to 25):** this is the single most ITC-relevant motivating example and it is described in two sentences of prose with no matrix shown. I wanted the actual $2\times 2$ vs $2\times 3$ design matrices drawn here, at the very start, so I could see the rank drop concretely before the abstract vocabulary begins.
- **@lem-subspace-criterion (lines 155 to 172):** the equivalence of "is a subspace" with "closed under linear combinations" is stated and proved, but the *practical* reason it matters (it lets us check three conditions instead of eight) is never said. As a beginner I do not know why this lemma is worth proving; tell me it is a labor-saving device.
- **@lem-basis-extension (lines 387 to 409):** the "deletion scan" is used in two proofs but the scan procedure itself is never illustrated on a small matrix. I had to construct my own mental picture of "scan left to right, delete if in span of survivors."
- **@def-rank (lines 591 to 600):** rank is defined as $\dim\mathcal{C}(A)$, but for a health researcher "dimension of the column space" is not intuitive. I need a sentence like "rank = the number of genuinely independent pieces of information the columns carry" or "how many distinct effects the design can resolve."
- **The remark at lines 780 to 792:** the direct-sum decomposition $\mathbb{R}^n=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\oplus\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})$ is name-checked and deferred to Chapter 2, but I have no picture of what a "direct sum" is. Even a one-line "every vector splits uniquely into a part the model can see and a part it cannot" would help.
- **@prp-system-solvability remark on identifiability (lines 839 to 847):** this is the most important bridge to ITC in the whole chapter and it is buried in a remark. The phrase "the treatment effect is identifiable precisely when the relevant contrast is constant on the coset $\boldsymbol\beta+\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})$" is the crux, but "coset" is undefined jargon and the idea that "two parameters differing by a null-space vector give identical predictions" deserves its own subsection with a figure, not a remark.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"
- **@sec-la1-vector-spaces (lines 40 to 44):** the section opens with "A vector space is an abstraction..." but never says *why a health researcher needs the abstraction*. After the strong ITC intro, the abstraction feels like a detour. I need a sentence: "We abstract because every model in this book, regression or NMA or ML-NMR, is a statement about a vector space, and the theorems we prove once apply to all of them."
- **@thm-steinitz-exchange (lines 307 to 311):** the theorem is called "the engine" of dimension well-definedness, but I do not understand *why* "any two bases have the same size" is something I should care about. What breaks in the statistics if dimension were ill-defined? A sentence tying this to "the number of estimable effects is well-defined" would motivate it.
- **@thm-rank-nullity (lines 602 to 613):** called "the conservation law that governs all of the above," but the *why* for ITC is missing. I want: "this is why a trial with $p$ patients and $k$ covariates has $p-k$ residual degrees of freedom, and why adding a collinear covariate steals a degree of freedom from the effect you actually want."
- **@thm-row-rank-column-rank (lines 700 to 704):** why does it matter that row rank equals column rank? For a rectangular design matrix this is genuinely surprising; the chapter never says why a health researcher should find it useful (e.g., "you can compute rank from either the patient-side or the covariate-side and get the same answer").
- **@thm-invertibility-characterization (lines 934 to 951):** the theorem is said to be "used constantly" to certify $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is invertible, but the link from "invertible $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$" to "I can uniquely estimate the treatment effect $\beta_1$" is asserted only at the very end (line 1091) and never explained in the theorem's own motivation.
- **The whole chapter** never says, in plain language, *why we prove things at all* rather than just cite them. The preface promise (Part I written from scratch) is honored, but a beginner needs a one-paragraph "we prove these because every identifiability claim later is a corollary; if you trust the proofs here, you trust the whole book" statement near the top.

## Missing examples and intuition
- There is exactly one fully worked numeric example, @exm-design-matrix (lines 1030 to 1092), and it appears at the very end. The entire abstract development (vector spaces, span, independence, basis, Steinitz, rank-nullity, four subspaces, row-echelon, invertibility) proceeds with *zero* concrete matrices. As a weak-math reader I needed small $2\times 2$ or $3\times 3$ numerical companions *throughout*, not just at the close.
- **@exm-coordinate-space (lines 73 to 85)** is the only example in the first 600 lines and it is purely definitional ("$\mathbb{R}^n$ is a vector space"). There is no example showing linear dependence on concrete vectors, no example computing a span of two specific vectors, no example of a basis that is *not* the standard basis.
- **@exm-other-spaces (lines 87 to 98)** mentions polynomials and functions as vector spaces but never returns to them; I was left wondering why they were introduced. Either drop them or use them later (e.g., the function-space view is exactly what ML-NMR needs for integration; say so).
- No geometric picture is ever offered. A single figure of two vectors, their span as a plane, and a third dependent vector lying in that plane would carry more intuition than several paragraphs of axiom manipulation. The chapter is figure-free.
- The "dummy variable trap" is mentioned three times (intro, remark at lines 1094 to 1110, exercise at lines 1172 to 1180) but never *worked numerically* in the body. The remark computes $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}')$ symbolically but never shows the resulting non-unique $\boldsymbol\beta$ values (e.g., "$(2,1,0)$ and $(1,0,-1)$ give the same predictions"). A concrete pair of distinct coefficients producing identical fitted values would make non-identifiability viscerally clear.

## Notation and jargon problems
- "Morphism" (line 421): undefined. I had to look it up; for a health researcher this is pure jargon. Say "structure-preserving map" or drop it.
- "Affine subspace" (line 806) and "coset" (line 844): both used without definition. These are not in `notation.qmd` either. A beginner does not know what "affine" means versus "linear."
- "Internal direct sum" and $\oplus$ (line 786): introduced in a remark with no definition; the symbol $\oplus$ is not in the notation table.
- $\mathbf{e}_i$ is used at line 296 and listed in `notation.qmd`, good, but the notation file lists $\mathbf{1}_n$ and $\mathbf{0}_n$ which the chapter uses at line 1019 without introducing them in-text; a reader who skips the notation file is stranded.
- "Nonsingular" (line 929) is introduced as a synonym for invertible but never used again; either commit to it or drop it so beginners are not confused about whether "nonsingular" and "invertible" might differ.
- "Pivot," "row-echelon form," "reduced row-echelon form" (lines 888 to 893): the algorithm is deferred to @strang2016, but these terms are then used in @prp-rank-equals-pivots as if defined. A two-sentence definition in-line would avoid forcing the reader out of the book.
- The notation $\mathcal{C}(A)$ for column space and $\mathcal{N}(A)$ for null space appears at lines 586 to 587 before @def-column-space (line 678) formally defines them; there is a forward-reference problem. The rank-nullity section (lines 584 to 648) leans on symbols that are only defined two sections later.
- "Star" ($\star$) on exercises (lines 1219, 1229) is unexplained; I do not know if it means hard, optional, or "solution in appendix."

## Pacing issues
- The jump from the ITC-rich intro (lines 5 to 38) straight into eight vector-space axioms (lines 46 to 68) is abrupt. There is no gentle transition through "here is a concrete vector, here is what adding and scaling mean for a trial dataset, now here is the abstraction." The pacing collapses from applied to axiomatic in one paragraph.
- @sec-la1-basis (lines 224 to 416) is the densest section: linear independence, dependence lemma, basis, unique representation, standard basis, Steinitz exchange, dimension well-defined, dimension definition, reduction-to-basis, basis extension, and a remark, all in roughly 190 lines with no example. This is too much machinery without a numerical breather; I would split it or interleave a small example after the dependence lemma and after Steinitz.
- The four-fundamental-subspaces section (lines 671 to 792) introduces orthogonality, orthogonal complement, the inner product notation, *and* proves both dimension counts and orthogonality in one block. For a beginner this is a wall; the orthogonality definitions should sit in their own short subsection before the theorem.
- The invertibility characterization (lines 920 to 1012) lists ten equivalent conditions and proves them in a single long proof. A beginner cannot hold ten conditions in working memory; grouping them (e.g., "the rank conditions: (4),(5),(6),(7)"; "the inverse conditions: (1),(2),(3),(10)"; "the solution conditions: (8),(9)") with a short roadmap before the proof would help.
- The worked example at the end (@sec-la1-design-example, lines 1014 to 1110) is excellent but arrives too late; by then I had already given up trying to connect the abstraction to trials. Moving a stripped-down version of this example to *right after* the intro, as a preview, would reframe the whole chapter.

## What worked well
- The intro paragraph (lines 5 to 16) is genuinely the best part: it names the three features (column space, rank, null space) and ties each to a concrete statistical question. I came in excited.
- The rank-nullity proof (@thm-rank-nullity, lines 615 to 648) is, once I understood the setup, the cleanest proof in the chapter: extend a kernel basis, show the images of the complementary basis form an image basis. The structure is visible and I could follow it.
- The worked design-matrix example (@exm-design-matrix, lines 1030 to 1092) is exactly the kind of thing I needed; computing all four subspaces, the normal-equations matrix, and its inverse by hand, with explicit numbers, finally made the abstraction land. If the whole chapter had this density of concrete computation, I would have learned far more.

5. Promote the identifiability remark (lines 839 to 847) to a named subsection "Identifiability as a null-space condition" with a figure showing two distinct $\boldsymbol\beta$ vectors giving the same $\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta$. This is the chapter's payoff for the ITC reader and it is currently a parenthetical.
6. Add at least one figure: two vectors and their span as a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, with a dependent vector lying in the plane. Pure prose cannot carry the geometric intuition beginners need.

9. After each major theorem (Steinitz, rank-nullity, row-equals-column-rank, four-subspaces), add one sentence "Why this matters for the rest of the book:" tying it to an ITC or regression payoff. Currently only rank-nullity gets a partial payoff sentence.
10. Reorder so that @def-column-space (the four subspaces) and the symbols $\mathcal{C}(A), \mathcal{N}(A)$ appear *before* the rank-nullity section that uses them, or remove the forward references at lines 586 to 587.
11. In the dummy-variable-trap remark (lines 1094 to 1110), show two explicit distinct coefficient vectors that produce identical fitted values; this is the most visceral demonstration of non-identifiability and it is currently only symbolic.
