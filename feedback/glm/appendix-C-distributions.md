# Feedback: Appendix C — Distributions

**Reviewer perspective:** Beginner health researcher, basic calculus, weak math foundation.
**File:** `appendices/C-distributions.qmd`

## Overall impression

I came to this appendix because the chapters kept throwing distributions at me (gamma, beta, Weibull, Dirichlet) and I hoped to find one calm place that would tell me, in plain language, what each one is, why it shows up in this book, and which clinical situation it matches. On a first read this appendix feels more like a cheat sheet written *for someone who already knows the material* than a teaching reference. The opening paragraph (lines 5 to 14) promises "a single place to look up a defining formula," and it delivers exactly that and only that: formulas, packed into dense tables, with short prose paragraphs that assume I already understand shapes, conjugacy, hazards, and why a rate parametrization matters. As a beginner I can copy the density of a Gamma out of @tbl-dist-continuous (line 92), but I cannot tell you *what a gamma distribution is for* in my own research life, and several rows give me formulas without the sentence of intuition I need to actually recognize when to use them.

The structure is reasonable (conventions, discrete, continuous, MVN, Dirichlet, standard normal, conjugacy), and the cross references to Chapter 4 and Chapter 8 are consistent. But the pacing is back loaded: the discrete section is almost purely a table plus two short paragraphs, while the continuous section carries all the real commentary. The health/ITC domain connection is almost entirely absent. There is one remark about "covariate-adjusted risks" (line 76) and one about proportional-hazards regression (line 138); otherwise this could be a generic probability appendix pasted into any statistics book. Given that the whole point of *this* book is indirect treatment comparison and population adjustment, that is a missed opportunity.

## What was unclear

- **MGF "determines the law uniquely" and "determines all moments" (lines 20 to 22).** I can parrot this back but I do not actually understand *why* differentiating $M_X(t)$ at zero gives moments, nor why finite in a neighborhood of the origin is the condition. The appendix points me to @thm-mgf-moments and @thm-mgf-uniqueness in Chapter 4, which is fine, but a one line intuition ("differentiate $e^{tX}$ and you pull down a factor of $X$ each time") would have made the whole MGF column of every table feel less magical.
- **"degenerate because the support lies in a hyperplane" (lines 24 to 25, and line 221).** I genuinely do not know what this means. The Dirichlet lives on the simplex, the components sum to one, so... the MGF is degenerate? Why does summing to one make $\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{t}^\top \mathbf{X}}]$ degenerate? This is stated twice and never unpacked. A beginner needs the actual reason: because $X_K=1-\sum_{k<K}X_k$, the MGF is really a function of only $K-1$ free variables, so the "joint" MGF on $\mathbb{R}^K$ does not characterize the law in the usual way.
- **Poisson binomial mass function (line 57).** The formula $\sum_{|S|=k}\prod_{i\in S}p_i\prod_{i\notin S}(1-p_i)$ is correct but I had to stare at it. There is no plain language gloss like "sum over every way of choosing exactly $k$ successes out of $n$ trials with possibly different success probabilities." The table caption (lines 60 to 61) only says $S$ ranges over subsets of size $k$; it does not explain the *idea*.
- **"the right tail being too heavy to integrate against $e^{tx}$ beyond that point" (lines 108 to 109).** This is the kind of sentence that is obvious to the author and opaque to me. Why does the tail being heavy stop the MGF from existing? A parenthetical ("for $t\ge\beta$ the integral $\int e^{tx}f(x)\,dx$ diverges") would have helped.
- **"by convexity of $p\mapsto p^2$" (line 74).** I believe it, but a one clause reminder of which direction convexity goes (equal $p_i$ maximize variance because $p^2$ is convex so $\sum p_i^2$ is *minimized* when equal) would save me from having to rederive it.
- **Weibull MGF existence condition (lines 127 to 130).** "finite on a neighborhood of the origin only when $k\ge 1$" is stated as a fact. I do not see why $k<1$ makes the tail decay slower than any exponential, and the sentence "the tail $\exp(-(x/\lambda)^k)$ decays slower than any exponential" is the conclusion, not the reasoning. Why is $(x/\lambda)^k$ slower than linear for $k<1$? One clause: "because $k<1$ makes the exponent grow sub-linearly, so $e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$ falls more slowly than $e^{-cx}$ for any $c>0$."

## What wasn't elaborated enough

- **The whole discrete section (lines 48 to 78).** Four distributions in one table, two paragraphs, one remark. Bernoulli gets zero prose. Binomial gets zero prose. Poisson gets the "law of rare events" line (line 66) and the additive property (line 69), both of which are good but one sentence each. For a beginner, "binomial = number of successes in $n$ identical independent trials" and "Poisson = limit of binomial when trials are many and each rare" deserve to be said in words, not only in symbols.
- **Beta distribution prose (lines 112 to 121).** This is the conjugate prior for a *probability*, which is the single most important fact for a health researcher (it is how I put a prior on a response rate). The appendix never says that in plain language. It tells me the MGF is the confluent hypergeometric function ${}_1F_1$ (line 115), which is the *least* useful thing for me to know, and skips "beta puts a flexible distribution on a probability; $a=b=1$ is uniform; bigger $a,b$ means more concentrated around $a/(a+b)$."
- **Exponential distribution prose (lines 102 to 110).** It is mentioned as "gamma of unit shape" and as the $k=1$ Weibull with constant hazard, but never as "the simplest model for time-to-event with constant hazard, i.e., memoryless." Memorylessness is the defining intuition for a clinician and it is missing.
- **Dirichlet concentration discussion (lines 227 to 230).** "The parameter $\alpha_0$ acts as a concentration" is the right idea but is one sentence. I would have liked a concrete picture: $\alpha_0$ small = vector spread out near the corners of the simplex; $\alpha_0$ large = vector tightly clustered around the mean vector. Without that, "variances and covariances shrink as $\alpha_0$ grows" is just algebra.
- **Conjugacy section (lines 271 to 298).** The table (@tbl-dist-conjugacy) is excellent and the closing paragraph (lines 292 to 298) on "posterior mean is a convex combination of prior mean and MLE" is the best single sentence in the appendix. But each row is delivered as a formula with no worked mini example. The normal-normal row gives precision addition but never says "if your prior says the effect is about $0.3$ and your data says about $0.5$, the posterior is a weighted average weighted by how confident each is." For a beginner that is the whole point of conjugacy and it is left implicit.
- **Multivariate normal conditioning (lines 169 to 177).** The Schur complement formula is given but never interpreted. "The conditional mean is a linear regression of $\mathbf{X}_1$ on $\mathbf{X}_2$ with regression coefficients $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}$, and the conditional covariance does not depend on the observed value $\mathbf{x}_2$" is the sentence that makes this click, and it is absent.

## Missing motivation / "why does this matter?"

This is the appendix's biggest weakness for my reader profile. The opening (lines 5 to 14) says the extra families "appear later as survival baselines in Chapters 25 and 26 and as conjugate priors in Chapter 8." That is the only global motivation. There is essentially no *per-distribution* motivation tying each family to a concrete ITC/clinical question. Specifically:

- **Which distribution models which clinical outcome?** The book is about comparative effectiveness in HTA. I want a mapping like: Bernoulli/binomial = binary response (e.g., responder vs non responder); Poisson = count of events (e.g., number of relapses); normal = continuous outcome (e.g., change in blood pressure); exponential/Weibull = time to event (e.g., overall survival, progression-free survival); beta = prior on a response probability; gamma = prior on a rate or variance component; Dirichlet = prior on a vector of treatment-choice or multinomial outcome probabilities. None of this is stated. The one near miss is the remark at line 76 about "covariate-adjusted risks," which is the closest the appendix comes to an ITC application, and even there it is abstract.
- **WHY is each distribution in this book at all?** The appendix tells me the Weibull is "the power-law-hazard survival model of @sec-genlik-survival in Chapter 25" (line 84), which is good. But for the beta it only says "conjugate prior of @thm-conjugate-beta-binomial" (line 85); it does not say *prior on what*. For the gamma it says "conjugate prior and count-data baseline of Chapter 8 and as survival baselines in Chapters 25 and 26" (lines 83 to 84), which conflates two very different roles (prior on a Poisson rate versus survival baseline) without distinguishing them.
- **Where does the Poisson binomial actually get used?** It is the most exotic of the discrete families and gets a full table row and a remark, but the appendix never points to a chapter or section that uses it. I assume it shows up in the discrete aggregation material of Part V (the "quantitative total-variation form" hint at line 68), but a forward reference would have reassured me it is not ornamental.
- **Multinomial is referenced but never defined here.** @tbl-dist-conjugacy (line 285) uses "Multinomial$(N,\mathbf{p})$" as a likelihood, and the Dirichlet section calls itself the conjugate for "categorical or multinomial outcome" (lines 184 to 186), but there is no multinomial row in @tbl-dist-discrete and no multinomial density anywhere in the appendix. For a beginner this is a hole: I am told the Dirichlet is conjugate to the multinomial but I have to leave the appendix to learn what a multinomial is.
- **Inverse-gamma is missing entirely.** The task framing for this appendix mentions "inverse-gamma" as one of the families to catalog (it is the standard conjugate prior for a normal variance in hierarchical Bayes, which Chapter 8 covers), but it does not appear in any table or in the conjugacy section. If it is used in Chapter 8's Gibbs samplers it should be here; if it is not used, a one line note saying so would prevent me from searching for it.

## Missing examples and intuition

- **No worked numerical example anywhere in the appendix.** Not one. A single short example for conjugacy would transform the section: "Prior $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ (uniform), observe $y=7$ responders out of $n=10$, posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(8,4)$ with mean $8/12\approx0.67$." That is four lines and it would make the pseudo-counts interpretation (line 295) concrete.
- **No plots.** A catalogue of distributions with no density plots is rough on a beginner. I cannot visualize what $\mathrm{Gamma}(0.5,1)$ versus $\mathrm{Gamma}(5,1)$ looks like, or how the Weibull hazard changes shape with $k$. Even one figure with a 2 by 3 panel of densities would carry more intuition than the entire prose section.
- **No "shape stories."** Gamma: shape $\alpha<1$ gives a density that blows up at zero (monotone decreasing); $\alpha=1$ is exponential; $\alpha>1$ gives a unimodal density starting at zero. Beta: $a=b$ is symmetric; $a>b$ leans right; $a,b<1$ is U-shaped; $a,b>1$ is unimodal. Weibull: $k<1$ decreasing hazard (infant mortality), $k=1$ constant, $k>1$ increasing (aging). None of these verbal shape guides appear, and they are exactly what a clinician needs to pick a model.
- **No example for the Poisson binomial remark (lines 72 to 78).** The "heterogeneous individual risks produce a less dispersed aggregate" point is genuinely useful and surprising, and it would land with a two sentence example: "100 patients with individual risks $p_i$ averaging 0.1 but heterogeneous; the Poisson binomial has smaller variance than $\mathrm{Bin}(100,0.1)$."

## Notation and jargon problems

- **Rate vs scale vs hazard.** The conventions paragraph (lines 41 to 46) warns that gamma and exponential use *rate* while Weibull uses *scale*, and the Weibull remark (lines 132 to 140) reconciles the hazard parametrization. This is the right thing to flag, but as a beginner I am still left juggling three parametrization conventions (rate, scale, hazard) for closely related survival distributions, and the warning is compressed into four lines. A small table ("family, parameter name, parameter symbol, units, mean in terms of it") would eliminate the confusion.
- **$\succ 0$ notation (lines 150, 169).** The MVN table uses $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0$ without restating that this means positive definite; it is in notation.qmd (line 42) but a reader of the appendix in isolation will stumble. One parenthetical would cost nothing.
- **${}_1F_1$ and the Pochhammer symbol $(c)_n$ (lines 115 to 116).** This is the most advanced object in the appendix and it is introduced inline with no warmup. A beginner has never seen the confluent hypergeometric function and the Pochhammer rising factorial. Since the appendix itself says the beta MGF has "no elementary form," the honest message is "you will not use the MGF for the beta; use the moments." Leading with that message and relegating ${}_1F_1$ to a footnote would be kinder.
- **"Lebesgue measure on the $(K-1)$-dimensional simplex, taking $x_1,\dots,x_{K-1}$ as free coordinates" (lines 199 to 200).** Correct and necessary, but jargon dense for my profile. "We treat $x_1,\dots,x_{K-1}$ as the free variables (the last is forced to be $1-\sum_{k<K}x_k$) and integrate over those" is the same content in plain language.
- **$B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ notation.** The multivariate beta $B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ is defined at line 37 and reused at lines 196 to 197, 208, 217. It is fine, but the scalar $B(a,b)$ and the vector $B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ share a symbol and a beginner may not immediately see that the second is the product-of-gammas generalization of the first. One clause linking them would help.
- **Mills ratio (lines 262 to 266).** Named and used but not motivated. Why do I care about bounding $1-\Phi(z)$ for large $z$? The answer (it is how tail probabilities for extreme standardized effects are approximated, and it underlies large-sample normal approximations elsewhere) is not stated.

## Pacing issues

- **The discrete section is too thin relative to the continuous section.** Discrete (lines 48 to 78, about 30 lines) gets one table, two short paragraphs, one remark. Continuous (lines 80 to 140, about 60 lines) gets one table plus three labeled prose blocks plus a remark. Given that Bernoulli, binomial, and Poisson are the workhorses of binary and count outcomes in HTA, the discrete section deserves at least a labeled prose block per family like the continuous section has.
- **The standard normal section (lines 232 to 269) is unusually long and detailed** relative to the discrete section, with differential identities, moments, error function, Mills ratio, probit, and Gaussian copula all packed in. This is valuable material but it makes the appendix end heavy and slightly encyclopedic; some of it (probit link, Gaussian copula generator) is more naturally part of the link-function or integration chapters and could be trimmed or cross referenced.
- **The conjugacy section is the last content section (lines 271 to 298)** even though it is arguably the most practically useful for a Bayesian-modeling beginner. Putting it earlier, right after the continuous distributions, would let the reader meet each family and immediately see its conjugate role, instead of meeting eight distributions and only then learning why.
- **The Dirichlet section (lines 182 to 230) sits between MVN and standard normal.** That ordering is a little odd: MVN, then Dirichlet, then back to univariate standard normal. A reader expecting the univariate families to stay together will be mildly disoriented. Grouping all univariate (including standard normal) first, then multivariate (MVN, Dirichlet), then conjugacy, would read more naturally.
- **The notes section (lines 300 to 311) is good but dense.** It crams every reference into one paragraph. Splitting into a short bullet list ("Bernoulli through Poisson and normal: Chapter 4; gamma, beta, exponential, Weibull, Dirichlet: standard, see Billingsley; conjugacy: Chapter 8; hazard parametrization: Chapter 25, Phillippo 2025") would make it scannable.

## What worked well

- The cross referencing discipline is excellent. Every result points to a named theorem or definition in Chapter 4 or Chapter 8 (e.g., lines 9, 22, 67, 85, 144, 151, 281 to 285), so I always know where to go for the proof.
- The parametrization warning (lines 41 to 46) and the Weibull hazard remark (lines 132 to 140) are exactly the kind of cross-literature trap a beginner needs flagged, and the explicit mapping $k=\nu$, $\lambda(\mathbf{x})=\lambda^{-\nu}$ is concrete and useful.
- The closing paragraph of the conjugacy section (lines 292 to 298), "the posterior mean is in every case a convex combination of the prior mean and the maximum likelihood estimate, the analytic form of shrinkage," is the single best pedagogical sentence in the appendix. It gives the unifying intuition for the whole table.
- The Poisson binomial remark (lines 72 to 78) on heterogeneous risks reducing variance is a genuinely useful, non-obvious fact, and it is correctly tied to the convexity argument.
- The MVN closure properties list (lines 160 to 177) is well organized: affine maps, uncorrelated implies independent, conditioning. As a reference for someone who has already seen the chapter it is clean and findable.
- The standard normal differential identities (@eq-dist-phi-derivs, lines 241 to 243) are a handy reference and the Taylor coefficient reading of the moments (@eq-dist-normal-moments, lines 248 to 252) is a nice touch.
- Honesty about what is not proved here (lines 11 to 13, 144 to 146) sets clear expectations and prevents the appendix from overpromising.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a per-distribution "what it models in HTA" sentence.** For each family, one sentence tying it to a clinical outcome type (binary response, count, time-to-event, prior on a probability, prior on a rate, prior on a variance). This is the highest value change for the beginner health researcher audience.
2. **Add a forward reference table** mapping each distribution to the chapter(s) that use it as a likelihood or prior (e.g., Weibull to @sec-genlik-survival; gamma-Poisson to Chapter 8 and to count outcomes in Part V; beta-binomial to Chapter 8; Dirichlet-multinomial to any multinomial outcome chapter). This turns the appendix into a navigation aid, not just a formula sheet.
3. **Include the multinomial distribution** as a row in @tbl-dist-discrete (or as a short paragraph before the Dirichlet section), with mass function, mean, variance, and the note that it reduces to binomial at $K=2$. The Dirichlet section currently assumes it.
4. **Add the inverse-gamma** family (conjugate prior for a normal variance) if Chapter 8 uses it, or a one line note if it does not, to match the appendix's stated scope.
5. **Add one density plot figure** with small multiples for the gamma, beta, and Weibull shapes, annotated with the shape-story sentences suggested above. Visual shape intuition is what a weak-math reader relies on.
6. **Add one worked conjugacy example** (beta-binomial is the cleanest) with numbers, placed right after @tbl-dist-conjugacy, to make the pseudo-counts and shrinkage interpretations concrete.
7. **Soften the MGF and special-function jargon.** Add a one line intuition for "differentiate $e^{tX}$ to get moments"; demote ${}_1F_1$ to a footnote or parenthetical on the beta MGF; explain the Dirichlet "degenerate MGF on a hyperplane" in plain language.
8. **Expand the discrete section** to one labeled prose block per family (Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson binomial, Poisson), mirroring the continuous section's structure, and add the plain language gloss for the Poisson binomial mass.
9. **Reorder for flow**: conventions, discrete, continuous univariate, standard normal, multivariate normal, Dirichlet, conjugacy. Keep the notes section but convert it to a short bullet list.
10. **Add a small parametrization cheat sheet** (family, parameter name and symbol, units, mean expression) to eliminate the rate/scale/hazard confusion that the conventions paragraph currently only warns about.
11. **Interpret the MVN conditioning formula** (line 169) as a linear regression with coefficients $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}$ and constant conditional covariance; this is the sentence that makes the Schur complement click for a beginner.
12. **State memorylessness for the exponential** explicitly, since it is the defining intuition for constant-hazard time-to-event models and is currently missing despite the exponential appearing in three places (lines 91, 102, 126).
