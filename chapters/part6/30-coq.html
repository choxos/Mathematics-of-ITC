30  Machine-Checked Foundations: The Coq Formalization

30.1 Introduction

Every theorem in the preceding twenty-nine chapters has been proved in the ordinary mathematical sense: a chain of deductions, written for a competent reader, that the author believes is correct and that a referee can in principle check. This is the verification regime of all of mathematics, and it works because of a social apparatus of peer review, replication, and the slow accumulation of trust. It is also fallible. Long algebraic derivations like the multilevel network meta-regression aggregation identity (Theorem 20.1), case analyses with many branches like the scale-specific population-adjustment theorems of Chapter 17, and arguments that turn on a single sign or a single cancellation like Bucher’s subtraction (Theorem 14.1) are exactly the places where a human proof can hide an error that survives review.

This chapter documents a second, independent verification track for part of the book: a companion formalization, the ITC_Coq development, in which a subset of the results is written in the language of the Coq (Rocq) proof assistant and checked, deduction by deduction, by a computer. A statement that compiles in Coq has been verified, relative to a small and explicit logical foundation, to follow from its stated hypotheses. No referee is trusted; no step is elided; the machine refuses to accept a proof with a gap. The development covers the algebraic and discrete-probabilistic core of population adjustment: the potential-outcomes calculus of Chapter 9, the propensity-score and inverse-probability-weighting theory of Chapter 10 (Theorem 10.3, Theorem 10.5), the Rosenbaum-Rubin balancing theorems, Bucher’s identity (Theorem 14.1), the effect-modifier versus prognostic-variable distinction of Chapter 11 (Definition 11.3, Definition 11.5), the matching-adjusted indirect comparison effective-sample-size bound (Theorem 18.6), and the collapsibility of the mean difference that underlies simulated treatment comparison (Definition 11.6).

The chapter is documentary. It does not reprove the mathematics; Chapters 9 through 19 do that. Its job is to describe accurately what the formalization contains, to be precise about what a proof assistant does and does not certify, and to give the reader a map from the book’s tagged theorems to the Coq lemmas that machine-check them. Two pieces of vocabulary are introduced as permanent reference items: the proof-status tiers (Definition 30.1), which classify how completely each catalogued result is formalized, and the active axioms (Definition 30.2), the three classical-logic principles that the development inherits from its mathematics library and on which every machine-checked theorem ultimately rests. Every claim below about what is or is not formally proved has been checked against the source files of the development, including a direct query of the Coq kernel for the axioms each central theorem uses; that query is reproduced in the worked example of Section 30.11.

30.2 Why this matters for a health researcher

This chapter is technical, so it is worth stating at the outset, in plain terms, why a reader who will never open a proof assistant should care, and anchoring the whole discussion in one small comparison that we return to at every step.

A running example: an indirect comparison for an assessment body. Suppose a health technology assessment (HTA) body must compare two drugs, \(A\) and \(B\), for a binary bad outcome such as disease relapse, and no head-to-head trial exists. Two trials share a common comparator \(C\) (standard care): Trial 1 compares \(A\) against \(C\), and Trial 2 compares \(B\) against \(C\). On the log-odds-ratio scale Trial 1 reports \(\hat d_{AC} = -0.80\) and Trial 2 reports \(\hat d_{BC} = -0.50\) (a negative value means lower odds of relapse, hence a better drug). Trial 2 supplies individual patient data (IPD); Trial 1 is published only as aggregate summaries, and its patients are older and sicker, so before comparing we reweight Trial 2’s patients to match Trial 1’s covariate profile, a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC). To keep the arithmetic in view, take Trial 2 to have four patients whose matching weights come out \((2,1,1,0)\); the fourth patient, whose covariate profile lies outside Trial 1’s range, receives weight zero. This example reappears in the Bucher comparison of Section 30.4 and the worked example of Section 30.11.

Three numbers now carry the decision, and each is a result the companion Coq development machine-checks.

  • The indirect contrast \(d_{AB} = \hat d_{AC} - \hat d_{BC} = -0.80 - (-0.50) = -0.30\), Bucher’s subtraction (Theorem 14.1): \(A\) lowers the log-odds of relapse by \(0.30\) relative to \(B\), an odds ratio of \(e^{-0.30} \approx 0.74\).
  • The effective sample size of the reweighted Trial 2, \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\) (Theorem 18.6): the four patients carry the information of at most four, and with weights \((2,1,1,0)\) only \(\mathrm{ESS} = 4^2/6 = 8/3 \approx 2.67\); the matched evidence is worth under three patients.
  • Double robustness of an augmented estimator (Theorem 10.5): a treatment-effect estimate stays valid if either the weighting model or the outcome model is correct.

Here is the point of formalization, made concrete on these numbers. A proof assistant can rule out a class of mistakes.

  • A sign slip. Writing \(\hat d_{BC} - \hat d_{AC} = +0.30\) instead of \(-0.30\) reports an odds ratio of \(1.35\) and flips the recommendation from “\(A\) is better” to “\(B\) is better.” Bucher’s subtraction is a single line of algebra, and one flipped sign changes the clinical conclusion. The Coq theorem bucher_unbiased fixes the orientation once and for all.
  • A vacuous safeguard. If the ESS bound were stated with a hypothesis that secretly already contained the conclusion, it would compile as “true” and mean nothing; the development’s discipline (Section 30.5, illustrated in Example 30.2) is built to prevent exactly this.

Equally, a proof assistant cannot rule out the judgments that matter most to a clinician: whether Trial 1 and Trial 2 populations are exchangeable, whether the covariates that drive relapse were measured, whether the log-odds scale is the intended one, or whether the analysis code that produced \(\hat d_{AC} = -0.80\) was run correctly. Those are scientific and statistical questions; no proof assistant touches them.

So the honest headline for a health researcher is this. You do not need to run Coq. You need to know that someone can, that the load-bearing algebra behind the ESS bound, Bucher’s subtraction, and double robustness has been checked deduction by deduction by a machine rather than trusted to the author’s care, and that the probabilistic steps the machine does not check are named in the open (Section 30.9), so you can see precisely where trust still rests on a paper proof. Three phrases recur in that claim, and they are worth pinning down in plain language now.

  • Machine-checked: a computer has verified every deductive step of the proof, not spot-checked it, relative to the axioms named in Section 30.7.
  • Zero admits: no proof in the development was skipped or left as a hole to be filled later; every stated theorem is proved to completion.
  • Zero (project) axioms: the development introduces no assumptions of its own; it borrows only the three generic logical axioms that all of classical mathematics already uses, and adds nothing about treatments, trials, or effects.

None of the three says the mathematics is clinically true, or that a method is appropriate for a given dataset; they say the stated mathematics is airtight. Keeping that distinction sharp is the recurring theme of the chapter.

Why formalize at all, rather than just be careful? Peer review catches most errors, but not all, and the errors it misses cluster exactly where this book is hardest: long algebraic identities, many-branch case analyses, and arguments that turn on a single sign or a single cancellation. It is not hard to imagine a population-adjustment identity published with a mislabeled contrast or a quietly missing positivity condition, used in an assessment, and corrected only years later. For the fragment it covers, the machine removes that possibility: a slip of that kind cannot compile. That is a different and stronger kind of assurance than “the author and two referees believe it,” and it is available for precisely the parts where human proof is least reliable.

30.3 A Coq vocabulary, and a one-line proof

The rest of the chapter uses the working vocabulary of proof assistants. None of it is deep, but a reader meeting it for the first time deserves plain definitions before the words start doing work. The glossary below is informal on purpose; the precise versions appear where each term is used.

Term Plain meaning
Proof assistant A program in which you write a proof in a formal language it can check completely. Coq, renamed the Rocq Prover in 2025, is the one used here.
Type; term A type is a category an object can belong to (the reals R, the natural numbers, “proofs that \(1+1=2\)”); a term is a specific object of a type. The notation t : P reads “t is a term of type P.”
Proposition, Prop A mathematical statement is a type: the type of its proofs. The statement is true exactly when that type has at least one term. Prop is the universe of such statement-types, as opposed to bool, the two-valued type of true and false used for decidable computation.
Dependent type A type that may depend on a value, such as “vectors of length \(n\),” a family indexed by the number \(n\). “Dependently typed” just means types this expressive are allowed.
Inductive definition The way data and propositions are built from constructors by recursion (the naturals as zero and successor; equality as its single reflexivity proof). “Calculus of Inductive Constructions” is the name of Coq’s logic; its details are not needed here.
Theorem, Lemma Keywords introducing a named claim to prove. They are interchangeable; Lemma conventionally marks a smaller helper.
Goal; hypothesis While a proof is built, the goal is what remains to show and the hypotheses are what may be used. A finished proof reduces the goal to nothing.
Tactic; proof script; Ltac A tactic is one command that transforms the goal (introduce an assumption, apply a known result, do algebra); a proof script is a sequence of tactics; Ltac is the small language for writing new tactics (the development defines two, by_contra and classical_case).
Qed; Admitted; admit. Qed ends a proof and triggers the kernel to check the term the tactics built; if it passes, the theorem is recorded. Admitted ends a proof by accepting it unproved (a hole); admit. is the tactic that leaves one hole. This development contains neither.
Axiom A statement declared true without proof. Print Assumptions t lists every axiom the proof of t ultimately depends on.
Named premise An ordinary hypothesis of one theorem, standing for a fact proved on paper elsewhere. Unlike an axiom it is local to the theorem that names it (contrast Section 30.7 with Section 30.9).
Kernel The small, fixed part of Coq that performs the final check; nothing else can make a false theorem accepted.
mathcomp, ssreflect, mathcomp-analysis The external formal libraries the development builds on: Mathematical Components and its ssreflect proof language, and their analysis layer supplying reals, measure, and probability.
.vo file The compiled, kernel-checked output for one source file; its existence certifies that file’s theorems type-check.

To make “goal,” “tactic,” and “Qed” concrete, here is a complete Coq proof of the arithmetic fact \(1+1=2\), with the machine’s goal state shown as a comment after each step.

Lemma one_plus_one : 1 + 1 = 2.
Proof.
  (* goal: 1 + 1 = 2 *)
  simpl.        (* compute the sum; goal becomes 2 = 2 *)
  reflexivity.  (* anything equals itself; goal closed *)
Qed.            (* kernel checks the finished term; lemma recorded *)

Read it as: the claim is stated, two tactics reduce the goal to nothing, and Qed hands the finished proof term to the kernel. Every proof in the development, however long, has this shape; only the number and ingenuity of the tactics differ.

It helps to picture where trust lives. Everything above the kernel may be arbitrarily complicated, and none of it is trusted; the kernel is the one component that must be correct, and the axioms and libraries below it are what the guarantee is stated relative to.

   your tactics / automation / notation        (untrusted: may be buggy)
                     |
                     v  produce
              a proof term
                     |
                     v  is checked by
   ============== THE KERNEL ==============    <- trust boundary
   |  a few thousand lines; if it accepts, |
   |  the theorem follows from what is below|
   ========================================
                     |
        rests on     v
   three logical axioms                         named premises
   (excluded middle, extensionality,            (paper-proved probability
   choice)  +  mathcomp-analysis                facts, one theorem
   (reals, measure, probability)                at a time)

The three logical axioms and the library sit under the kernel and are shared by all of classical mathematics (Section 30.7); the named premises sit to the side, one theorem at a time, and are the probability facts this project proves on paper rather than in Coq (Section 30.9). Keeping those two kinds of assumption visibly separate is the single distinction a first-time reader should carry forward: an axiom is a generic logical principle the whole library rests on, whereas a named premise is a specific mathematical fact one theorem borrows.

Two libraries deserve a word, since a reader weighing trust will want their pedigree. The ssreflect proof language and the Mathematical Components (mathcomp) library grew out of the fully machine-checked proofs of the four-color theorem and the Feit-Thompson odd-order theorem, two of the largest formalization efforts ever completed; their analysis layer, mathcomp-analysis, is the widely used, separately maintained foundation on which this development’s real numbers and probability rest. And Print Assumptions t, the command used repeatedly below as a certificate, reports the transitive closure of the axioms the proof of t actually uses: not the ordinary inference rules of the logic (those are built into the kernel and are not axioms), but every declared axiom reached through the entire chain of lemmas. An empty list is possible and would signal a fully constructive proof; the lists reported for this development’s central theorems contain exactly three lines.

30.4 A book theorem and its machine-checked twin

With the vocabulary in hand, the quickest way to see what formalization does, and does not, do is to set one theorem beside its Coq counterpart. We use Bucher’s subtraction from the running example, the identity behind the indirect contrast \(d_{AB} = d_{AC} - d_{BC}\).

Aspect Book statement (Theorem 14.1) Coq statement (bucher_unbiased)
Claim if two direct estimators are unbiased for the \(AC\) and \(BC\) contrasts, and the \(AB\) contrast is their difference, then the subtracted estimator is unbiased for \(AB\) the same claim, stated for an abstract operator
The expectation \(\mathbb{E}\), built in Chapter 4 from integration an abstract operator E : (Omega -> R) -> R, given only the property the proof uses (linearity) as a hypothesis
Hypotheses \(\mathbb{E}[\hat d_{AC}] = d_{AC}\); \(\mathbb{E}[\hat d_{BC}] = d_{BC}\); \(d_{AB} = d_{AC} - d_{BC}\) three explicit premises (verbatim below)
Conclusion \(\mathbb{E}[\hat d_{AC} - \hat d_{BC}] = d_{AB}\) E (fun omega => d_AC_hat omega - d_BC_hat omega) = d_AB
What the machine checks (nothing on paper) that the conclusion follows from the three hypotheses using only linearity of E
What stays scientific judgment that the two trials estimate contrasts in exchangeable populations, that \(C\) means the same thing in both, that the log-odds scale is intended identical, and outside Coq’s reach

The Coq statement itself, read token by token, is

Theorem bucher_unbiased (d_AB d_AC d_BC : R) :
  E d_AC_hat = d_AC -> E d_BC_hat = d_BC -> d_AB = d_AC - d_BC ->
  E (fun omega => d_AC_hat omega - d_BC_hat omega) = d_AB.

where each piece reads as follows.

  • Theorem bucher_unbiased names the claim.
  • (d_AB d_AC d_BC : R) introduces three real numbers, the true contrasts.
  • each -> separates one hypothesis from what follows it; there are three hypotheses, then the conclusion, exactly the rows of the table.
  • E d_AC_hat = d_AC is the first hypothesis: the operator E applied to the random estimator d_AC_hat returns the true value d_AC, that is, Trial 1 is unbiased.
  • fun omega => d_AC_hat omega - d_BC_hat omega is the anonymous function sending an outcome omega to the subtracted estimate; E of it is its expectation.
  • the line after the final -> is the goal the proof must reach.

Why this matters. Read together, the table and the statement make “machine-checked” concrete. The machine has verified the orientation and algebra of the subtraction, so the sign error that would flip the running example’s recommendation cannot occur for this result; it has verified nothing about whether the two trials are comparable, which is the scientific premise the researcher must still defend. That division of labor, algebra certified and science reserved, is the pattern of the entire development, and every later section is an instance of it.

Example 30.1 (Bucher’s subtraction with numbers) In the running example \(\hat d_{AC} = -0.80\) and \(\hat d_{BC} = -0.50\) on the log-odds-ratio scale, so the theorem gives \(d_{AB} = d_{AC} - d_{BC} = -0.80 - (-0.50) = -0.30\): drug \(A\) lowers the log-odds of relapse relative to drug \(B\), an indirect odds ratio of \(e^{-0.30} \approx 0.74\). The reversed subtraction \(d_{BC} - d_{AC} = +0.30\) would report an odds ratio of \(e^{0.30} \approx 1.35\) and recommend \(B\); the two conclusions differ by exactly the sign the theorem pins down. What the kernel certifies is that \(\hat d_{AC} - \hat d_{BC}\), and not its negation, is unbiased for \(d_{AB}\); the value \(-0.30\) is then plain arithmetic. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

30.5 What a proof assistant guarantees, and what it does not

Coq, renamed the Rocq Prover in 2025, is a dependently typed proof assistant built on a constructive type theory, the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. In the plain terms of Section 30.3: its types may depend on values (that is what “dependently typed” means), and “constructive” means it proves that something exists only by exhibiting a specific witness, until the classical axioms of Section 30.7 are invoked. Its operating principle is the Curry-Howard correspondence: a mathematical proposition is encoded as a type, a proof of that proposition is a term of that type, and verifying a proof is type-checking a term. The one-line proof of Section 30.3 is a concrete instance: the proposition \(1+1=2\) is a type, and the term the two tactics build is a proof, a member of that type. The component that performs this type-checking is the kernel, a small program (a few thousand lines) that is the entire trusted computing base. Tactics, automation, notation, and the interactive interface may be arbitrarily complicated and even buggy; none of them can make the system accept a false theorem, because whatever proof term they produce must pass the kernel before the theorem is recorded. This is the strong soundness guarantee of the LCF tradition (named for Milner’s Logic for Computable Functions prover, the system whose design introduced this small-trusted-kernel pattern): the trustworthiness of a megabyte of proof script reduces to the trustworthiness of the kilobyte-scale kernel.

The ITC_Coq development is built on mathcomp-analysis, the Mathematical Components extension for classical analysis, which supplies formalized real numbers, measure theory, Lebesgue integration, probability measures, and transition kernels. The development imports these structures rather than rebuilding them, so its real-analytic content stands on a large, widely used, separately maintained library.

It is essential to state precisely what a successful compilation does and does not certify.

Remark. What the kernel guarantees. If Theorem t : P compiles, then there is a term whose type is the encoding of P, accepted by the kernel, using only the inference rules of the type theory together with the axioms listed by Print Assumptions (Definition 30.2). In ordinary language: the statement P follows logically from its hypotheses and from the named axioms. The guarantee is unconditional in the sense that it does not depend on trusting the author, the tactics, or the comments; it is conditional only on the soundness of the kernel and the consistency of the axioms. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What the kernel does not guarantee. Four gaps remain, and honesty about formalization requires naming them.

  1. Statement faithfulness. The kernel checks that the proof proves the stated theorem; it cannot check that the stated theorem is the one the mathematician meant. A formalization of the wrong proposition, or of a proposition with an unintended hypothesis that makes it vacuous, type-checks just as readily as the intended one. Guarding against this is a human task: reading the Coq statement and confirming it says what the book says. The development addresses it by making every modeling hypothesis an explicit, visible premise (Section 30.9) rather than a hidden global assumption, and by avoiding True-valued placeholder definitions that would render downstream theorems vacuous.

  2. Model adequacy. A theorem about a mathematical model of a clinical trial is a theorem about the model, not about the trial. That potential outcomes, exchangeability, and positivity capture the relevant features of a real comparison is a scientific judgment outside the reach of any proof assistant.

  3. Truth of hypotheses. A conditional theorem H -> C says nothing about whether H holds. When H is a probabilistic fact admitted as a named premise (the tower property, say), the machine certifies the implication, not the premise.

  4. Completeness. The development formalizes a chosen subset of the book. Silence about a result is not a claim of any kind; Section 30.13 lists exactly what is in and what is out. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Of the four gaps, statement faithfulness is the one a skeptical methodologist should learn to watch for, so it deserves a concrete illustration rather than a warning in the abstract.

Example 30.2 (A theorem that is true but empty) Imagine formalizing “MAIC is consistent” as the implication assumption -> (estimator = truth), but defining the premise as Definition assumption : Prop := (estimator = truth), that is, letting the hypothesis simply be the conclusion. The implication (estimator = truth) -> (estimator = truth) is trivially provable, compiles without complaint, and earns a proud-looking Qed, yet it says nothing: it has assumed exactly what it claims. A subtler version defines the premise as True, so every downstream theorem True -> C reduces to C asserted for free. Both type-check; both are worthless. The kernel cannot catch either, because each is a valid proof of the sentence as written; only a human reading the premise can see that the sentence is empty. This is why the development refuses True-valued assumption definitions and writes every modeling hypothesis as a visible, contentful premise (Section 30.9), and why a skeptical reader’s first move on any formalization should be to read the hypotheses, not the proof. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

These limits are not peculiar to this project; they apply to all formalized mathematics. Stating them is what lets the positive claims of the chapter be read precisely.

30.6 The proof-status vocabulary

The last of the four gaps in Section 30.5, completeness, leaves a question open: of the results that are formalized, how much of each does the machine actually carry? The proof-status tiers are the chapter’s structured response to that gap. The companion development distinguishes several degrees of formalization, because not every result in a book of this size is formalized to the same depth. The proof catalogue that accompanies the Coq tree assigns each catalogued result one of five status tiers. We adopt that vocabulary as a permanent reference, since it is used throughout this chapter and in the cross-reference table of Section 30.10.

Definition 30.1 (Proof-status tiers) Each result discussed in relation to the companion formalization carries exactly one of the following five status labels.

  • F (fully proved). A complete proof from foundations, machine-checked in Coq or derived in the proof catalogue using only standard library machinery, self-contained and depending on no project-specific unproved assertion.
  • P (partial). The result is stated and its substantive content proved under sufficient conditions, but some step is supplied as an explicit named hypothesis standing for a standard fact (catalogued with its justification), or cites a standard external theorem rather than deriving it. The machine-checked content is the algebra and logic around that hypothesis.
  • S (summarized). The result is stated and its role explained, but the full proof is referenced rather than derived; it is not formalized.
  • O (out of scope). Applied or empirical content (case studies, software-specific implementation detail) that is intentionally not formalized.
  • E (extension). Material beyond the 2019 thesis that frames the work; stated with proof where applicable but not part of the formalized core.

Within the Coq tree itself every compiled statement is, in the kernel’s sense, fully proved: there are no admitted proofs. The tier distinction records how much of a book result a given Coq statement captures. A theorem proved in Coq with a probabilistic premise supplied as a named hypothesis is tier F as a Coq object (it compiles with no gap) and tier P as a formalization of the book theorem (the premise is named, not derived). The table of Section 30.10 makes this two-level reading explicit for each entry. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. In one breath: F means the machine carries the whole result; P means the machine carries the algebra while one clearly named probability fact is proved on paper; S means only the statement and its role are recorded, the sharp proof living on paper; O and E mark material deliberately outside the formalized core. In compact form:

Tier Plain meaning Example in this development
F fully machine-checked, no project premise the ESS bound ESS_upper_bound
P algebra machine-checked; one probability fact named as a premise IPW unbiasedness mu1_IPW_unbiased
S stated and situated; full proof referenced, not formalized the MAIC-STC equivalence
O applied or software detail, intentionally not formalized case-study specifics
E extension beyond the framing thesis; proved where applicable, not in the core later transportability material

The subtle point, and the one worth slowing down for, is that a single Coq theorem carries two readings. As a Coq object it is always fully proved: it compiled, so its own goal follows from its own hypotheses with no gap. As a formalization of the book theorem it may be only partial, because one of those hypotheses stands in for a book step that was proved on paper, not in Coq. The template below records both readings at once and is worth keeping in mind for the whole chapter.

Result What Coq proves Still supplied outside Coq Practical reading
ESS bound the arithmetic inequality \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\) whether \(\mathrm{ESS}\) is the right diagnostic at all a strong algebraic check
IPW unbiasedness the estimator algebra, given the premise the tower-property identification step a conditional check

A health researcher should read a tier the way one reads a confidence interval: as information about how much has been established, never as a verdict on whether the method suits a particular dataset.

30.7 The active axioms

A constructive type theory proves a great deal without any axioms, but classical mathematics, and in particular measure-theoretic probability, needs a small classical extension: the law of the excluded middle, the ability to prove sets and functions equal from their pointwise behavior, and a form of the axiom of choice for selecting representatives and constructing conditional objects. The development does not declare these itself; it inherits them from mathcomp-analysis, whose boolp (“Boolean propositions”) module declares exactly three axioms from which the rest of classical reasoning is derived.

Definition 30.2 (The active classical axioms) Every machine-checked theorem in the companion development rests, beyond the rules of the type theory, on exactly the following three axioms imported from the boolp module of mathcomp-analysis. The statements are as the Coq kernel reports them.

  1. Propositional extensionality. Logically equivalent propositions are equal: propositional_extensionality : forall P Q : Prop, (P <-> Q) -> P = Q. This licenses treating an event as the set of outcomes satisfying it, so that \(\{x : P(x)\} = \{x : Q(x)\}\) whenever \(P \iff Q\).

  2. Dependent functional extensionality. Functions that agree at every argument are equal: functional_extensionality_dep : forall (A : Type) (B : A -> Type) (f g : forall x, B x), (forall x, f x = g x) -> f = g. This licenses proving two random variables, densities, or conditional expectations equal by comparing their values pointwise; it is the workhorse behind almost every integrand rewrite in the tree.

  3. Constructive indefinite description (choice). From a proof that a satisfying element exists, one may extract such an element: constructive_indefinite_description : forall (A : Type) (P : A -> Prop), (exists x, P x) -> {x : A | P x}. This is the type-theoretic axiom of choice on which mathcomp’s classical infrastructure is built; it underlies the existence of conditional expectations and the selection of representatives.

The law of the excluded middle is not a separate axiom: it is a theorem, derived from these three by Diaconescu’s argument (the short classical construction that, given any proposition \(P\), builds two subsets of a two-element set whose membership is entangled with \(P\), uses the choice axiom to select an element from each, and uses extensionality to compare the selections; the comparison decides \(P\) either way), and exported by boolp as EM and by the development’s Axioms.v as excluded_middle. Consequently a proof that uses classical case analysis adds no axiom beyond the three above. There are no project-specific axioms and no active admissions: the development declares no Axiom of its own and contains no Admitted proof or admit tactic. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What each axiom buys, in the language of estimands. The three principles are generic, but each has a familiar shadow in this book’s mathematics, which makes them less forbidding.

  • Propositional extensionality lets an event be identified with the set of outcomes satisfying it: two events described by logically equivalent conditions are the same set to integrate over, so one may rewrite “relapsed, and on \(A\) or on \(B\)” into any logical equivalent without changing the probability.
  • Functional extensionality lets two functions be declared equal once they agree at every input: two predicted-outcome surfaces, say two fitted \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]\) functions, are the same object as soon as they match at every covariate profile \(x\). This is the workhorse that turns a pointwise rewrite of an integrand into an equation between whole estimands.
  • Constructive indefinite description (choice) lets one name and compute with an object known only to exist: from “a conditional expectation exists” one may select a version and use it, the same silent selection an analyst makes in picking a measurable \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\).

And the distinction a first-time reader most needs: these three are logical axioms, principles of classical mathematics itself, sitting under the kernel and shared by every theorem. They are not the same as the named premises of Section 30.9, which are specific probability facts (the tower property, Jensen’s inequality) that individual theorems borrow. Conflating the two would make the formalization look circular; keeping them apart is what makes the trust claim honest. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why these three are uncontroversial. The trio is the standard foundation of classical mathematics as practiced. Propositional and functional extensionality are conservative over, and validated by, set models of type theory; the choice principle is the same one used silently in every analysis textbook that picks a sequence realizing a limit or a measurable selection. They are jointly consistent with Coq’s type theory, a fact relied upon by the entire mathcomp-analysis ecosystem. Crucially, they are generic mathematical axioms, not assumptions about treatment effects: nothing causal, nothing about trials, and nothing that could quietly make a population-adjustment theorem come out the way one hoped. Every domain assumption in the development is instead a visible premise (Section 30.9). The contrast matters for trust: a reader who accepts ordinary classical analysis has already accepted everything the formalization assumes about logic. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

That the closure of the central theorems is exactly these three axioms, and nothing more, is not an article of faith: the Coq kernel will print the axioms a given theorem depends on, and Section 30.11 reproduces that output for six headline results.

30.8 The structure of the theories directory

The development is fifteen Coq source files in theories/, listed in _CoqProject in dependency order and grouped into five phases. We walk through them in that order, naming the load-bearing definitions and lemmas of each.

A design choice recurs in every file and is worth understanding once. Many theorems are stated over an abstract expectation operator written E : (Omega -> R) -> R, a function that takes a random quantity (itself a function from outcomes Omega to reals) and returns a number. It plays the role of the book’s expectation \(\mathbb{E}\) from the Notation, but it is deliberately not the same object: \(\mathbb{E}\) is built in Chapter 4 out of integration, whereas E is left abstract and handed only the properties a given proof actually uses, chiefly linearity, as named hypotheses. Far from weakening the results, this isolates the algebraic part of an argument, which Coq then certifies completely, from the measure-theoretic part, which enters as a visible premise and is proved on paper. The gap is meant to be closed by later instantiating E with mathcomp-analysis’s Lebesgue integral; leaving it abstract for now keeps the checked content honest about exactly which step has been mechanized and which has not. The pattern was pioneered in Bucher.v and reused throughout. Note that E is a Coq identifier standing in for the book symbol \(\mathbb{E}\); the gap between them is the point, not a typographical accident.

30.8.1 Phase 1: foundations

For ITC, this phase fixes the vocabulary before any method appears: what treatments, outcomes, and causal estimands are, and when a treatment effect is even identifiable from data.

Axioms.v centralizes the classical-logic helpers over the three boolp axioms: excluded_middle, proof_by_contradiction, double_negation, the classical De Morgan equivalences (not_and_or, not_or_and), and the non-dependent functional-extensionality wrapper fun_ext used pervasively for integrand rewriting. BasicTypes.v fixes the foundational types: binary treatment as bool with the embedding bool_to_R into the reals, proved to take values in \(\{0,1\}\) (treatment_01) with the complement identity treatment_complement; abstract probability spaces; outcome, treatment, and covariate random-variable types; and basic measurability facts (measurable_composition, measurable_const).

PotentialOutcomes.v builds the Rubin causal model on an abstract expectation. It defines the potential outcomes \(Y(0), Y(1)\), the individual treatment effect ITE, and the estimands ATE, ATT, ATC, and naive_comparison. The proved results include ATE_split (\(\mathrm{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0)]\) from linearity), ATE_decomposition (the law of total expectation over the treatment partition, with E_total named), the consistency-conversion identities (consistency_expectation, consistency_treated_mean), and Holland’s selection_bias_decomposition, which exhibits the naive comparison as \(\mathrm{ATT}\) plus an exactly identified selection-bias term. The fundamental problem of causal inference appears as ITE_unobservable: a concrete two-world counterexample over \(\Omega = \texttt{bool}\) with identical observed data but different individual effects, proving that the ITE is not a function of the observables.

CausalAssumptions.v formalizes the identifying assumptions. SUTVA is made substantive: outcomes a priori depend on the whole treatment vector, no_interference is the stability condition, and no_interference_unit_level proves that under no interference a unit-level outcome function exists, licensing the two-potential-outcomes setup. Positivity (weak_positivity, strong_positivity), unconfoundedness, and strong ignorability are defined, with strong_implies_weak_unconf proved. The two identification theorems, ATE_identification (the g-formula) and ATE_IPW_identification, are proved with the per-arm probabilistic content supplied as named premises and the consistency conversion machine-checked pointwise.

30.8.2 Phase 2: conditional independence and balancing scores

This phase proves the propensity-score theory that licenses adjusting for a summary of the covariates instead of all of them, the backbone of every weighting method in Part II; it is also the part discharged outright, with no probabilistic premise, over a finite discrete model.

ConditionalIndep.v is the technical heart of the tree, and every statement in it is proved. At kernel level it defines conditional expectation against a probability kernel, cond_exp, and proves cond_exp_linear (from integralD, integralZl) and cond_exp_const (from integral_cst and the kernel normalization). Conditional independence is defined as rectangle factorization of the joint kernel (cond_indep), with cond_indep_equiv proving the equivalence to the indicator-expectation form in both directions; the extension to all bounded measurable test functions is a monotone-class argument documented as out of scope and neither assumed nor used. The graphoid properties are then proved over a discrete model, a finite joint probability mass function in cross-multiplied, division-free form: cond_indep_weak_union and cond_indep_contraction, with no positivity assumptions, zero-mass conditioning events handled by marginal monotonicity. The engine of the propensity-score theory is transfer_independence, proved from a within-fiber pointwise identity (transfer_pointwise) summed over the fiber.

BalancingScore.v defines is_balancing_score and proves Rosenbaum-Rubin Theorem 2, balancing_score_sufficiency: unconfoundedness given \(X\) together with the balancing property of a score \(b\) implies unconfoundedness given \(b(X)\), by direct instantiation of the transfer lemma. It also proves identity_is_balancing and propensity_is_coarsest, the statement that every balancing score refines the propensity score.

PropensityScore.v proves the kernel-level facts propensity_score_range (\(0 \le e(x) \le 1\)) and propensity_as_conditional_expectation (\(e(x) = \mathbb{E}[T \mid X = x]\)), and then the discrete Rosenbaum-Rubin theorems with complete proofs and no axioms: propensity_score_is_balancing (Theorem 1, the propensity score is a balancing score, valid even at zero-mass covariate points because of the mathcomp-analysis library convention that a real division by zero returns zero, safe here for the reason given in Section 30.9: the theorem is written in a cross-multiplied form where such a point makes both sides zero rather than dividing by it) and propensity_score_sufficiency_discrete (Theorem 3, by composing Theorem 1 with the transfer lemma).

30.8.3 Phase 3: estimators

Here are the three estimators a researcher actually computes, inverse-probability weighting, G-computation, and their doubly robust combination, each shown unbiased. Their algebra is machine-checked; the identification steps enter as the named premises of Section 30.9.

IPWEstimator.v defines the inverse-probability-weighting estimators mu1_IPW, mu0_IPW, ATE_IPW, their Hajek normalizers, and the variance objects. It proves mu1_IPW_unbiased, mu0_IPW_unbiased, and ATE_IPW_unbiased with the consistency conversion machine-checked and the tower-property premise named; var_mu1_IPW_ge0 and ESS_variance_bound (the population effective-sample-size fraction is at most one) from the L2 Jensen hypothesis E_jensen_sqr, the formal counterpart of Theorem 4.6; and IPW_bias_formula, the misspecification bias in difference-of-inverses form, by pure linearity.

OutcomeRegression.v defines the G-computation estimators mu1_OR, mu0_OR, ATE_OR and proves ATE_as_averaged_CATE (by linearity alone), the per-arm unbiasedness theorems under named identification premises, and OR_bias_formula.

DoublyRobust.v defines the augmented estimators mu1_AIPW, mu0_AIPW, ATE_AIPW. Its core is the pointwise cancellation identity AIPW1_pointwise (with control mirror AIPW0_pointwise), proved by case analysis with total division and no positivity. Double robustness is then genuine: AIPW_consistent_correct_PS and AIPW_consistent_correct_OR each conclude consistency from one named mean-zero-residual premise, double_robustness is the honest case split over the disjunction, and ATE_AIPW_double_robust assembles both arms via ATE_split. These are the formal counterparts of Theorem 10.5.

30.8.4 Phase 4: indirect-comparison methods

This phase reaches the population-adjustment methods themselves, and the results the running example uses: the MAIC effective-sample-size bound, the collapsibility that makes STC’s plug-in valid, and the MAIC-versus-STC contrast.

MAIC.v defines the entropy-balanced weight maic_weight_fn x := expR (linpred x), proved positive (maic_weight_pos), and the effective sample size ESS. Its mathematical content is the effective-sample-size bound, proved from scratch: the termwise arithmetic-geometric-mean inequality two_mul_le_sqr, assembled by induction into cauchy_schwarz_ones, specialized to the weight vector as cauchy_schwarz_n_weights, and divided through to give ESS_upper_bound (\(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\)). This is the machine-checked counterpart of Theorem 18.6 and the worked example of Section 30.11. The four MAIC assumptions are abstract named propositions, deliberately not defined as True; the consistency argument itself is the paper proof of Chapter 18.

STC.v defines the marginal simulated-treatment-comparison estimator over a target-population expectation E2 and proves STC_collapse_linear: for a linear outcome model \(\mu(t,x) = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma\mathbf{1}\{t\}\), the marginal contrast equals \(\gamma\), using only linearity and the constant rule. This is the machine-checked core of why the mean difference is collapsible (Definition 11.6, Definition 7.7): the proof uses exactly the linearity that a nonlinear link destroys. The consistency theorem STC_consistent holds under named identification premises.

Comparison.v records the MAIC-versus-STC method profiles as decidable records and proves the profile-level facts assumption_difference, MAIC_robust_to_outcome_model, and STC_robust_to_poor_overlap by computation; methods_agree_when_correct, that the two estimates coincide when both identification hypotheses hold; and MAIC_variance_inflation_ge1, the quantitative statement that the variance inflation factor \(n/\mathrm{ESS}\) is at least one whenever \(0 < \mathrm{ESS} \le n\).

30.8.5 Phase 5: indirect-comparison algebra

The final phase certifies the pure algebra of indirect comparison: Bucher’s subtraction from the running example, and the effect-modifier versus prognostic-variable distinction that decides when anchored adjustment is even needed.

Bucher.v proves transitivity_subtract, bucher_identity (the exact-estimate form), bucher_unbiased (the expectation form over an abstract linear E, the counterpart of Theorem 14.1), and bucher_variance (additivity of variances under zero covariance). This file established the abstract-operator pattern reused by the rest of the tree.

EffectModifiers.v formalizes the effect-modifier versus prognostic-variable distinction (Definition 11.3, Definition 11.5). It defines not_EM (the conditional contrast does not depend on the covariate) and not_PV_for_A/not_PV_for_C, proves anchored_PV_cancellation (if a covariate is not an effect modifier, the conditional contrast is constant in it, so anchored adjustment is unaffected by its distribution), and exhibits the separation not_EM_does_not_imply_not_PV by the concrete counterexample \(\mu_A(x,w) = \mu_C(x,w) = \mathbf{1}\{w\}\) over \(X_{\mathrm{unmeasured}} = \texttt{bool}\): the contrast is identically zero (so not_EM holds) while each surface depends on \(w\) (so not_PV_for_A fails). The inequality \(1 \ne 0\) is discharged by Num.Theory.ltr01.

Example 30.3 (Effect modifier versus prognostic variable, with numbers) Take the unmeasured covariate \(w \in \{0,1\}\) and outcome surfaces \(\mu_A(w) = \mu_C(w) = \mathbf{1}\{w=1\}\), so each surface equals \(0\) when \(w=0\) and \(1\) when \(w=1\). The conditional contrast is \(\mu_A(w) - \mu_C(w) = 0\) for both \(w=0\) and \(w=1\): the contrast does not depend on \(w\), so \(w\) is not an effect modifier (not_EM holds). Yet each surface itself moves from \(0\) to \(1\) as \(w\) goes \(0 \to 1\), so \(w\) is prognostic (not_PV_for_A fails). One covariate, two roles. This is the separation the Coq lemma not_EM_does_not_imply_not_PV certifies, and it is why anchored indirect comparison may ignore the distribution of a pure prognostic variable while unanchored comparison cannot; the single inequality the proof needs, \(1 \ne 0\), is the one discharged by Num.Theory.ltr01. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

30.9 The named-hypothesis boundary and the discrete model

The design decision that makes a gap-free development possible is the treatment of probabilistic content. Rather than postulate measure-theoretic facts as global axioms, the development admits them as named section hypotheses displayed in each theorem’s own statement, so that what is assumed is always visible and never hidden.

For a health researcher this visibility is the useful part: instead of a method that works “under standard assumptions,” one sees, printed in the theorem, exactly where exchangeability, positivity, a tower-property step, or a mean-zero-residual condition enters, and therefore exactly which judgment is carrying the weight. The cost of the design deserves stating as plainly as the benefit. A theorem that names its probabilistic premises is, as a formalization of the book result, weaker than one that derives them: the machine certifies the algebra around the premise, not the premise itself. What is bought is a gap-free, fully visible account of which steps are mechanized; what is deferred is the machine-checking of the probability facts, which stay paper-proved in the chapters cited. The table below is therefore also a map of where trust still rests on human proof.

There is one important class of results where the probabilistic content is not deferred but discharged outright: the Rosenbaum-Rubin theorems and the graphoid properties. These are proved over a finite discrete model, a joint probability mass function over finite types, with conditional independence written in cross-multiplied form such as \[ q_{XYZ}(x,y,z)\, q_Z(z) \;=\; q_{XZ}(x,z)\, q_{YZ}(y,z). \tag{30.1}\] In this form the proofs need no positivity assumptions: a zero conditioning mass annihilates both sides by marginal monotonicity (every marginal is dominated by the conditioning mass).

The reason is elementary and worth spelling out, since it is the whole point of the cross-multiplication. Written as a division, conditional independence would read \(q_{XYZ}/q_Z = (q_{XZ}/q_Z)(q_{YZ}/q_Z)\), which is meaningless at a stratum \(z\) where the conditioning mass \(q_Z(z)\) is zero. Written as the product identity Equation 30.1, that same stratum forces every factor on both sides to be zero (a marginal can never exceed the total mass it is a marginal of; this domination is the “marginal monotonicity” invoked), so the line reads \(0 = 0\) and holds with nothing to prove. The cross-multiplication converts a potential division by zero into a trivially true equation, which is exactly why no positivity assumption is needed.

Example 30.4 (An empty covariate cell) Take a covariate stratum \(z\) that no trial patient occupies, so \(q_Z(z) = 0\): an age-by-comorbidity cell that happens to be empty. Then \(q_{XZ}(x,z) = 0\) for every \(x\), \(q_{YZ}(y,z) = 0\) for every \(y\), and \(q_{XYZ}(x,y,z) = 0\), so Equation 30.1 reads \(0 \cdot 0 = 0 \cdot 0\) and the propensity-score balancing theorem holds at this cell automatically. This is exactly the behavior one wants: an empty stratum should neither break the theorem nor have to be excluded by a positivity assumption. The divided form would instead stumble on \(0/0\) at precisely this cell, which is why the continuous version, lacking the cross-multiplication trick, must currently assume positivity or defer the step. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The transfer lemma transfer_independence, Theorem 1 (propensity_score_is_balancing), Theorem 3 (propensity_score_sufficiency_discrete), and the graphoid laws cond_indep_weak_union and cond_indep_contraction are therefore tier F with no named probabilistic premise at all. Lifting these discrete proofs to the continuous kernel model awaits a conditional-expectation and disintegration library (a disintegration is the measure-theoretic operation that slices a joint distribution into a family of conditional distributions, one for each value of the conditioning variable; it is what makes \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]\) rigorous when \(X\) is continuous) that mathcomp-analysis does not yet provide; the kernel-level statements in the tree are shaped to receive that lift, taking the disintegration compatibility as an explicit hypothesis in the interim.

30.11 A worked example: tracing the ESS bound to the kernel

To make the abstract guarantee concrete, we follow a single book theorem all the way into the machine: the effective-sample-size bound \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\) of Theorem 18.6. It is an ideal specimen because it depends on no probabilistic premise, only on classical logic and arithmetic, so its machine-checked closure is the cleanest possible illustration of Definition 30.2.

Recall from Chapter 18 the definition of the effective sample size of a nonnegative weight vector \(\mathbf{w} = (w_1,\dots,w_n)\), \[ \mathrm{ESS} \;=\; \frac{\big(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\big)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2}, \tag{30.2}\] and the claim \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\), with equality iff the weights are equal (this is F.8 in the proof catalogue). In MAIC.v the quantities and the theorem read as follows, verbatim.

Definition ESS : R :=
  if (0 < sum_w2)%R then (sum_w)^+2 / sum_w2 else 0.

Theorem ESS_upper_bound :
  0 < sum_w2 -> ESS <= n%:R.

The proof is built bottom-up from three lemmas. The base case is the termwise arithmetic-geometric-mean inequality, itself a one-line consequence of \(0 \le (x-y)^2\):

Lemma two_mul_le_sqr (x y : R) : x * y *+ 2 <= x ^+ 2 + y ^+ 2.

This is summed, by induction on the number of terms, into a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality against the all-ones vector:

Lemma cauchy_schwarz_ones (m : nat) (f : 'I_m -> R) :
  (\sum_(i < m) f i) ^+ 2 <= m%:R * \sum_(i < m) (f i) ^+ 2.

In the inductive step, the cross term \(2 S a\) produced by expanding \((S + a)^2\) is bounded summand by summand using two_mul_le_sqr, and the pieces reassemble into the \((m+1)\)-term bound. Specializing the test function to the weights gives \((\sum_i w_i)^2 \le n \sum_i w_i^2\) (cauchy_schwarz_n_weights), and dividing by the positive quantity \(\sum_i w_i^2\) gives exactly \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\). No nonnegativity of the weights is even required for the inequality; positivity enters only to license the division.

Now the decisive step, the one a paper proof cannot perform: ask the kernel which axioms the finished theorem actually uses. Loading the compiled development and issuing Print Assumptions ESS_upper_bound produces

Axioms:
boolp.propositional_extensionality : forall P Q : Prop, P <-> Q -> P = Q
boolp.functional_extensionality_dep :
  forall (A : Type) (B : A -> Type) (f g : forall x : A, B x),
  (forall x : A, f x = g x) -> f = g
boolp.constructive_indefinite_description :
  forall (A : Type) (P : A -> Prop), (exists x : A, P x) -> {x : A | P x}

exactly the three axioms of Definition 30.2 and nothing else: no project axiom, no admitted lemma, no hidden assumption. The same query on cauchy_schwarz_ones, bucher_unbiased, not_EM_does_not_imply_not_PV, propensity_score_is_balancing, and double_robustness returns the identical three-line list. This is the verification, run against the compiled tree, that backs every “machine-checked” claim in this book.

Example 30.5 (The certified inequality by hand) To close the loop with a hand computation, take \(n = 4\). Uniform weights \(\mathbf{w} = (1,1,1,1)\) give \(\sum_i w_i = 4\) and \(\sum_i w_i^2 = 4\), so \(\mathrm{ESS} = 16/4 = 4 = n\): the bound is attained exactly, as the equality case predicts. The imbalanced weights \(\mathbf{w} = (2,1,1,0)\) give \(\sum_i w_i = 4\) and \(\sum_i w_i^2 = 4 + 1 + 1 = 6\), so \(\mathrm{ESS} = 16/6 = 8/3 \approx 2.667 < 4\), and the variance inflation factor is \(n/\mathrm{ESS} = 4/(8/3) = 3/2\), consistent with MAIC_variance_inflation_ge1. Every arithmetic step here is an instance of the inequality the kernel certified. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

For the running example this is the difference between counting patients and counting information. Trial 2’s four patients, after matching to Trial 1, carry an effective sample size of \(8/3 \approx 2.67\), and the fourth patient, with weight zero, contributes nothing at all: her covariate profile lay outside Trial 1’s range, so matching could not place her in the target population. Uneven weights are the signature of poor overlap between the two trials; poor overlap means less effective information, which means a larger variance inflation factor \(n/\mathrm{ESS} = 3/2\), which means a wider interval on the transported effect and a less stable population adjustment. The bound \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\) is thus no technicality: it is the certified statement that reweighting can only spend information, never create it, so an assessment body reading a MAIC can treat the reported ESS as a hard ceiling on how much the matched evidence is worth. The machine has verified the ceiling; whether the matching was appropriate in the first place remains, as always, a scientific judgment.

Remark. What the example does and does not show. The kernel has certified that \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\) follows from arithmetic and the three axioms; it has not certified that \(\mathrm{ESS}\) as defined in Equation 30.2 is the right diagnostic for weighted estimation, nor that the entropy weights of Theorem 18.1, whose derivation is paper-only, are the weights one should use. The mathematics of why this quantity governs variance is Chapter 18; the machine guarantees only the inequality, which is precisely the load-bearing algebra. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

30.12 Building and checking the development

The development is self-contained and reproducible. The build is driven by coq_makefile from the file list in _CoqProject, which also declares the logical mapping -R theories ITC, so that the modules are addressed as ITC.Bucher, ITC.MAIC, and so on. The toolchain requirements, as declared in _CoqProject and the project README, are Coq (Rocq) 9.1 or later, mathcomp-ssreflect 2.0 or later, mathcomp-algebra, mathcomp-analysis 1.9 or later, and Hierarchy Builder 1.6 or later; the development was checked with the Rocq Prover 9.1.0.

To compile and check the whole tree, from the repository root:

make

The top-level Makefile regenerates Makefile.coq from _CoqProject if needed and then compiles all fifteen .v files; a clean rebuild is make clean && make. A successful run produces a .vo (compiled, kernel-checked) object for each source file and exits without error, which is itself the certificate that every theorem in the tree type-checks. A health researcher is not expected to read the Coq scripts or even to run this build; the value of quoting the commands is that they exist and are cheap. The assurance does not depend on the reader executing them, only on their being executable by anyone who wishes to, which is what makes the result independent of trust in the author.

The inventory claims of this chapter can be reproduced directly. That there are no admitted proofs and no project axioms is confirmed by

grep -nE "Admitted|^Axiom|admit\." theories/*.v   # no output

which returns nothing across the fifteen files. Reading the search pattern: Admitted is Coq’s keyword for a proof abandoned and accepted unproved, Axiom declares a new unproved assumption, and admit. is the tactic that leaves a single hole in an otherwise finished proof; the command looks for any of the three in every .v file. Its empty output is the evidence that the tree contains no abandoned proof and no project-specific axiom, the two ways a formalization could quietly accept something on trust. The axiom closure of any individual theorem is confirmed, as in Section 30.11, by a short script that requires the relevant module and calls Print Assumptions on the theorem of interest; for the central results this reports exactly the three boolp axioms of Definition 30.2. The canonical inventory, the named-hypothesis catalogue, and these verification commands are recorded in theories/AXIOMS.md within the development.

30.13 What is and is not machine-checked

We close with an explicit accounting, so that the silence noted in Section 30.5 is filled in.

Machine-checked, depending only on the three active axioms: the classical-logic foundations; the real causal estimands and their algebra (ATE split and decomposition, Holland’s selection-bias decomposition, the consistency conversions); the fundamental problem of causal inference by concrete counterexample; the SUTVA representation lemma; conditional-expectation linearity and the constant rule at kernel level; the factorization-versus-indicator-expectation equivalence; the graphoid weak-union and contraction laws over the discrete model with no positivity; the transfer lemma; Rosenbaum-Rubin Theorems 1, 2, and 3 over the discrete model; kernel-level propensity-score range and its conditional-expectation characterization; the IPW, G-computation, and AIPW estimator algebra, the AIPW cancellation identity, and double robustness as a genuine case split; Bucher’s identity in expectation and variance form; the anchored cancellation and the effect-modifier versus prognostic-variable separation; Cauchy-Schwarz and the \(\mathrm{ESS} \le n\) bound; and the linear-model collapsibility of the marginal contrast.

Machine-checked modulo named probabilistic premises (tier P): IPW, G-computation, and AIPW unbiasedness and identification, where the tower property, the law of total expectation, conditional-expectation locality, L2 Jensen, and the mean-zero-residual facts enter as the explicit hypotheses catalogued in Section 30.9. The paper proofs of these premises are in Parts A and B of the catalogue and in Chapters 4, 9, and 10.

Paper proof only (tiers S, O, E), not formalized: the probability and inference foundations beyond the algebra above (Chapters 1 through 5, including Theorem 5.7, Theorem 5.6, and Definition 5.11); standard and network meta-analysis (Chapters 13 through 16); the scale-specific anchored and unanchored population-adjustment theorems and the transportability framework (Chapters 12 and 17, including Theorem 12.6, Definition 12.3, Definition 17.6, Theorem 17.2); the MAIC consistency and entropy-balancing derivation (Chapter 18), of which only the weight form, positivity, and the ESS bound are formalized; the STC plug-in bias and the MAIC-STC equivalence (Theorem 19.7, Chapter 19); the entire multilevel network meta-regression development, including the aggregation theory (Theorem 20.1, Theorem 20.2), the Poisson-binomial approximations, the quasi-Monte-Carlo theory (Theorem 22.3), and the copula material (Definition 22.5) of Chapters 20 through 25; the multilevel unanchored extension (Definition 26.2, Theorem 26.5, Chapter 26); the distance-matching algebra of Chapter 27; the quantitative-bias-analysis machinery of Chapter 28; and the simulation theory of Chapter 29. These are proved in full in the chapters cited; their absence from the Coq tree reflects the current reach of the underlying analysis library and the project’s choice to formalize the algebraic and discrete core first, not any doubt about the results.

The two tracks are complementary. The machine checks the parts that are pure algebra and discrete probability, where a hidden slip is most likely and least visible; the paper proofs carry the measure-theoretic and asymptotic arguments that the library does not yet support. A reader who wants the strongest available assurance for, say, double robustness or the ESS bound can compile the development and read the kernel’s verdict; a reader who wants the full sweep of the theory reads the book.
