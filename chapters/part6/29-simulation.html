29  Simulation Study Theory

29.1 Introduction

The theorems of Parts II to V are limit statements. They certify that an estimator is consistent: that matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) recovers the target contrast as the index sample grows (Theorem 18.4), that the unanchored estimators do so only under the far stronger absolute-constancy hypothesis (Theorem 17.2), that multilevel network meta-regression removes the aggregation bias a standard network meta-analysis cannot (Theorem 20.2, Theorem 16.1). What these theorems do not say is how an estimator behaves at the sample sizes a health-technology-assessment dossier actually carries: a single index trial of a few hundred patients, a comparator known only through a published mean. Consistency is a statement about the horizon; the analyst stands at a finite distance from it. Four questions remain open at that distance, and none of them is answerable by a limit theorem alone.

How large is the finite-sample bias, and is the residual bias of an “asymptotically unbiased” method negligible at the sample size in hand? How does one method’s variance compare with another’s, so that a slightly biased but stable estimator can be preferred to an unbiased but volatile one? Do the nominal ninety-five percent confidence intervals actually contain the truth ninety-five percent of the time, or does the variance approximation that produced them quietly fail? And how do all of these degrade when the method’s assumptions are violated, when an effect modifier is omitted, when the populations barely overlap, when the outcome model is wrong? These are empirical questions about the sampling distribution of an estimator at finite \(n\), and the instrument that answers them is the simulation study: fix a data-generating mechanism whose truth you control, draw many datasets from it, apply each candidate method to each dataset, and summarize how the resulting estimates scatter around the known target. The decisive advantage of this instrument is that in a simulation we build the world ourselves, so the truth is known by construction: unlike a real analysis, where the target effect can never be observed and an estimator’s error can only be guessed at, a simulation lets us measure that error directly, by comparing every estimate against the number we put in.

This chapter is the mathematics of that instrument. The central conceptual move, which organizes everything that follows, is to recognize that a simulation study has two sample sizes, governing two asymptotics. The first is \(n\), the size of each simulated dataset; it controls the statistical behavior of the estimator and is the variable in every consistency theorem of the preceding chapters. The second is \(R\), the number of simulated datasets, the replications; it controls how precisely the simulation measures that behavior, and it is a budget the analyst sets, not a feature of the data. A performance measure such as bias or coverage is a functional of the estimator’s sampling distribution at a fixed \(n\), hence a fixed but unknown number; here a functional is simply a rule that takes an entire distribution as its input and returns a single number, the way a mean or a variance summarizes a distribution, and the sampling distribution in question, written \(F_n\) and defined precisely in Definition 29.2 below, is what the average estimates; the simulation estimates it by a Monte Carlo average over the \(R\) replications, an estimate that carries its own sampling error. Confusing the two asymptotics is the cardinal sin of simulation practice: reporting a bias to four decimal places from \(R=100\) replications, or declaring two methods’ coverages different when the difference is within Monte Carlo noise. We will be scrupulous about keeping them apart, and the recurring discipline is to attach to every reported performance number a Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) that quantifies the \(R\)-driven uncertainty.

Remark. A running clinical example. To keep the symbols attached to something concrete, we illustrate every performance measure on one indirect comparison, returned to at each step and worked in full in Section 29.10. A health-technology-assessment team must compare a new interleukin-23 inhibitor \(A\) against an established interleukin-17 inhibitor \(B\) for moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. No head-to-head trial exists; instead an index trial compares \(A\) against ustekinumab \(C\) with full individual patient data, while a published trial compares \(B\) against the same ustekinumab \(C\) but reports only arm means. The common comparator \(C\) anchors the comparison (the anchored setting of Chapter 17). The endpoint is the reduction in the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score from baseline to week 16, measured in points, so the natural scale is the identity (mean-difference) scale. Two baseline covariates matter: an effect modifier \(x_1\) (baseline disease severity, which changes how large the treatment benefit is) and a purely prognostic covariate \(x_2\) (age, which shifts outcomes but not the benefit). The target estimand is the marginal \(A\)-versus-\(B\) mean difference in the comparator trial’s population, \(\theta=d_{AB(\mathcal{P}_{BC})}\). All numbers used below are illustrative, chosen for arithmetic transparency, not taken from real trials. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The two sample sizes of a simulation, and the two limits they govern, can be laid side by side. The columns are independent: a large \(R\) cannot cure a small-\(n\) deficiency, and a large \(n\) cannot excuse too few replications.

The plan is as follows. Section 29.2 sets out the ADEMP framework of Morris et al. (2019), the now-standard checklist (aims, data-generating mechanism, estimands, methods, performance measures) that disciplines the design and reporting of a simulation study, and formalizes a simulation study as a triple of a sampling distribution, a family of performance functionals, and their Monte Carlo estimators (Definition 29.1, catalogue K.1). Section 29.3 treats bias and its Monte Carlo standard error, proving that the Monte Carlo bias estimator is exactly unbiased for the true bias with a variance that shrinks as \(1/R\) (Definition 29.3, K.2). Section 29.4 develops the empirical and model-based standard errors and interval coverage, proving the unbiasedness of the empirical variance and the binomial law of the coverage estimator (Definition 29.4, K.3). Section 29.5 proves the mean-squared-error decomposition into squared bias plus variance, both as a population identity and as an exact finite-sample identity for the Monte Carlo estimators (Theorem 29.1, K.4). Section 29.6 is the analytic heart of the chapter: it derives the limiting coverage of a Wald interval as an explicit function of a single standardized asymptotic bias parameter, showing that coverage is nominal exactly when the method is asymptotically unbiased and that under a fixed specification bias coverage collapses to zero as \(n\to\infty\), so that more data makes a biased method’s intervals worse (Theorem 29.2, K.5). Section 29.7 treats Type I error and power, which turn out to be the coverage theorem read through the duality of tests and intervals (Definition 29.5, K.6). Section 29.8 anchors the variance comparisons of a simulation in the Cramér-Rao bound (Theorem 5.7), the theoretical floor below which no regular method’s empirical standard error can fall. Section 29.9 states, as precisely as an empirical finding can be stated, the main robustness conclusion of the Phillippo (2019) simulation study and its unanchored extension (Proposition 29.2, K.7), tying each ranked claim either to a theorem proved earlier or to the cited simulation evidence. Section 29.10 carries a complete miniature simulation from its ADEMP specification through every performance computation by hand. This chapter owns catalogue results K.1 to K.7.

Throughout, \(\theta\) denotes the scalar estimand (a relative effect \(d_{ab(\mathcal{P})}\) on a stated scale, in the applications of interest), \(\hat\theta_n\) a method’s estimator computed from a dataset of size \(n\), \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) the standard error that method reports for it, and \(I_n\) the associated nominal \((1-\alpha)\) interval. A simulation produces \(R\) independent replicate values \(\hat\theta^{(1)}_n,\dots,\hat\theta^{(R)}_n\), with \(\bar{\hat\theta}=R^{-1}\sum_{r}\hat\theta^{(r)}_n\) their average. We write \(z_{\alpha/2}\) for the upper \(\alpha/2\) standard-normal quantile, so that \(\Phi(z_{\alpha/2})=1-\alpha/2\) and \(P(|Z|\le z_{\alpha/2})=1-\alpha\) for \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\); for the usual \(\alpha=0.05\), \(z_{\alpha/2}=1.96\). The asymptotic toolkit is the one assembled in Chapter 5: the weak law of large numbers, the central limit theorem, Slutsky’s theorem (Theorem 5.2), the continuous mapping theorem (Theorem 5.1), and the delta method (Theorem 5.3).

The chapter uses a compact vocabulary of performance quantities. The following glossary collects them; the single most important distinction, stressed throughout, is between the two kinds of standard error, the one a method reports for a single analysis and the one the simulation reports for its own summary.

Confusing \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\), the uncertainty in one estimate, with \(\mathrm{MCSE}\), the uncertainty in the reported bias, coverage, or mean squared error, is a frequent novice error; the two answer different questions and have different remedies.

29.2 The ADEMP framework

A simulation study can be run badly in many ways: by simulating from a mechanism that no one can reconstruct, by reporting an estimand the methods were never trying to estimate, by summarizing with a statistic that hides the behavior of interest. Morris et al. (2019) distilled good practice into a five-part specification whose initials spell ADEMP. We adopt it as the definitional backbone of the chapter and then attach to it the probabilistic objects that the performance measures will be functionals of. The value of the checklist is that each letter forces one decision into the open where it can be criticized: the aims stop a study from being a fishing expedition, the data-generating mechanism makes the simulated world reproducible, the estimand fixes what “the truth” even is before any method is judged against it, the methods list what is being compared, and the performance measures decide what “good” means. A simulation that skips any one of these can silently answer the wrong question.

Definition 29.1 (The ADEMP framework for a simulation study) A simulation study is specified by five components.

  1. Aims (A). The scientific questions to be answered, stated before any data are simulated: for example, “quantify the bias of MAIC for the marginal \(A\)-versus-\(B\) log-odds-ratio when a binary effect modifier is omitted, as a function of its imbalance between trials.”

  2. Data-generating mechanism (DGM, D). A fully specified probability model \(\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_n\) from which datasets of size \(n\) are drawn: the covariate distribution, the treatment assignment, the outcome model with all its parameters, and any features (overlap, effect-modification strength) that the aims require to be varied across scenarios. A study comprises a finite set of DGMs, one per scenario.

  3. Estimands (E). The target quantities, defined as functionals \(\theta=\theta(\mathbb{P})\) of the DGM. In the population-adjustment setting \(\theta\) is a marginal relative effect \(d_{ab(\mathcal{P})}\) in a named target population \(\mathcal{P}\) on a named scale \(g\); because the DGM is known, \(\theta\) is known exactly, by analytic calculation or by a large auxiliary simulation.

  4. Methods (M). The estimation procedures under comparison, each a measurable map from a dataset to a triple \((\hat\theta_n,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n,I_n)\) of a point estimate, a standard error, and a nominal \((1-\alpha)\) interval. In this book the methods are the naive Bucher comparison (Theorem 14.1), MAIC (Chapter 18), simulated treatment comparison (Chapter 19), and multilevel network meta-regression (Chapter 20), with their unanchored counterparts (Chapters 17, 26).

  5. Performance measures (P). The summaries of a method’s behavior across replications: bias, empirical and model-based standard error, mean squared error, interval coverage, and, where the aim is testing, Type I error and power. Each is a functional \(\mu(F_n)\) of the sampling distribution \(F_n\) of \((\hat\theta_n,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n,I_n)\) under the DGM at fixed \(n\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The following table instantiates the five components for the running psoriasis comparison, so the abstract checklist has a concrete referent before we turn it into probability.

ADEMP component The running psoriasis comparison

Which pieces are which is worth stressing, because a novice designing a simulation must know what to fix and what to vary. The covariate distributions and the outcome model are chosen by the analyst to resemble the real trials; the between-trial imbalance and the effect-modification strength are varied across scenarios, where each scenario is one cell of the design grid, that is, one combination of overlap, effect-modifier imbalance, effect-modification strength, and sample size; the trial sizes are set to mimic the real dossier; and the estimand is derived from the chosen model, not observed. Only the performance measures are outputs. A method, stripped of the formal language of measurable maps, is in plain terms a computer program that takes one simulated trial dataset and returns three things: a point estimate, a standard error, and a confidence interval.

Remark. How the truth is known, and how it is computed. In a real analysis the target effect \(\theta\) is unknown, which is the entire difficulty; in a simulation the analyst chose the data-generating mechanism, so \(\theta=\theta(\mathbb{P})\) is determined and can be computed to any desired accuracy. Computing it is a marginalization, not a guess. On the identity (mean-difference) scale of the running example, the marginal estimand is the average of the conditional treatment effect over the target population’s covariate distribution, \[ \theta=\int \tau(x)\,f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\,dx,\qquad \tau(x)=\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x), \] which for a linear outcome model reduces to evaluating the conditional effect at the covariate mean. On a non-collapsible scale (Definition 7.7), such as a marginal log-odds-ratio, one cannot average the conditional log-odds-ratios: the truth must be obtained by marginal standardization, integrating the conditional outcome probabilities over \(f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\) first, forming the marginal event probabilities under each treatment, and only then taking the contrast. When the integral has no closed form (a survival estimand, a probit-normal average), the analyst computes \(\theta\) by a large auxiliary simulation, drawing an enormous sample from the known mechanism and reading off the marginal effect; this auxiliary draw has nothing to do with the \(R\) replications and is made once, as accurately as compute allows. The lesson is that even with the model fully known, “the truth” for a marginal, non-collapsible estimand is an integral, which is exactly why population adjustment is subtle. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The fourth and fifth components hide the structure that makes the chapter mathematical. Fix a scenario (a DGM) and a method. Applying the method to a dataset of size \(n\) yields a random triple whose law, written \(F_n\), is the sampling distribution. Every performance measure is a deterministic functional of \(F_n\), hence a fixed number once \(n\) is fixed; what the simulation reports is a Monte Carlo estimate of that number.

Definition 29.2 (Performance measures as functionals; the two sample sizes) Let \(F_n\) be the sampling distribution of \((\hat\theta_n,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n,I_n)\) under a fixed DGM, and let \(\theta\) be the estimand. The performance measures are the functionals \[ \mathrm{Bias}_n=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]-\theta,\quad \mathrm{Var}_n=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_n),\quad \mathrm{MSE}_n=\mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2],\quad \mathrm{Cov}_n=P(\theta\in I_n), \tag{29.1}\] all expectations and probabilities taken under \(F_n\). A Monte Carlo experiment draws \(R\) independent datasets from the DGM, producing independent and identically distributed (iid) replicate triples \((\hat\theta^{(r)}_n,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{(r)}_n,I^{(r)}_n)\), \(r=1,\dots,R\), each with law \(F_n\); the performance measures are estimated by their sample analogues over these \(R\) replicates. Two asymptotic regimes act on the experiment: the statistical limit \(n\to\infty\), which governs the functionals Equation 29.1 themselves and is the subject of the consistency theorems of earlier chapters; and the Monte Carlo limit \(R\to\infty\), which governs the precision with which the sample analogues estimate Equation 29.1. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Read \(F_n\) as the joint distribution of everything one run of a method produces on a dataset of size \(n\): the estimate, the standard error it reports, and the interval it forms. It is a distribution over a triple, not a single number, though most performance measures use only one margin of it, such as the spread of \(\hat\theta_n\). At a fixed \(n\) this artificial world has a definite true bias and a definite true coverage; we simply do not know them, because we cannot evaluate the distribution in closed form. So we do the next best thing: we rebuild the artificial world \(R\) times and average. Every performance number in this chapter is such an average, and every one therefore comes with a Monte Carlo standard error measuring how much the average would wobble if we reran the \(R\) replications. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why the two-sample-size view matters. A consistency theorem controls \(\mathrm{Bias}_n\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\); it says nothing about how well \(R\) replications measure \(\mathrm{Bias}_n\) at a fixed, practically relevant \(n\). Conversely, no number of replications can reveal small-sample bias that the chosen \(n\) does not exhibit: pushing \(R\to\infty\) at fixed \(n\) converges the sample analogues to the exact functionals Equation 29.1 at that \(n\), not to their \(n\to\infty\) limits. The analyst chooses the grid of \(n\) values to span the regime of interest and chooses \(R\) large enough that Monte Carlo error does not swamp the differences between methods. The performance measures below are designed so that each comes with an MCSE quantifying exactly the second source of error. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The remaining sections instantiate the fifth ADEMP component, one performance measure at a time, proving in each case (i) what functional of \(F_n\) the measure targets, (ii) that the Monte Carlo estimator is unbiased or consistent for it, and (iii) the size of its Monte Carlo standard error. This corresponds to K.1 in the proof catalogue.

Remark. Numbers to hang the symbols on. It helps to carry one arithmetic instance through the definitions that follow. In the running example the true effect is \(\theta=1.5\) PASI points. Imagine five simulated trials of the adjusted method returning estimates \(0.9,0.9,1.4,1.9,1.9\) points. Their average is \(1.4\), so the estimated bias is \(1.4-1.5=-0.1\) point; their spread gives an empirical standard error of \(0.5\); and if three of the five \(95\%\) intervals contain \(1.5\), the estimated coverage is \(3/5=0.6\). Every definition below turns one of these back-of-envelope numbers into a general formula, and Section 29.10 works the same instance in full, including why five replications are far too few to trust any of these numbers. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.3 Bias and its Monte Carlo standard error

Bias is the systematic component of an estimator’s error: the gap between where its estimates cluster on average and the truth. Its Monte Carlo estimator and the precision of that estimator are the content of catalogue result K.2.

Definition 29.3 (Monte Carlo bias estimator, relative bias, and Monte Carlo standard error) With replicate estimates \(\hat\theta^{(1)}_n,\dots,\hat\theta^{(R)}_n\) and known estimand \(\theta\), the Monte Carlo bias estimator is \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}=\bar{\hat\theta}-\theta=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\theta, \tag{29.2}\] and, when \(\theta\neq 0\), the relative bias is \(\widehat{\mathrm{RelBias}}=\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}/|\theta|\). For any Monte Carlo performance estimator \(\hat\mu\) targeting a functional \(\mu\), its Monte Carlo standard error is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution over the \(R\) replications, \(\mathrm{MCSE}(\hat\mu)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu)}\). In particular the Monte Carlo standard error of the bias estimator is \[ \mathrm{MCSE}(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}})=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Var}_n}{R}}, \tag{29.3}\] estimated in practice by \(\mathrm{EmpSE}/\sqrt{R}\) with \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\) the empirical standard error of Definition 29.4 below. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The defining properties of the bias estimator are that it is exactly unbiased for the true bias at finite \(R\) and that its uncertainty is governed by Equation 29.3. We prove both. Unbiasedness matters because it certifies that the number we headline as “the bias” is not itself systematically off: were the bias estimator biased, our headline error would rest on a biased foundation that more replications could not repair. What replications do shrink is the random wobble, at the \(1/\sqrt{R}\) rate of Equation 29.3.

Lemma 29.1 (Exact unbiasedness and variance of the Monte Carlo bias estimator) Suppose \(\mathrm{Var}_n=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_n)<\infty\). Then for every \(R\ge 1\) the Monte Carlo bias estimator Equation 29.2 satisfies \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\big]=\mathrm{Bias}_n, \qquad \mathrm{Var}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\big)=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_n}{R}, \tag{29.4}\] so \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\) is unbiased for the true bias \(\mathrm{Bias}_n\), its standard deviation is the Monte Carlo standard error Equation 29.3, and \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{Bias}_n\) as \(R\to\infty\).

Proof. The replicates \(\hat\theta^{(1)}_n,\dots,\hat\theta^{(R)}_n\) are iid with mean \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]=\theta+\mathrm{Bias}_n\) and variance \(\mathrm{Var}_n\). By linearity of expectation, \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\big] =\mathbb{E}\Big[\tfrac1R\textstyle\sum_r\hat\theta^{(r)}_n\Big]-\theta =\tfrac1R\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta^{(r)}_n]-\theta =\big(\theta+\mathrm{Bias}_n\big)-\theta=\mathrm{Bias}_n. \] Subtracting the constant \(\theta\) does not change the variance, and the variance of an average of \(R\) iid terms each of variance \(\mathrm{Var}_n\) is \(\mathrm{Var}_n/R\) because cross-covariances vanish by independence: \[ \mathrm{Var}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\big) =\mathrm{Var}\Big(\tfrac1R\textstyle\sum_r\hat\theta^{(r)}_n\Big) =\frac{1}{R^2}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n)=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_n}{R}, \] which is the square of Equation 29.3. Finally, since \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\) has mean \(\mathrm{Bias}_n\) and variance \(\mathrm{Var}_n/R\to 0\), Chebyshev’s inequality gives \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{Bias}_n\) as \(R\to\infty\). \(\square\)

The variance formula Equation 29.4 is what makes the \(1/\sqrt{R}\) rate explicit and turns the choice of \(R\) into an arithmetic exercise.

Corollary 29.1 (Replications needed for a target bias precision) To estimate the bias with a Monte Carlo standard error no larger than a tolerance \(m>0\) requires \[ R\;\ge\;\frac{\mathrm{Var}_n}{m^{2}}. \tag{29.5}\] Equivalently, halving the Monte Carlo standard error of the bias estimate requires quadrupling \(R\).

Proof. By Lemma 29.1 the Monte Carlo standard error is \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n/R}\), which is nonincreasing in \(R\) and at most \(m\) exactly when \(\mathrm{Var}_n/R\le m^2\), that is when \(R\ge\mathrm{Var}_n/m^2\). Replacing \(m\) by \(m/2\) multiplies the right-hand side by \(4\). \(\square\)

To feel the \(1/\sqrt{R}\) rate before trusting the algebra, fix a per-replication standard deviation \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}=\sigma\) and tabulate the bias Monte Carlo standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{R}\) and the worst-case coverage Monte Carlo standard error \(1/(2\sqrt{R})\) as \(R\) grows.

\(R\) bias MCSE \(=\sigma/\sqrt{R}\) coverage MCSE \(\le 1/(2\sqrt{R})\)
\(100\) \(0.100\,\sigma\) \(0.050\)
\(1{,}000\) \(0.032\,\sigma\) \(0.016\)
\(10{,}000\) \(0.010\,\sigma\) \(0.005\)

Each tenfold increase in \(R\) buys only a threefold (\(\sqrt{10}\)) reduction in Monte Carlo error, the diminishing return that makes \(R\) in the low thousands the usual compromise: enough to resolve differences between methods, not so many that the study never finishes.

Remark. Reporting discipline. The bias and its Monte Carlo standard error must be reported together. A bias estimate of \(0.03\) with \(\mathrm{MCSE}=0.05\) is consistent with no bias at all; the same estimate with \(\mathrm{MCSE}=0.003\) is firm evidence of a small systematic error. In the population-adjustment literature, \(R\) between \(500\) and several thousand is typical; Morris et al. (2019) recommend choosing \(R\) from Equation 29.5 using a pilot estimate of \(\mathrm{Var}_n\). Relative bias is often the more interpretable summary when the estimand varies across scenarios, since a fixed absolute bias means something different for an estimand of \(0.1\) than for one of \(3\). This Monte Carlo standard error is a property of the simulation, measuring how firmly the study has pinned the bias down; it is a different object from the \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) a method reports for a single dataset, as the glossary at the start of the chapter stresses. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.4 Empirical and model-based standard error; coverage

Two notions of “the standard error” coexist in a simulation, and comparing them is the single most informative diagnostic the study produces. The empirical standard error is the actual spread of the estimates across replications, the truth about the estimator’s variability. The model-based standard error is what each method claims its variability to be, averaged across replications. When the claim matches the truth, interval coverage is correct; when it does not, coverage fails, and the direction of the failure is diagnostic. This is catalogue result K.3.

In plain terms, coverage answers a reliability question: if we could rerun the entire study many times, in what fraction of runs would the reported \(95\%\) interval actually contain the true effect? A trustworthy method answers close to \(95\%\). The two standard errors are the ingredients that determine that fraction, so we define all three objects together.

Definition 29.4 (Empirical standard error, model-based standard error, and coverage) With replicate estimates \(\hat\theta^{(1)}_n,\dots,\hat\theta^{(R)}_n\), per-replicate reported standard errors \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{(1)}_n,\dots,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{(R)}_n\), and nominal \((1-\alpha)\) intervals \(I^{(r)}_n=[\hat L^{(r)},\hat U^{(r)}]\):

  • the empirical standard error is the sample standard deviation of the estimates, \[ \mathrm{EmpSE}=\sqrt{S^2},\qquad S^2=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\big(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta}\big)^2; \tag{29.6}\]

  • the model-based standard error is the average of the reported standard errors, \[ \mathrm{ModSE}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{(r)}_n; \tag{29.7}\]

  • the coverage is the proportion of intervals that contain the estimand, \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbf{1}\{\hat L^{(r)}\le\theta\le\hat U^{(r)}\}. \tag{29.8}\] The target functionals are \(\mathrm{Var}_n\) for \(\mathrm{EmpSE}^2\), the average reported variance for \(\mathrm{ModSE}\), and the true coverage \(\mathrm{Cov}_n=P(\theta\in I_n)\) for \(\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The empirical standard error squared is an honest estimate of the estimator’s variance: the familiar \((R-1)\) denominator makes it exactly unbiased.

Lemma 29.2 (Unbiasedness of the empirical variance and the Monte Carlo error of the empirical standard error) Suppose \(\mathrm{Var}_n<\infty\). Then \(S^2\) of Equation 29.6 satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[S^2]=\mathrm{Var}_n\), so \(S^2\) is unbiased for the sampling variance, and \(S^2\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{Var}_n\) as \(R\to\infty\). The empirical standard error \(\mathrm{EmpSE}=\sqrt{S^2}\) is consistent for \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}\) but slightly biased downward at finite \(R\). If in addition \(\hat\theta_n\) is normally distributed under the DGM, then \[ \mathrm{MCSE}(\mathrm{EmpSE})\;\approx\;\frac{\mathrm{EmpSE}}{\sqrt{2(R-1)}}. \tag{29.9}\]

Proof. The one idea behind the \((R-1)\) denominator is that each deviation is measured from the sample mean \(\bar{\hat\theta}\), itself computed from the same replicates and therefore sitting slightly closer to the data than the unknown true mean \(\nu\) does; the squared deviations come out systematically a touch too small, and dividing by \(R-1\) instead of \(R\) corrects for the single degree of freedom spent estimating the mean. The algebra makes this exact.

Unbiasedness of \(S^2\). Write \(\nu=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]\) and expand each squared deviation about \(\nu\) rather than \(\bar{\hat\theta}\). Using \(\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})^2 =\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\nu)^2-R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)^2\) (the algebraic identity obtained by adding and subtracting \(\nu\) inside the square and noting the cross term sums to \(-2R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)^2+\cdots\); explicitly, \(\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})^2=\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\nu)^2 -2(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\nu)+R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)^2\) and \(\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\nu)=R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)\), so the middle term is \(-2R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)^2\) and the last two combine to \(-R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)^2\)), take expectations: \[ \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})^2\Big] =\sum_r\mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\nu)^2]-R\,\mathbb{E}[(\bar{\hat\theta}-\nu)^2] =R\,\mathrm{Var}_n-R\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Var}_n}{R}=(R-1)\mathrm{Var}_n, \] using \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\hat\theta})=\mathrm{Var}_n/R\) from Lemma 29.1. Dividing by \(R-1\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[S^2]=\mathrm{Var}_n\). Consistency \(S^2\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{Var}_n\) follows from the weak law of large numbers applied to \(\tfrac1R\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n)^2\) and to \(\bar{\hat\theta}\) together with the continuous mapping theorem (Theorem 5.1), since \(S^2=\frac{R}{R-1}\big(\tfrac1R\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n)^2-\bar{\hat\theta}^2\big)\).

Downward bias of \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\). The square root is strictly concave on \((0,\infty)\), so by Jensen’s inequality (Theorem 4.6), \(\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{S^2}]\le\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[S^2]}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}\), with strict inequality unless \(S^2\) is degenerate; hence \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\) underestimates \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}\) on average, the bias vanishing as \(R\to\infty\) because \(S^2\) concentrates at \(\mathrm{Var}_n\).

Monte Carlo error under normality. If the \(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n\) are iid normal with variance \(\mathrm{Var}_n\), then \((R-1)S^2/\mathrm{Var}_n\sim\chi^2_{R-1}\), whose variance is \(2(R-1)\); therefore \(\mathrm{Var}(S^2)=\big(\mathrm{Var}_n/(R-1)\big)^2\cdot 2(R-1)=2\,\mathrm{Var}_n^2/(R-1)\). Apply the delta method (Theorem 5.3) to the smooth map \(x\mapsto\sqrt{x}\) at \(x=\mathrm{Var}_n\), whose derivative is \(1/(2\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n})\): \[ \mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{EmpSE})=\mathrm{Var}(\sqrt{S^2}) \approx\Big(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}}\Big)^2\mathrm{Var}(S^2) =\frac{1}{4\,\mathrm{Var}_n}\cdot\frac{2\,\mathrm{Var}_n^2}{R-1}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_n}{2(R-1)}. \] Taking the square root and substituting the plug-in \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}\approx\mathrm{EmpSE}\) yields Equation 29.9. \(\square\)

One caveat deserves flagging next to the formula. The Monte Carlo standard error Equation 29.9 is the one performance-error formula in this chapter that assumes normality of \(\hat\theta_n\): it comes from the chi-square law of the sample variance and holds only approximately when the replicate estimates are skewed or heavy-tailed. The bias, coverage, and mean-squared-error Monte Carlo standard errors carry no such assumption.

Coverage is the proportion of a sequence of indicator variables, hence a binomial proportion, and its Monte Carlo error is exactly the binomial standard error.

Lemma 29.3 (The coverage estimator is a binomial proportion) Let \(C_r=\mathbf{1}\{\theta\in I^{(r)}_n\}\) and \(\pi=\mathrm{Cov}_n=P(\theta\in I_n)\). Then \(C_1,\dots,C_R\) are iid \(\mathrm{Ber}(\pi)\), the coverage estimator Equation 29.8 satisfies \(R\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(R,\pi)\), and \[ \mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}]=\pi, \qquad \mathrm{MCSE}(\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}})=\sqrt{\frac{\pi(1-\pi)}{R}}\;\le\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt{R}}, \tag{29.10}\] so \(\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}\) is unbiased and consistent for the true coverage \(\pi\), with Monte Carlo standard error estimated by \(\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(1-\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}})/R}\).

Proof. Each replicate produces an interval \(I^{(r)}_n\) with the law of \(I_n\), so the event \(\{\theta\in I^{(r)}_n\}\) has probability \(\pi\), and the events are independent across the independent replicates; hence \(C_r\) are iid \(\mathrm{Ber}(\pi)\) and their sum is \(\mathrm{Bin}(R,\pi)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}]=\tfrac1R\sum_r\mathbb{E}[C_r]=\pi\), and by independence \(\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}})=\tfrac1{R^2}\sum_r\mathrm{Var}(C_r)=\tfrac1{R^2}\cdot R\,\pi(1-\pi) =\pi(1-\pi)/R\), whose square root is Equation 29.10. The bound \(\pi(1-\pi)\le 1/4\) holds because \(\pi(1-\pi)\) is maximized at \(\pi=1/2\), giving \(\mathrm{MCSE}\le 1/(2\sqrt R)\). Consistency is the weak law of large numbers for the iid bounded variables \(C_r\). \(\square\)

Corollary 29.2 (Replications needed for a target coverage precision) To estimate coverage with Monte Carlo standard error at most \(m\) for any true coverage requires at most \(R\ge 1/(4m^2)\) replications; near the nominal \(\pi=0.95\) it suffices that \(R\ge 0.95\cdot 0.05/m^2\). To resolve coverage to within a Monte Carlo standard error of \(m=0.0066\) at \(\pi=0.95\) thus requires \(R\approx 1090\), the source of the common “use at least a thousand replications” recommendation.

Proof. By Lemma 29.3 the worst-case Monte Carlo standard error is \(1/(2\sqrt R)\), at most \(m\) when \(R\ge 1/(4m^2)\); substituting the specific \(\pi=0.95\) into \(\sqrt{\pi(1-\pi)/R}\le m\) gives \(R\ge 0.95\cdot0.05/m^2=0.0475/m^2\), which for \(m=0.0066\) is \(0.0475/0.0066^2\approx 1090\). \(\square\)

Remark. Replication planning as a workflow. The two corollaries Corollary 29.1 and Corollary 29.2 are usually run as a four-step recipe, not as one-off formulas. (1) Run a small pilot, perhaps \(R_0=100\) replications of one representative scenario. (2) From the pilot, estimate the ingredient the target needs: the estimator’s variance \(\mathrm{Var}_n\) (via \(\mathrm{EmpSE}^2\)) if bias precision is the goal, or an anticipated coverage near \(0.95\) if interval reliability is. (3) Choose a tolerance \(m\), the largest Monte Carlo standard error you are willing to report. (4) Solve for \(R\): \(R\ge\mathrm{Var}_n/m^2\) for bias, \(R\ge\pi(1-\pi)/m^2\) for coverage. The following table shows the coverage Monte Carlo standard error near the nominal \(\pi=0.95\) at the replication counts most studies actually use.

Remark. The empirical-versus-model-based diagnostic. Coverage of a Wald interval \(\hat\theta_n\pm z_{\alpha/2}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) is governed by how the reported standard error \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\), summarized by \(\mathrm{ModSE}\), compares with the true variability summarized by \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\). Three regimes occur. When \(\mathrm{ModSE}\approx\mathrm{EmpSE}\) and the estimator is approximately unbiased, coverage is near nominal: the intervals are the right width and centered correctly. When \(\mathrm{ModSE}<\mathrm{EmpSE}\), the method understates its own uncertainty, the intervals are too narrow, and coverage falls below nominal; this is the dangerous failure, an overconfident method. When \(\mathrm{ModSE}>\mathrm{EmpSE}\), the intervals are wider than necessary and coverage exceeds nominal, an inefficient but conservative failure. For MAIC and simulated treatment comparison the relevant \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) is the sandwich standard error of Definition 5.11 and Theorem 18.9 (as a one-line refresher: the sandwich standard error corrects the naive one for the fact that MAIC’s weights are themselves estimated from the data rather than known in advance, and ignoring that extra source of variability is what makes the reported standard error too small), and a naive standard error that treats the estimated weights as fixed produces exactly the \(\mathrm{ModSE}<\mathrm{EmpSE}\) undercoverage that the sandwich correction repairs. For Bayesian methods \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) is the posterior standard deviation and \(I_n\) a credible interval, whose frequentist coverage the simulation measures. The next section shows that this diagnostic is only half the story: coverage also depends on bias, and under bias coverage can fail no matter how accurate the standard error. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.5 The mean-squared-error decomposition

Bias and variance are not competing summaries to be weighed by taste; they combine, exactly, into a single measure of total error. The mean squared error is the expected squared distance from the estimand, and it splits into a systematic part and a stochastic part with no remainder. This is catalogue result K.4 and the one genuine theorem among the performance measures. It earns the name theorem, where the other performance measures are definitions with elementary moment calculations, because it is an identity with content: it certifies that total error splits cleanly into a part a simulation can blame on noise (variance, shrinkable by a larger trial or a more efficient method) and a part it must blame on the method itself (squared bias, removable only by fixing the model), with nothing left over. Every method’s mean squared error can therefore be attributed to exactly these two causes.

Theorem 29.1 (Bias-variance decomposition of the mean squared error) For any estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) of an estimand \(\theta\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n^2]<\infty\), \[ \mathrm{MSE}_n=\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2\big]=\mathrm{Bias}_n^{\,2}+\mathrm{Var}_n, \qquad \mathrm{Bias}_n=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]-\theta,\ \ \mathrm{Var}_n=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_n). \tag{29.11}\]

Proof. Write \(\nu=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]\) and insert it inside the square by adding and subtracting: \[ (\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2=\big((\hat\theta_n-\nu)+(\nu-\theta)\big)^2 =(\hat\theta_n-\nu)^2+2(\hat\theta_n-\nu)(\nu-\theta)+(\nu-\theta)^2. \] Take expectations term by term, using that \(\nu-\theta=\mathrm{Bias}_n\) is a constant. The first term has expectation \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta_n-\nu)^2]=\mathrm{Var}_n\) by definition of the variance. The cross term has expectation \(2(\nu-\theta)\,\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n-\nu]=2(\nu-\theta)\cdot 0=0\), because \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n-\nu]=\nu-\nu=0\). The third term is the constant \((\nu-\theta)^2=\mathrm{Bias}_n^2\). Summing, \[ \mathrm{MSE}_n=\mathrm{Var}_n+0+\mathrm{Bias}_n^2, \] which is Equation 29.11. The finiteness \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n^2]<\infty\) guarantees all three expectations are finite. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters: four numbers, four questions. The decomposition is the reason a simulation reports bias, variance, mean squared error, and coverage as a set rather than picking one. Bias asks whether the method is valid, that is, whether it aims at the right target; variance asks whether it is precise, that is, how much its answers scatter; mean squared error Equation 29.11 fuses the two into a single accuracy score that lets a slightly biased but stable method beat an unbiased but volatile one; and coverage, developed next, asks whether the method’s uncertainty statements can be trusted. In an indirect comparison these map directly onto the choice between MAIC, simulated treatment comparison, and multilevel network meta-regression: the reweighting methods trade a little bias for controlled variance, the model-based methods aim to reduce both, and only the full four-number report reveals which trade the data actually reward. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The decomposition extends verbatim to vector estimands by summing over coordinates, which is the form needed whenever a method is judged on several contrasts at once.

Corollary 29.3 (Vector form of the decomposition) For a vector estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n\in\mathbb{R}^k\) of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) with finite second moments, the total mean squared error decomposes as \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n-\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2^2\big] =\|\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n)\|_2^2+\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n), \tag{29.12}\] where \(\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n)=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n]-\boldsymbol{\theta}\) and \(\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}\) is the sum of the coordinatewise variances.

Proof. Write \(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n-\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2^2=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\hat\theta_{n,j}-\theta_j)^2\) and apply Theorem 29.1 to each coordinate: the \(j\)-th term has expectation \(\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta_{n,j})^2+\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_{n,j})\). Summing over \(j\), the squared biases sum to \(\|\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n)\|_2^2\) and the variances sum to the trace of the covariance matrix, since the diagonal entries of \(\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n)\) are exactly the coordinate variances. \(\square\)

The population identity Equation 29.11 has an exact finite-sample shadow at the level of the Monte Carlo estimators, with no approximation: the empirical mean squared error decomposes into the squared empirical bias plus a multiple of the empirical variance.

Lemma 29.4 (Exact decomposition of the empirical mean squared error) Let \(\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\theta)^2\) be the Monte Carlo mean-squared-error estimator. Then, identically in the data, \[ \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}=\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}^{\,2}+\frac{R-1}{R}\,\mathrm{EmpSE}^2, \tag{29.13}\] with \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}=\bar{\hat\theta}-\theta\) and \(\mathrm{EmpSE}^2=S^2\) as above; and \(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}]=\mathrm{MSE}_n\).

Proof. Apply the same add-and-subtract step as in Theorem 29.1, but to the sample mean \(\bar{\hat\theta}\) in place of the population mean. With \(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\theta =(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})+(\bar{\hat\theta}-\theta)\), \[ \sum_{r}(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\theta)^2 =\sum_{r}(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})^2 +2(\bar{\hat\theta}-\theta)\sum_{r}(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta}) +R(\bar{\hat\theta}-\theta)^2. \] The middle sum vanishes because \(\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})=0\) by definition of the sample mean. Dividing by \(R\) and using \(\sum_r(\hat\theta^{(r)}_n-\bar{\hat\theta})^2=(R-1)S^2\) and \(\bar{\hat\theta}-\theta=\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\) gives \[ \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{(R-1)S^2}{R}+\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}^{\,2}, \] which is Equation 29.13. For the expectation, take \(\mathbb{E}\) of Equation 29.13: \(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}^2]=\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}) +(\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}})^2=\mathrm{Var}_n/R+\mathrm{Bias}_n^2\) by Lemma 29.1, and \(\mathbb{E}[\tfrac{R-1}{R}S^2]=\tfrac{R-1}{R}\mathrm{Var}_n\) by Lemma 29.2; their sum is \(\mathrm{Bias}_n^2+\mathrm{Var}_n=\mathrm{MSE}_n\) by Theorem 29.1. \(\square\)

Remark. The bias-variance trade-off in population adjustment. The decomposition Equation 29.11 is the reason a biased method can be preferred. Omitting a weakly imbalanced effect modifier from a MAIC introduces a small \(\mathrm{Bias}_n^2\) but, by retaining a larger effective sample size (Theorem 18.6), lowers \(\mathrm{Var}_n\); if the variance saving exceeds the squared bias incurred, the omission lowers the mean squared error. The minimizer of Equation 29.11 over the set of balanced moments is in general not the full set, exactly the phenomenon that the no-extrapolation limit (1) forces under poor overlap, where balancing every moment drives the effective sample size, and with it the variance term, toward catastrophe. A simulation study quantifies this trade-off scenario by scenario; the decomposition guarantees that the two contributions it reports, squared bias and variance, account for the total error with nothing left over. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.6 Coverage under correct and incorrect specification

The coverage diagnostic of Section 29.4 assumed an approximately unbiased estimator and asked whether the standard error was right. The deeper question is what happens to coverage when the estimator is biased, as it must be when a method’s assumptions are violated: an omitted effect modifier, a misspecified outcome model, a transportability assumption that does not hold. The answer, catalogue result K.5, is sharp and at first counterintuitive: a fixed specification bias drives coverage not toward some degraded constant but all the way to zero as the per-replication sample size grows. The intervals shrink around the wrong value, and more data only sharpens the error.

Remark. The phenomenon in plain words, before any formulas. Here is the whole result in one breath. Suppose a method is biased by a fixed amount: it aims at \(1.0\) PASI points when the truth is \(1.5\). As the trial grows, the method’s confidence interval shrinks, because more data makes each estimate more precise; but it shrinks around the wrong center, \(1.0\), not \(1.5\). So the true value \(1.5\) starts inside a wide early interval and is squeezed out as the interval tightens: coverage falls as \(n\) grows. This is counterintuitive because in everyday statistics more data is always better; here more data makes a biased method’s intervals worse, since precision without accuracy is confident error. The worked example computes exactly this collapse for the naive comparison in the running scenario, coverage dropping from \(0.83\) to \(0.48\) to \(0.001\) as the sample size climbs (Section 29.10). The rest of this section proves that the collapse is inevitable and computes its exact rate; a reader content with the picture can skim the analysis and rejoin at Corollary 29.4. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We make this precise by computing the limiting coverage of a Wald interval as a function of a single standardized bias. Throughout, \(\theta\) is the true estimand and \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\) is the value the method’s estimator actually converges to, the estimand under the working model (we write \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\), not \(\theta_0\), to keep it distinct from the null value \(\theta_0\) of the next section). It equals \(\theta\) when the method is correctly specified and differs from it otherwise; it is the M-estimation target \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) of Definition 5.10 transported to the present scalar setting. A concrete instance fixes the idea: when a MAIC omits an imbalanced effect modifier of strength \(\beta_1\) whose mean differs by \(\Delta\bar x_1\) between the two trials, the omitted-covariate estimator no longer converges to the target \(\theta\) but to a shifted value \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\approx\theta+\beta_1\,\Delta\bar x_1\); the gap \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta\) is exactly the omitted-effect-modifier bias of Theorem 16.1 and Theorem 20.2. Two further symbols appear below. The sequence \(\sigma_n>0\) is the estimator’s true standard deviation at sample size \(n\), a theoretical quantity the analyst never sees and which shrinks to \(0\) as \(n\) grows; \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) is the operational standard error the method reports and can compute, and condition (C2) below says the two agree in the limit. The standardized bias \(b=\lim (\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\sigma_n\) measures the fixed miss \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta\) in units of the shrinking \(\sigma_n\): if the miss is a fixed nonzero constant while \(\sigma_n\to 0\), the ratio blows up and we record \(b=\pm\infty\), the case that will destroy coverage.

First, an elementary lemma about the function that will turn out to be the limiting coverage.

Lemma 29.5 (The coverage curve) For fixed \(z>0\) define \(h:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)\) by \[ h(b)=\Phi(z-b)-\Phi(-z-b). \tag{29.14}\] Then \(h\) is even, \(h(0)=\Phi(z)-\Phi(-z)=2\Phi(z)-1\), \(h\) is strictly increasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and strictly decreasing on \((0,\infty)\) with a unique global maximum at \(b=0\), and \(h(b)\to 0\) as \(|b|\to\infty\).

Proof. Even. Using \(\Phi(-x)=1-\Phi(x)\), \(h(-b)=\Phi(z+b)-\Phi(-z+b)=\big(1-\Phi(-z-b)\big)-\big(1-\Phi(z-b)\big) =\Phi(z-b)-\Phi(-z-b)=h(b)\).

Monotonicity. Differentiate, using \(\Phi'=\phi\) and the evenness of \(\phi\): \[ h'(b)=-\phi(z-b)+\phi(-z-b)=\phi(z+b)-\phi(z-b), \] since \(\phi(-z-b)=\phi(z+b)\). For \(b>0\) compare the arguments by absolute value: \((z+b)^2-(z-b)^2=4zb>0\), so \(|z+b|>|z-b|\), and because \(\phi\) is strictly decreasing in the absolute value of its argument, \(\phi(z+b)<\phi(z-b)\), whence \(h'(b)<0\) for all \(b>0\). By evenness \(h'(b)>0\) for \(b<0\), and \(h'(0)=0\). Thus \(h\) increases up to \(b=0\) and decreases after, with a unique global maximum \(h(0)=2\Phi(z)-1\).

Limit. As \(b\to+\infty\) both \(z-b\to-\infty\) and \(-z-b\to-\infty\), so \(\Phi(z-b)\to 0\) and \(\Phi(-z-b)\to 0\), giving \(h(b)\to 0\); by evenness the same holds as \(b\to-\infty\). \(\square\)

A short table of the coverage curve \(h(b)=\Phi(z-b)-\Phi(-z-b)\) at \(z=1.96\) shows its shape at a glance: a symmetric peak of \(0.95\) at \(b=0\), decaying on both sides, so that any nonzero standardized bias costs coverage and a large one erases it.

How to read the theorem below. It computes the large-sample coverage of a Wald interval as this single function \(h(b)\) of the standardized bias \(b\). The three conditions are the fine print that makes the computation valid: (C1) the estimate is approximately normal around its own limit \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\); (C2) the reported standard error \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) is asymptotically right for the true scale \(\sigma_n\); and (C3) the standardized bias settles to a value \(b\), possibly \(\pm\infty\). The payoff is the dichotomy: coverage hits its nominal \(1-\alpha\) exactly when \(b=0\) (no bias), sits below nominal for any finite \(b\neq 0\), and collapses to \(0\) when \(b=\pm\infty\) (a fixed bias against a shrinking standard error).

Theorem 29.2 (Limiting coverage of a Wald interval under asymptotic bias) Fix \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) and let \(z=z_{\alpha/2}\). For each \(n\) let \(I_n=[\hat\theta_n-z\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n,\ \hat\theta_n+z\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n]\) with \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n>0\), and suppose there is a deterministic scale sequence \(\sigma_n>0\) with \(\sigma_n\to 0\) such that:

(C1) (asymptotic normality about the working-model target) \((\hat\theta_n-\theta_{\mathrm{work}})/\sigma_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\);

(C2) (ratio-consistent standard error) \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n/\sigma_n\xrightarrow{p}1\);

(C3) (calibrated asymptotic bias) \((\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\sigma_n\to b\) for some \(b\in[-\infty,\infty]\).

Then the coverage of the true estimand \(\theta\) satisfies \[ \mathrm{Cov}_n=P(\theta\in I_n)\ \longrightarrow\ \begin{cases} \Phi(z-b)-\Phi(-z-b), & b\in\mathbb{R},\\[2pt] 0, & b=\pm\infty. \end{cases} \tag{29.15}\] Consequently, by Lemma 29.5, the limiting coverage equals the nominal \(1-\alpha=2\Phi(z)-1\) if and only if \(b=0\), is strictly less than nominal for every finite \(b\neq 0\), and degrades to \(0\) as the standardized bias diverges.

Proof. The estimand lies in the interval exactly when the studentized statistic is within the critical values: \[ \theta\in I_n \iff |\hat\theta_n-\theta|\le z\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n \iff -z\le T_n\le z,\qquad T_n:=\frac{\hat\theta_n-\theta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}. \] Split \(T_n\) at the working-model target \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\): \[ T_n=\underbrace{\frac{\hat\theta_n-\theta_{\mathrm{work}}}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}}_{W_n} +\underbrace{\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}}_{D_n}. \] For the first term write \(W_n=\dfrac{\hat\theta_n-\theta_{\mathrm{work}}}{\sigma_n}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_n}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}\). By (C1) the first factor converges in distribution to \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), and by (C2) \(\sigma_n/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\xrightarrow{p}1\); Slutsky’s theorem (Theorem 5.2) gives \(W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\).

Finite \(b\). Write \(D_n=\dfrac{\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta}{\sigma_n}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_n}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}\). The first factor is a deterministic sequence converging to \(b\) by (C3) and the second converges in probability to \(1\) by (C2), so \(D_n\xrightarrow{p}b\). Applying Slutsky’s theorem again to \(T_n=W_n+D_n\) gives \(T_n\xrightarrow{d}Z+b\sim\mathcal{N}(b,1)\). The points \(\pm z\) are continuity points of the limiting distribution function, so \[ P(-z\le T_n\le z)\to P(-z\le Z+b\le z)=P(-z-b\le Z\le z-b)=\Phi(z-b)-\Phi(-z-b)=h(b), \] which is the first branch of Equation 29.15.

Infinite \(b\). Suppose \(b=+\infty\); the case \(-\infty\) is symmetric. Fix \(M>0\). Since \(D_n=(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) and \((\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\sigma_n\to+\infty\) while \(\sigma_n/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\xrightarrow{p}1\), we have \(D_n\xrightarrow{p}+\infty\), so \(P(D_n<M)\to 0\). In words: we force the bias term \(D_n\) to be at least a large constant \(M\); the test statistic \(T_n\) is then dragged above \(z\) unless the random part \(W_n\) compensates by being very negative (below \(z-M\)), an event of vanishing probability, so the interval almost never reaches up to \(\theta\). Formally, on the event \(\{D_n\ge M\}\) we have \(T_n\ge W_n+M\), hence \(\{T_n\le z\}\subseteq \{W_n\le z-M\}\cup\{D_n<M\}\), and therefore \[ P(\theta\in I_n)\le P(T_n\le z)\le P(W_n\le z-M)+P(D_n<M). \] Letting \(n\to\infty\), the first term tends to \(\Phi(z-M)\) because \(W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\) and \(z-M\) is a continuity point, and the second tends to \(0\); thus \(\limsup_n P(\theta\in I_n)\le\Phi(z-M)\). As \(M>0\) was arbitrary and \(\Phi(z-M)\to 0\) as \(M\to\infty\), the limit is \(0\), the second branch of Equation 29.15.

The final consequences are Lemma 29.5: \(h(0)=2\Phi(z)-1=1-\alpha\) is the unique maximum, so finite \(b\neq 0\) gives \(h(b)<1-\alpha\), and the infinite-\(b\) branch gives \(0\). \(\square\)

The case that matters in practice is a fixed specification bias, where \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\neq\theta\) does not depend on \(n\) while \(\sigma_n\to 0\); then \(b=\pm\infty\) and coverage collapses.

Corollary 29.4 (Fixed specification bias destroys coverage) Suppose (C1) and (C2) hold and the working-model target \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\) is a fixed constant with \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}\neq\theta\). Then \((\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\sigma_n\to\pm\infty\) as \(\sigma_n\to 0\), so by Theorem 29.2 the coverage of \(\theta\) tends to \(0\): a consistently misspecified method has asymptotic coverage zero, and its undercoverage worsens with increasing \(n\).

Proof. With \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta\neq 0\) a nonzero constant and \(\sigma_n\downarrow 0\), the ratio \((\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\sigma_n\) diverges to \(+\infty\) if \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}>\theta\) and to \(-\infty\) if \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}<\theta\); either way \(b=\pm\infty\) in (C3), and the second branch of Equation 29.15 gives limiting coverage \(0\). Monotone worsening follows because the half-width \(z\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\approx z\sigma_n\) shrinks while the centering error \(|\hat\theta_n-\theta|\) stays near the fixed \(|\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta|\), so the fixed miss eventually exceeds any shrinking half-width. \(\square\)

Corollary 29.5 (Correct specification yields nominal coverage) If the method is correctly specified, so that \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}=\theta\), and (C1) and (C2) hold, then \(b=0\) and the coverage of \(\theta\) tends to the nominal level \(1-\alpha\).

Proof. Correct specification gives \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta=0\), hence \((\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta)/\sigma_n=0\to 0=b\), and the first branch of Equation 29.15 evaluates to \(h(0)=\Phi(z)-\Phi(-z)=2\Phi(z)-1=1-\alpha\) by the definition \(\Phi(z_{\alpha/2})=1-\alpha/2\). \(\square\)

Remark. Two distinct ways coverage fails, and why bias is the worse one. Corollary 29.5 shows correct specification with an accurate standard error gives nominal coverage. The diagnostic of Section 29.4 concerns failures of (C2): if \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\) is biased so that \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n/\sigma_n\xrightarrow{p}c\neq 1\), the studentized limit is \(\mathcal{N}(0,1/c^2)\) and coverage tends to \(2\Phi(cz)-1\), above nominal if \(c>1\) and below if \(c<1\); this is a fixed, often modest, miscalibration. Theorem 29.2 concerns failures of unbiasedness, failures of (C3) with \(b\neq 0\), and these are categorically worse: a fixed bias sends coverage to \(0\). The practical moral is that for a biased method, a larger trial or a longer Markov chain Monte Carlo run does not rescue the interval; it tightens a confidently wrong statement. In the population-adjustment setting the fixed bias \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}-\theta\) is precisely the aggregation or transitivity bias quantified earlier: the omitted effect-modifier bias of Theorem 16.1 and Theorem 20.2, or the non-collapsibility-induced scale mismatch of Definition 7.7 when contrasts are combined across populations on a non-collapsible scale. A simulation study that reports coverage as a function of \(n\) exhibits the collapse of Corollary 29.4 directly: the telltale signature of a biased method is coverage that falls as \(n\) grows, distinguishing it from a merely inefficient one whose coverage stays near nominal. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The telltale signature. Coverage that falls as \(n\) grows is the fingerprint of bias; coverage that holds near nominal as \(n\) grows is the fingerprint of a merely inefficient method. A single simulation run across a grid of sample sizes distinguishes the two at a glance, which is the practical reason coverage is tabulated against \(n\) rather than reported at one sample size. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.7 Power and Type I error

When the aim of a study is detection rather than estimation, the relevant performance measures are the Type I error rate and the power of a test. In the population-adjustment setting these are secondary, since the goal is almost always a point estimate with an interval; but they are the natural language for sample-size and design questions, and, as we show, they are governed by exactly the machinery of the previous section through the duality between tests and intervals. This is catalogue result K.6.

Definition 29.5 (Wald test, Type I error rate, and power) For testing \(H_0:\theta=\theta_0\) against \(H_1:\theta\neq\theta_0\) at nominal level \(\alpha\), the two-sided Wald test rejects \(H_0\) when \(\theta_0\) falls outside the interval \(I_n\), equivalently when \[ |T_{0,n}|>z_{\alpha/2},\qquad T_{0,n}=\frac{\hat\theta_n-\theta_0}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}. \tag{29.16}\] The Type I error rate is the rejection probability when \(H_0\) is true, \(P_{\theta=\theta_0}(\text{reject})\), whose target is the nominal level \(\alpha\). The power at a true value \(\theta_1\neq\theta_0\) is the rejection probability \(P_{\theta=\theta_1}(\text{reject})\). In a simulation each is estimated by the proportion of replications in which \(H_0\) is rejected, a binomial proportion whose Monte Carlo standard error is given by Lemma 29.3 with \(\pi\) the true rejection probability. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The asymptotic level and power follow from one theorem, which is Theorem 29.2 applied to the null value, because the test rejects exactly when the interval misses \(\theta_0\).

Remark. How to read the power theorem, and why an ITC analyst should care. The single input is the standardized signal \(\Delta_n=(\theta-\theta_0)/\sigma_n\): the true departure from the hypothesized null \(\theta_0\), measured in standard-error units, the mirror image of the standardized bias \(b\) of the previous section. Part (1) says a correctly centered test rejects a true null at the intended rate \(\alpha\); part (2) gives the power at a local alternative, meaning an alternative that shrinks toward the null as \(n\) grows, \(\Delta_n\to\Delta\) finite, so that power settles at a value strictly between \(\alpha\) and \(1\) rather than racing to \(1\) (this shrinking is the device that keeps the calculation informative at every sample size); part (3) says that against any fixed alternative the test eventually rejects with certainty. An indirect-comparison analyst uses part (2) in reverse, as a sample-size rule: to detect a target effect with prescribed power, solve Equation 29.17 for the \(n\) that makes \(\sigma_n\) small enough (Exercise 29.7 carries this out numerically). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 29.3 (Asymptotic level and power of the Wald test) Let \(z=z_{\alpha/2}\) and suppose the estimator satisfies (C1) and (C2) of Theorem 29.2 with working-model target equal to the true value, \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}=\theta\), and define the standardized signal \(\Delta_n=(\theta-\theta_0)/\sigma_n\) measuring the departure from the hypothesized \(\theta_0\) in standard-error units.

  1. (Level.) If \(H_0\) is true, so \(\theta=\theta_0\) and \(\Delta_n\equiv 0\), then the Type I error rate converges to \(\alpha\): \[ P_{\theta=\theta_0}(|T_{0,n}|>z_{\alpha/2})\to P(|Z|>z_{\alpha/2})=\alpha. \]

  2. (Power, local alternatives.) If \(\Delta_n\to\Delta\in\mathbb{R}\), the power converges to \[ 1-\big(\Phi(z-\Delta)-\Phi(-z-\Delta)\big) =\Phi(\Delta-z_{\alpha/2})+\Phi(-\Delta-z_{\alpha/2}). \tag{29.17}\] For \(\Delta>0\) the second term is negligible, giving the standard one-sided approximation \(\mathrm{Power}\approx\Phi\!\big(\Delta-z_{\alpha/2}\big)=\Phi\!\big(|\theta-\theta_0|/\sigma_n-z_{\alpha/2}\big)\).

  3. (Consistency, fixed alternatives.) If \(\theta\neq\theta_0\) is fixed and \(\sigma_n\to 0\), so \(\Delta_n\to\pm\infty\), the power converges to \(1\).

Proof. By the same studentization as in Theorem 29.2, the test statistic decomposes as \[ T_{0,n}=\frac{\hat\theta_n-\theta_0}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n} =\frac{\hat\theta_n-\theta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}+\frac{\theta-\theta_0}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n} =W_n+\Delta_n\cdot\frac{\sigma_n}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n}, \] where \(W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) as shown there (using \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}=\theta\) in (C1) and (C2)) and \(\sigma_n/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\xrightarrow{p}1\).

(1) Under \(H_0\), \(\Delta_n\equiv 0\), so \(T_{0,n}=W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\); since \(\pm z_{\alpha/2}\) are continuity points, \(P(|T_{0,n}|>z_{\alpha/2})\to P(|Z|>z_{\alpha/2})=2\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2})=2(\alpha/2)=\alpha\), using \(\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2})=1-\Phi(z_{\alpha/2})=\alpha/2\).

(2) If \(\Delta_n\to\Delta\) finite, then \(\Delta_n\cdot\sigma_n/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n\xrightarrow{p}\Delta\) by Slutsky, and \(T_{0,n}\xrightarrow{d}Z+\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(\Delta,1)\). Hence the acceptance probability \(P(-z\le T_{0,n}\le z)\to\Phi(z-\Delta)-\Phi(-z-\Delta)=h(\Delta)\) as in Theorem 29.2, and the power is \(1-h(\Delta)\). Rewriting with \(1-\Phi(z-\Delta)=\Phi(\Delta-z)\) and \(\Phi(-z-\Delta)=\Phi(-\Delta-z)\) gives Equation 29.17. For \(\Delta>0\) the term \(\Phi(-\Delta-z)\) is the upper tail beyond \(\Delta+z>0\) and is small, leaving the one-sided approximation.

(3) If \(\theta\neq\theta_0\) is fixed and \(\sigma_n\to 0\), then \(|\Delta_n|=|\theta-\theta_0|/\sigma_n\to\infty\), so \(h(\Delta_n)\to 0\) by the divergence branch of Lemma 29.5 (made rigorous by the same bounding argument as in Theorem 29.2), and the power \(1-h(\Delta_n)\to 1\). \(\square\)

Remark. Tests are intervals turned inside out. Part (1) is Corollary 29.5 read at the null: the event \(\{\theta_0\notin I_n\}\) has limiting probability \(1-(1-\alpha)=\alpha\), which is the Type I error. Part (2) is Theorem 29.2 read with \(\theta_0\) playing the role of the “true” value and \(\theta\) the role of the working-model target, so that the noncentrality \(\Delta\) is the bias parameter \(b\) with a sign change. The power function Equation 29.17 evaluated at \(\Delta=0\) returns \(2\Phi(-z_{\alpha/2})=\alpha\), recovering the level, as it must. This duality means a simulation that reports interval coverage already contains the Type I error information; power requires only re-centering the test at a hypothesized null. Population-adjustment simulations report power chiefly to inform the sample size a future head-to-head or single-arm study would need, with \(\Delta\) supplied by a target effect size and \(\sigma_n\) by the method’s standard error at the planned \(n\). For a concrete instance in the running example: to detect a true \(1.0\)-point PASI advantage at \(80\%\) power one needs \(\Delta=z_{0.025}+z_{0.20}=1.96+0.84=2.80\), hence a design reaching a sample size at which the method’s standard error has fallen to \(\sigma_n=1.0/2.80=0.36\) points; the method’s standard-error-versus-\(n\) curve then reads off the required \(n\), and a less efficient method, needing the same \(\sigma_n=0.36\) from a larger \(n\), is precisely the one a precision simulation flags. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.8 The efficiency baseline

A simulation that ranks methods by empirical standard error is estimating their relative efficiency, and relative efficiency has a theoretical floor. The Cramér-Rao bound (Theorem 5.7) fixes the smallest variance any regular estimator can achieve; the empirical standard error of the best possible method approaches that floor, and the empirical standard error of any competitor measures its distance above it. Anchoring the variance comparisons of a simulation to this baseline is what separates a finding that one method is “more precise” from a finding that it is “near-optimal.”

Remark. What the baseline buys, in plain terms. A precision ranking answers “is method X more precise than method Y.” The Cramér-Rao floor answers a more useful question: “is method X close to the best precision any reasonable method could achieve, or is there room to do better?” Two pieces of fine print make the statement rigorous, and a reader who wants only the conclusion may skip them. A regular model and estimator (Definition 5.6, Definition 5.9) are ones without pathological tricks: regularity excludes the super-efficient estimators that beat the bound only at isolated parameter values by exploiting discontinuous behavior, so the bound is a genuine floor for the methods a practitioner would use. Uniform integrability of \(\{n(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2\}_n\) is the technical condition that lets us pass from convergence of the distribution to convergence of the variance; concretely it rules out heavy tails, the situation where a rare enormous estimate, such as a single dominating MAIC weight under near-failing overlap, inflates the true variance far above the nominal asymptotic value. The conclusion, stripped of fine print, is that the empirical standard error estimates \(\sqrt{V(\theta)/n}\), and \(V(\theta)\) can never fall below the information floor \(\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proposition 29.1 (Empirical standard error estimates the asymptotic variance, bounded below by the Cramér-Rao floor) Let \(\hat\theta_n\) be an estimator of a scalar \(\theta\) in a regular parametric model (Definition 5.6) with per-observation Fisher information \(\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)>0\), and suppose \(\sqrt{n}\,(\hat\theta_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,V(\theta))\) with \(\{n(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2\}_n\) uniformly integrable.

  1. For each fixed \(n\), the empirical variance \(S^2\) from \(R\) iid replications satisfies \(S^2\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{Var}_n\) as \(R\to\infty\), and \(n\,\mathrm{Var}_n\to V(\theta)\) as \(n\to\infty\); hence \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\) estimates \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_n}\approx\sqrt{V(\theta)/n}\).

  2. If \(\hat\theta_n\) is regular and asymptotically unbiased, then \[ V(\theta)\ \ge\ \mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}, \tag{29.18}\] with equality if and only if \(\hat\theta_n\) is asymptotically efficient (Definition 5.9, Corollary 5.2). Consequently the empirical standard error of any regular method satisfies, to first order, \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\gtrsim\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}/n}\), the efficiency baseline, and the empirical relative efficiency \(\mathrm{EmpSE}_2^2/\mathrm{EmpSE}_1^2\) of two methods estimates the ratio \(V_2(\theta)/V_1(\theta)\) of their asymptotic variances.

Proof. (1) Sample-variance consistency is Lemma 29.2: \(S^2\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{Var}_n\) as \(R\to\infty\). For the second limit, convergence in distribution of \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n-\theta)\) to \(\mathcal{N}(0,V(\theta))\) together with uniform integrability of the squares \(\{n(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2\}_n\) upgrades to convergence of second moments, \(n\,\mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2]\to V(\theta)\) (uniform integrability plus convergence in distribution implies convergence of the corresponding expectations; see Billingsley (1995)). Asymptotic unbiasedness, which the same uniform integrability gives via convergence of first moments \(\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{Bias}_n\to 0\), yields \(n\,\mathrm{Bias}_n^2\to 0\), so by Theorem 29.1 \(n\,\mathrm{Var}_n=n\,\mathrm{MSE}_n-n\,\mathrm{Bias}_n^2\to V(\theta)-0=V(\theta)\).

(2) The information for \(n\) iid observations is \(n\,\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)\) by additivity of Fisher information over independent samples. For an exactly unbiased estimator the Cramér-Rao inequality (Theorem 5.7) gives \(\mathrm{Var}_n\ge (n\,\mathcal{I}_1(\theta))^{-1}\), hence \(n\,\mathrm{Var}_n\ge\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}\); letting \(n\to\infty\) through part (1), \(V(\theta)\ge\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}\). The statement that no regular, asymptotically unbiased estimator can have asymptotic variance below \(\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}\), with equality characterizing asymptotic efficiency, is the local-asymptotic-minimax and convolution theory cited in Chapter 5 following Definition 5.9 and realized by the maximum likelihood estimator in Corollary 5.2; we invoke it here rather than reprove it. The relative-efficiency claim is then immediate: by part (1), \(\mathrm{EmpSE}_k^2\approx V_k(\theta)/n\) for method \(k\), so the ratio estimates \(V_2(\theta)/V_1(\theta)\). \(\square\)

Remark. What a precision ranking means. When a simulation reports that multilevel network meta-regression has a smaller empirical standard error than MAIC under good overlap, Proposition 29.1 says it is estimating a ratio \(V_{\mathrm{ML\text{-}NMR}}/V_{\mathrm{MAIC}}<1\) of asymptotic variances, both bounded below by the same Cramér-Rao floor \(\mathcal{I}_1(\theta)^{-1}\). The reweighting methods pay for covariate balance with the variance inflation factor \(n/\mathrm{ESS}\) of Theorem 18.6 and the variance-inflation theorem of Chapter 18, lifting their \(V(\theta)\) above the floor; a likelihood-based method that models the outcome can sit closer to it. A simulation thus turns the abstract efficiency ordering into measured numbers, with the bound certifying how much room for improvement remains. The uniform-integrability hypothesis is not a formality: an estimator with occasional extreme values, such as MAIC under near-failing overlap where a single weight dominates, can have a finite-sample variance far larger than its nominal asymptotic variance, and the simulation will, correctly, report the large empirical standard error. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.9 The empirical robustness finding

We now state the principal conclusion of the simulation literature on population adjustment, the synthesis of the Phillippo (2019) study and its successors. It is an empirical statement: it summarizes measured bias and coverage across a designed grid of data-generating mechanisms, not a theorem with a proof. We label it a proposition to record it precisely and to tie each of its claims either to a theorem proved earlier in the book or to the cited simulation evidence, but its warrant is the simulation, and different data-generating mechanisms could in principle reorder it. This is catalogue result K.7.

Proposition 29.2 (Empirical robustness ordering of population-adjustment methods) Across data-generating mechanisms with correlated baseline covariates, varying covariate overlap between the index and comparator populations, varying effect-modification strength, and varying inclusion or omission of effect modifiers, the Phillippo (2019) simulation study and its replications and extensions (Chandler and Ishak (2026)) find the following qualitative ordering, on the relative-effect scale of interest, for the anchored comparison.

  1. Multilevel network meta-regression with all effect modifiers attains low bias and near-nominal coverage when the effect-modifier specification is correct and overlap is adequate; it is the most robust method studied. Its consistency is the aggregation theorem Theorem 20.1, and it avoids the ecological/aggregation bias Theorem 20.2 by integrating the individual-level model rather than matching means.

  2. Simulated treatment comparison with all effect modifiers and a correctly specified outcome model attains low bias comparable to multilevel network meta-regression for pairwise comparisons; under a correct linear model it is asymptotically equivalent to MAIC (Theorem 19.7), and it can interpolate where MAIC cannot.

  3. MAIC with all effect modifiers and adequate overlap attains low bias, at the cost of somewhat higher variance than the model-based methods, the variance inflation being the \(n/\mathrm{ESS}\) factor of Theorem 18.6 and Chapter 18.

  4. MAIC with all effect modifiers but poor overlap has high variance and can be substantially biased, and may fail outright as the effective sample size collapses toward zero, the no-extrapolation limit

  1. Any method that omits an imbalanced effect modifier incurs substantial bias, increasing with the omitted modifier’s interaction strength and its imbalance across populations; by Corollary 29.4 this fixed bias also drives interval coverage toward zero as \(n\) grows. This is the transitivity failure Theorem 16.1 and the ecological bias Theorem 20.2 seen through the performance measures.

  2. The unadjusted Bucher comparison exhibits the largest bias whenever effect modifiers are distributed differently across populations, being the \(b=\pm\infty\) extreme of Theorem 29.2; it is unbiased only under transitivity (Theorem 14.1), which population adjustment exists to repair.

For the unanchored comparison, where no common comparator anchors the contrast (Chapters 17, 26), the ordering is shorter but the demands are stiffer.

  1. Multilevel unanchored meta-regression under a correct shared-prognostic-factor assumption (Definition 17.6, in its absolute form Definition 17.5) attains near-nominal coverage and low bias. The residual bias concentrates in scenarios where strong effect modification acts in the index or target population.

  2. The same method with the assumption relaxed by subgroup aggregate data restores near-nominal coverage under any effect-modification strength, at the price of requiring that extra subgroup information.

  3. All unanchored methods remain far more demanding than their anchored counterparts, because dropping the common comparator forfeits the cancellation that makes an anchored contrast robust. This is the content of Theorem 17.2 and of the insufficiency of the shared-effect-modifier assumption alone (Theorem 12.6).

Remark. Empirical, but not arbitrary. Each ranked claim above is anchored to a theorem of the preceding chapters: items 1 to 3 to consistency and equivalence results, items 4 to 6 to the limitation theorems and to the coverage-collapse corollary Corollary 29.4 of this chapter. What the simulation adds is the finite-sample magnitude and the relative ordering, which no theorem supplies: how much variance MAIC pays at a given overlap, how large the omitted-modifier bias is at a given imbalance, at what sample size coverage has already collapsed. The proposition is robust in the sense that independent simulation studies reproduce the ordering, but it is contingent on the designed mechanisms; an application whose covariates, overlap, or scale differ markedly from the studied grid should verify the relevant scenarios for itself, which is precisely what the ADEMP framework of Definition 29.1 equips it to do. The practical reading is unchanged across studies: when individual patient data are available and overlap is adequate, prefer the model-based methods, multilevel network meta-regression for anchored and multilevel unanchored meta-regression for unanchored comparisons, reserving MAIC for settings where its assumptions are mild and its effective sample size healthy, and treating the unadjusted comparison as the bias-prone baseline it is. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Practice, in one rule. When individual patient data are available and overlap is healthy, prefer the model-based methods: multilevel network meta-regression for anchored comparisons and multilevel unanchored meta-regression for unanchored ones. Reserve MAIC for settings where its assumptions are mild and its effective sample size stays healthy. Treat the unadjusted Bucher comparison as the bias-prone baseline against which the others are judged, never as the answer when effect modifiers are imbalanced. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.10 A worked miniature simulation

We carry a complete simulation through its ADEMP specification and every performance computation, choosing the numbers so that each performance measure and its Monte Carlo standard error can be checked by hand, and so that the coverage-collapse theorem can be evaluated against a standard normal table.

Example 29.1 (An anchored simulation comparing an adjusted method with the naive comparison) We reuse the data-generating geometry of the worked MAIC example (Example 18.3), now wrapped in the ADEMP framework and pushed through the performance measures. Read concretely, this is the running plaque-psoriasis comparison of Section 29.1: \(A\) a new interleukin-23 inhibitor, \(B\) an established comparator biologic, \(C\) ustekinumab as the common anchor, the effect modifier \(x_1\) baseline disease severity, the prognostic covariate \(x_2\) age, and the outcome the reduction in PASI score at week 16 in points, so every number below is a PASI mean difference.

Aims (A). Quantify, on the identity scale, the bias and ninety-five percent interval coverage of two methods for the target contrast \(d_{AB(\mathcal{P}_{BC})}\): an adjusted method (MAIC or simulated treatment comparison) and the unadjusted Bucher comparison, under an imbalanced effect modifier.

Data-generating mechanism (D). An index trial \(AC\) with one effect modifier \(x_1\) and one prognostic covariate \(x_2\), covariate mean \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{AC}=(0,0)\); a comparator trial \(BC\) reporting covariate mean \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{BC}=(0.5,0.5)\) and a \(B\)-versus-\(C\) benefit \(d_{BC}=3.0\). The conditional benefit of \(A\) over \(C\) is \(\mu_C(\mathbf{x})-\mu_A(\mathbf{x})=4+x_1\), depending on the effect modifier \(x_1\) but not on \(x_2\). Each replication draws \(n\) patients per arm with independent mean-zero outcome noise of variance making the per-replication sampling standard deviation of each method’s estimator equal to \(\sigma_n\).

Anatomy of one replication. Concretely, one replication of this mechanism does the following. Draw \(n\) patients for the \(A\) arm and \(n\) for the \(C\) arm of the index trial; for each patient draw baseline severity \(x_1\) and age \(x_2\) from the index-trial covariate distribution, then draw a PASI reduction from the linear model, where the \(A\)-versus-\(C\) conditional benefit is \(4+x_1\) points plus mean-zero noise. Reduce the comparator trial to its reported means \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{BC}=(0.5,0.5)\) and \(d_{BC}=3.0\), mimicking the published aggregate data. Apply each method to this single dataset to obtain its triple \((\hat\theta_n,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_n,I_n)\). That one pass produces one row of the table below; the study repeats it \(R\) times with fresh random draws. Seeing this makes plain why \(R=5\) is a teaching device and not a real study: five datasets pin down neither the bias nor the coverage with any precision, as the Monte Carlo standard errors will show.

Estimands (E). The target is the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) benefit in the comparator population. From Example 18.3, \(d_{AC(\mathcal{P}_{BC})}=4+\bar x_{1,BC}=4.5\), so \[ \theta=d_{AB(\mathcal{P}_{BC})}=d_{AC(\mathcal{P}_{BC})}-d_{BC}=4.5-3.0=1.5. \]

Methods (M). The adjusted method targets \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=d_{AC(\mathcal{P}_{BC})}-d_{BC}=1.5=\theta\), correctly specified, asymptotically unbiased. The naive Bucher method uses the index-population benefit \(d_{AC(\mathcal{P}_{AC})}=4+\bar x_{1,AC}=4.0\), so it targets \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}^{\mathrm{naive}}=4.0-3.0=1.0\), a fixed bias of \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}^{\mathrm{naive}}-\theta=-0.5\).

Performance measures (P): the adjusted method by hand. Suppose at the chosen \(n\) we run \(R=5\) replications of the adjusted method (an unrealistically small \(R\), used only so the arithmetic is transparent; the Monte Carlo standard errors below show why \(R=5\) is far too few in practice) and observe the point estimates and nominal ninety-five percent Wald intervals \(\hat\theta^{(r)}\pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{(r)}\) with a common reported standard error \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{(r)}=0.30\) (half-width \(1.96\times 0.30=0.588\)):

Bias. The mean estimate is \(\bar{\hat\theta}=(0.9+0.9+1.4+1.9+1.9)/5=7.0/5=1.4\), so by Equation 29.2 \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}=1.4-1.5=-0.1\) and the relative bias is \(-0.1/1.5=-6.7\%\).

Empirical standard error. The deviations from \(\bar{\hat\theta}=1.4\) are \(-0.5,-0.5,0,0.5,0.5\), with squares \(0.25,0.25,0,0.25,0.25\) summing to \(1.0\); by Equation 29.6, \(S^2=1.0/(5-1)=0.25\) and \(\mathrm{EmpSE}=0.5\).

Monte Carlo standard error of the bias. By Equation 29.3, \(\mathrm{MCSE}(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}) =\mathrm{EmpSE}/\sqrt{R}=0.5/\sqrt{5}=0.224\). The estimated bias \(-0.1\) is smaller in magnitude than its Monte Carlo standard error, so from \(R=5\) replications we cannot distinguish it from zero; pinning the bias to a Monte Carlo standard error of \(0.02\) would require, by Equation 29.5, \(R\ge\mathrm{Var}_n/m^2\approx 0.25/0.02^2=625\) replications.

Mean squared error and the exact identity. The squared errors from \(\theta=1.5\) are \((0.9-1.5)^2=0.36\), \(0.36\), \((1.4-1.5)^2=0.01\), \((1.9-1.5)^2=0.16\), \(0.16\), summing to \(1.05\), so \(\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}=1.05/5=0.21\). The exact identity Equation 29.13 checks: \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}^2+\frac{R-1}{R}\,\mathrm{EmpSE}^2 =(-0.1)^2+\frac{4}{5}(0.25)=0.01+0.20=0.21=\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}. \]

Coverage and the diagnostic. Three of the five intervals contain \(\theta=1.5\), so by Equation 29.8 \(\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}=3/5=0.6\), with Monte Carlo standard error \(\sqrt{0.6\times 0.4/5}=\sqrt{0.048}=0.219\) by Equation 29.10. The model-based standard error is \(\mathrm{ModSE}=0.30\), well below the empirical \(\mathrm{EmpSE}=0.50\); this is the \(\mathrm{ModSE}<\mathrm{EmpSE}\) regime of Section 29.4, the reported standard error understates the true variability, the intervals are too narrow, and coverage falls below nominal. (With only \(R=5\) the coverage estimate is itself very imprecise; the point is the arithmetic and the diagnostic direction, not the value \(0.6\).)

Performance measures (P): the naive method by theory. The naive method has the fixed bias \(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}^{\mathrm{naive}}-\theta=-0.5\), which does not shrink with \(n\), while the per-replication scale \(\sigma_n\to 0\) (recall \(\sigma_n\) is the estimator’s true standard deviation at sample size \(n\), so it shrinks as the trial grows, at the usual \(1/\sqrt{n}\) rate). By Theorem 29.2 its limiting coverage is \(h(b)=\Phi(z-b)-\Phi(-z-b)\) with \(z=1.96\) and standardized bias \(b=(\theta_{\mathrm{work}}^{\mathrm{naive}}-\theta)/\sigma_n=-0.5/\sigma_n\). Evaluating at three sample sizes (so three values of \(\sigma_n\)):

\(0.50\) \(-1.0\) \(\Phi(2.96)-\Phi(-0.96)=0.9985-0.1685=0.830\)

As \(n\) grows and \(\sigma_n\) shrinks, the naive method’s coverage falls from \(0.83\) to \(0.48\) to \(0.001\), marching to \(0\) exactly as Corollary 29.4 predicts: more data tightens the interval around the wrong value \(1.0\). The adjusted method, with \(b=0\), keeps coverage at the nominal \(h(0)=2\Phi(1.96)-1=0.95\) for every \(\sigma_n\). The contrast between a coverage that collapses with \(n\) (biased) and one that holds at nominal (unbiased) is the simulation signature distinguishing the two, and it is the empirical face of items 5 and 6 of Proposition 29.2. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What the miniature shows and what it hides. The example makes three lessons concrete. First, the exact identity Equation 29.13 holds in the data, not merely in expectation: \(0.21=0.01+0.20\) with no slack. Second, a performance estimate without its Monte Carlo standard error is uninterpretable: the bias \(-0.1\) is invisible against its noise \(0.224\) at \(R=5\), and only Equation 29.5 tells us that \(R\approx 625\) would resolve it. Third, coverage is a joint verdict on bias and standard error: the adjusted method undercovers here only because its reported standard error is too small (\(\mathrm{ModSE}<\mathrm{EmpSE}\)), a curable calibration fault, whereas the naive method undercovers because it is biased, an incurable fault that worsens with \(n\). What the miniature hides is the realism of a true data-generating mechanism, where outcomes are drawn with noise, weights are estimated, and \(\sigma_n\) emerges from the design rather than being posited; a genuine study fixes the mechanism, draws \(R\ge 1000\) datasets per scenario, and tabulates these same measures across a grid of overlap and effect-modification settings. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The decision the numbers imply. Read as a dossier, the miniature says this. The adjusted method looks valid, since no bias is detectable, but its intervals look overconfident, reporting a standard error of \(0.30\) against a true spread of \(0.50\); that fault is repaired by using the sandwich standard error rather than by collecting more data. The naive method looks invalid in a way no standard error can fix, and its intervals will only worsen as the real trials grow. The next scenario an analyst would run varies the severity imbalance \(\Delta\bar x_1\) to trace how fast the naive method’s coverage collapses and to confirm that the adjusted method holds nominal across the grid, which is exactly the aim stated at the top of this ADEMP specification. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

29.11 Notes and references

The ADEMP framework of Definition 29.1 is due to Morris et al. (2019), whose tutorial is the standard reference for the design, execution, and reporting of simulation studies in statistics; the five-component structure, the emphasis on stating estimands and aims before simulating, the definitions of the performance measures, the Monte Carlo standard error formulas of Section 29.3 and Section 29.4, and the rule of thumb for choosing the number of replications from a target Monte Carlo standard error (Corollary 29.1, Corollary 29.2) all follow that paper. The distinction between the empirical and model-based standard errors and its use as a coverage diagnostic (Definition 29.4 and the remark following Lemma 29.3) is likewise from Morris et al. (2019); the normal-theory Monte Carlo error of the empirical standard error Equation 29.9 is their formula, derived here from the chi-square law of the sample variance and the delta method (Theorem 5.3).

The mean-squared-error decomposition Theorem 29.1 is classical and elementary; the exact finite-sample identity Equation 29.13 for the Monte Carlo estimators, which mirrors it at the sample level, is the simulation-specific companion and is the algebraic content behind reporting bias, variance, and mean squared error as a coherent triple. The coverage-collapse phenomenon of Theorem 29.2, that a fixed asymptotic bias sends Wald coverage to zero as the standard error shrinks, is folklore in the asymptotics literature; the explicit limiting-coverage curve \(h(b)\) of Lemma 29.5 and its consequences are assembled here from Slutsky’s theorem (Theorem 5.2) and the continuous mapping theorem (Theorem 5.1). The duality of Section 29.7, that Type I error and power are the coverage theorem read at the null and at the alternative, is the standard correspondence between two-sided Wald tests and Wald intervals. The efficiency baseline of Proposition 29.1 grounds the variance comparisons of a simulation in the Cramér-Rao bound (Theorem 5.7, Definition 5.9, Corollary 5.2); the upgrade from convergence in distribution to convergence of variances requires uniform integrability and is cited from Billingsley (1995), and the optimality of the information bound among regular estimators is the convolution and local-asymptotic-minimax theory cited in Chapter 5.

The empirical robustness ordering Proposition 29.2 is the synthesis of Chapter 8 of Phillippo (2019), whose simulation study compared multilevel network meta-regression, simulated treatment comparison, MAIC, and the unadjusted Bucher comparison across a designed grid of overlap, effect-modification, and effect-modifier-inclusion scenarios; the unanchored addendum follows Chandler and Ishak (2026). We have been careful to mark this result as empirical rather than proved, and to anchor each of its claims to a theorem established earlier, the consistency and equivalence results of Chapters 17 to 20 for the favorable items and the limitation theorems Theorem 16.1, Theorem 20.2, 1, Theorem 12.6 together with the coverage collapse Corollary 29.4 for the unfavorable ones.

Simulation theory connects to the quantitative bias analysis of Chapter 28 from the other direction: a simulation fixes the data-generating mechanism and measures a method’s error, whereas probabilistic bias analysis (Definition 28.4) fixes a method and propagates assumed distributions of unmeasured bias parameters through it; both are Monte Carlo computations over a designed input distribution, and the performance measures of this chapter are the natural summaries of either output.

No part of this chapter has a machine-checked counterpart. The companion Coq development formalizes the algebraic and causal core of the population-adjustment methods (the effective-sample-size bound Theorem 18.6 in theories/MAIC.v, the Bucher and balancing-score results, and the estimator definitions), but it contains no simulation, mean-squared-error, or coverage content: a simulation study is an empirical procedure whose conclusions, such as Proposition 29.2, are findings under specific data-generating mechanisms rather than theorems, and the elementary identities of Section 29.3 to Section 29.7, while provable, are not part of the formalized development. Chapter 30 documents exactly what the Coq sources do and do not establish; the simulation theory of this chapter is deliberately outside that scope, and the absence of a machine-checked tag on any result here is itself accurate to the sources. 29.12 Exercises

The exercises progress from warm-up interpretation to hand computation to proof and design. A \(\star\) marks an exercise whose full solution appears in Appendix F.

Exercise 29.1 (Warm-up, interpretation.) A simulation reports a bias of \(0.08\) with Monte Carlo standard error \(0.03\) for method X, and a bias of \(0.08\) with Monte Carlo standard error \(0.005\) for method Y. Both headline the same bias. Explain in one or two sentences why the second is firm evidence of a real systematic error while the first is not, and say what the analyst should do about method X to reach a verdict.

Exercise 29.2 (Warm-up, interpretation.) Two methods are simulated at sample sizes \(n=200,800,3200\). Method P has coverage \(0.94,0.95,0.94\); method Q has coverage \(0.90,0.71,0.32\). Using Corollary 29.4 and the telltale-signature remark of Section 29.6, say which method is biased and which is merely less efficient, and explain what the trend of Q’s coverage with \(n\) reveals that a single sample size could not.

Exercise 29.3 (Computation.) Performance measures by hand. A simulation of an unbiased method returns \(R=8\) replicate estimates of an estimand \(\theta=2.0\): \(1.6,1.8,1.9,2.0,2.1,2.2,2.3,2.5\). Compute \(\bar{\hat\theta}\), \(\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\), the empirical standard error \(\mathrm{EmpSE}\), the Monte Carlo standard error of the bias, and the Monte Carlo mean squared error \(\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}\), and verify the exact identity Equation 29.13. Then determine, using Equation 29.5, how many replications would be needed to estimate the bias with a Monte Carlo standard error of \(0.01\).

Exercise 29.4 (Computation, design.) Designing the replication count. Using Corollary 29.2, find the smallest \(R\) that guarantees a Monte Carlo standard error of at most \(0.005\) for an estimated coverage, valid for every true coverage. Then find the smaller \(R\) that suffices if the true coverage is believed to be near \(0.95\), and explain why the worst-case requirement occurs at a true coverage of \(0.5\).

Exercise 29.5 (Computation.) The coverage curve. For the curve \(h(b)=\Phi(1.96-b)-\Phi(-1.96-b)\) of Lemma 29.5, compute \(h(b)\) at \(b=0,0.5,1,2,3\) from a standard normal table, confirm that \(h(0)=0.95\) and that \(h\) is strictly decreasing in \(|b|\), and find the standardized bias \(b^\star\) at which coverage has dropped to \(0.90\). Interpret \(b^\star\) in words: how large, in standard-error units, must an asymptotic bias be to cost five percentage points of coverage? Hint. For the inverse problem, note from your table that \(h(0.5)=0.921\) and \(h(1)=0.830\), so the value \(b^\star\) with \(h(b^\star)=0.90\) lies between \(0.5\) and \(1\); interpolate, then refine by evaluating \(\Phi(1.96-b)-\Phi(-1.96-b)\) at your candidate.

Exercise 29.6 (Interpretation.) Diagnosing a standard-error failure. A simulation reports, for a MAIC estimator at fixed \(n\) over \(R=1000\) replications, \(\mathrm{EmpSE}=0.42\) and \(\mathrm{ModSE}=0.31\), with empirical coverage of nominal ninety-five percent intervals equal to \(0.86\). Argue from Section 29.4 and the remark after Corollary 29.5 that the undercoverage is attributable to standard-error underestimation rather than to bias, estimate the ratio \(c=\mathrm{ModSE}/\mathrm{EmpSE}\) and the coverage \(2\Phi(cz)-1\) it predicts under no bias, and relate the underestimation to treating the estimated MAIC weights as fixed (Theorem 18.9).

Exercise 29.7 (\(\star\), design.) Power and sample size. Using Theorem 29.3, an analyst plans a study to detect a true contrast \(\theta=0.4\) against the null \(\theta_0=0\) at level \(\alpha=0.05\), with a method whose standard error at sample size \(n\) is \(\sigma_n=c/\sqrt{n}\) for a known constant \(c=2.0\). (a) Express the standardized signal \(\Delta_n\) as a function of \(n\). (b) Using the one-sided approximation \(\mathrm{Power}\approx\Phi(\Delta_n-z_{\alpha/2})\), find the sample size \(n\) giving power \(0.80\). (c) Show from Equation 29.17 that the two-term and one-term power formulas differ negligibly at this \(n\) by bounding the dropped term \(\Phi(-\Delta_n-z_{\alpha/2})\). (d) Explain, via the test-interval duality remark, how the same calculation would read off as a statement about the width of the confidence interval. Hint. For (b), set \(\Phi(\Delta_n-1.96)=0.80\), so \(\Delta_n-1.96=z_{0.20}=0.84\) and \(\Delta_n=2.80\); with \(\Delta_n=|\theta-\theta_0|/\sigma_n=0.4\sqrt{n}/2\), solve \(0.2\sqrt{n}=2.80\). For (c), bound the dropped term by \(\Phi(-\Delta_n-1.96)=\Phi(-4.76)\).

Exercise 29.8 (\(\star\), proof.) A mean-squared-error-optimal moment set. Consider, in the geometry of Example 29.1, a family of adjusted estimators indexed by whether the prognostic covariate \(x_2\) is balanced in addition to the effect modifier \(x_1\). Balancing only \(x_1\) leaves a small omitted-covariate bias \(\beta\) on a non-collapsible scale but retains effective sample size \(\mathrm{ESS}_1\), giving variance \(\sigma^2 n/\mathrm{ESS}_1\); balancing both removes the bias but lowers the effective sample size to \(\mathrm{ESS}_2<\mathrm{ESS}_1\), giving variance \(\sigma^2 n/\mathrm{ESS}_2\). (a) Write the mean squared error of each estimator using Theorem 29.1. (b) Derive the exact condition on \(\beta\), \(\mathrm{ESS}_1\), and \(\mathrm{ESS}_2\) under which balancing only \(x_1\) achieves the smaller mean squared error. (c) Relate the condition to Theorem 18.6 and to the bias-variance remark following Lemma 29.4, and explain why, on the identity scale where \(\beta=0\) for a purely prognostic covariate, balancing \(x_2\) can only hurt.
