6  Linear Regression from First Principles

6.1 Introduction

Every method in this book that adjusts an indirect comparison for differences between trial populations is, at its core, a regression. Simulated treatment comparison fits an outcome model on individual patient data and then evaluates it at a comparator population; multilevel network meta-regression writes the linear predictor of every arm as a regression on covariates; and matching-adjusted indirect comparison reweights individuals to balance covariates, a procedure we will see (Chapter 18) is closely tied to a weighted regression. Before any of that can be made rigorous we must understand the simplest and most important regression model completely: the linear model, fit by ordinary least squares. This chapter builds that theory from the ground up and proves every statement it makes.

The questions we answer are the following. Given data, what does “the line of best fit” mean precisely, and what formula produces it? In what sense is that formula optimal, and under what assumptions? If we repeated the experiment, how would the estimate vary, and how do we quantify our uncertainty about it with a confidence interval or a test? Finally, the philosophical question that justifies the whole enterprise: why squares? Why minimize the sum of squared residuals rather than, say, the sum of absolute residuals? We preview the answer at once, so the reader does not spend the chapter wondering: under Gaussian errors, minimizing the sum of squares is exactly maximum likelihood (proved in Section 6.8); minimizing the sum of absolute residuals would instead be maximum likelihood under a different, Laplace-shaped error model. The choice of squares is therefore a statement about the assumed shape of the noise, not an arbitrary convention.

We will see that under the assumption of normal, homoskedastic (equal-variance), independent errors the least-squares estimator is selected by four different optimality principles at once:

  1. Projection (pure geometry). The fitted vector is the point of the model space closest to the data. This uses only linear algebra, no probability (Section 6.3).

  2. Best linear unbiased estimator, BLUE (Gauss-Markov). Among all estimators that are linear in the data and unbiased, ordinary least squares has the smallest variance. This uses only the first two moments of the errors, not their shape (Theorem 6.3).

  3. Maximum likelihood estimator, MLE. Under Gaussian errors, least squares is the coefficient value that makes the observed data most probable (Theorem 6.11).

  4. Uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimator, UMVU (Cramér-Rao). Under Gaussian errors, least squares beats every unbiased estimator, even nonlinear ones (Corollary 6.2).

These are four genuinely different statements: (i) is geometric, (ii) ranges over linear estimators assuming only two moments, (iii) is likelihood-based, and (iv) ranges over all unbiased estimators. That they select the same estimator is the reason linear regression is the workhorse of applied statistics, and it is the foundation on which the later chapters build.

How to read this chapter. Every result is proved in full, because every later chapter leans on these facts and we want nothing downstream to be a black box. On a first reading you may take the formulas on trust and skip the proofs without losing the through-line; the prose around each result tells you what it means and why it matters for indirect comparison. The chapter is built in four layers, summarized here so you always know which layer you are in.

Layer Where What it delivers Assumptions used

Optimality without normality Section 6.4 OLS is BLUE; an unbiased variance estimate G1, G2

The development is self-contained but rests on the linear algebra of Chapters 1 to 3 (column spaces, Theorem 2.5, the Theorem 3.2) and the probability and inference of Chapters 4 and 5 (the multivariate normal, Definition 4.7, Theorem 5.7). We recall what we use and prove everything that is specific to regression.

6.2 The linear model and its assumptions

Before the abstractions arrive, here is the concrete example we will return to at every transition.

Example 6.1 (A running example: a small two-arm trial) Six patients in a trial are randomized to a control arm or an active arm. For each patient we record the change in HbA1c (a continuous glycemic outcome, where a more negative change is better) together with the patient’s baseline disease severity. To model the expected change we use three regressors: a constant term, a treatment indicator \(T\) (equal to \(1\) on the active arm and \(0\) on control), and the mean-centered baseline severity \(z\). In words, \[ \mathbb{E}[\text{change}]=\beta_0\cdot 1+\beta_1\cdot T+\beta_2\cdot z . \] Each regressor is one column of the design matrix, and each carries a clinical role:

Column of \(\mathbf{X}\) Meaning Coefficient Clinical role
\(\mathbf{1}\) constant (intercept) \(\beta_0\) mean outcome in the reference arm at average covariates
\(T\) treatment indicator \(\beta_1\) the adjusted treatment effect we care about
\(z\) baseline severity, centered \(\beta_2\) a prognostic-covariate adjustment
(\(\mathrm{age}\)) (a further covariate) (\(\beta_3\)) (a further adjustment, omitted below for hand arithmetic)

Taking three patients per arm at centered baseline values \(z=-1,0,1\), the design matrix and response are \[ \mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\1&0&0\\1&0&1\\1&1&-1\\1&1&0\\1&1&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\\-1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \] the first three rows being the control arm and the last three the active arm. We compute the fit in Example 6.2 and reuse this design when we reach the Gauss-Markov theorem and the variance formulas. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We observe a response vector \(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) and, for each of the \(n\) units, the values of \(p\) regressors collected as the rows of a known matrix \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\). The model asserts that, up to random error, \(\mathbf{y}\) lies in the column space \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\).

Definition 6.1 (The linear model) The linear model for a response \(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) with design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\) (\(p\le n\)) is \[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \tag{6.1}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) is an unknown vector of coefficients and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) is an unobserved random error vector. The Gauss-Markov assumptions are

(G1) zero mean, \(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]=\mathbf{0}_n\);

(G2) homoskedasticity and no correlation, \(\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\sigma^2 I_n\) for some unknown \(\sigma^2>0\), that is, \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2\) for every \(i\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_i,\varepsilon_k)=0\) for \(i\ne k\).

The model has full column rank when \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\). For the exact distributional theory of Section 6.5 onward we add

(G3) normality, \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{0}_n,\sigma^2 I_n)\),

which (with G1, G2) is equivalent to \(\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2 I_n)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. The model says the response is a straight-line combination of the regressors, \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\), plus random noise \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) that on average is zero (G1), has the same spread for every patient and does not link patients together (G2), and, when we want exact tests, follows a bell curve (G3). In the running example \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is the part of a patient’s HbA1c change explained by their arm and baseline severity, and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) is everything else.

A recurring source of confusion is the difference between the error and the residual. The table below fixes the roles of the symbols used throughout the chapter.

Symbol Name Observed? Meaning
\(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) error (disturbance) no the true random noise in Equation 6.1; a property of nature
\(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) residual yes what is left after fitting; computed from the data
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) coefficients no the true unknown effects
\(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) estimator yes our estimate of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) from the data
\(\sigma^2\) error variance no the true noise variance in G2
\(s^2\) variance estimate yes our estimate of \(\sigma^2\) (Theorem 6.4)
\(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) fitted values yes the model’s prediction at each observed row

The left-hand quantities \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2\) are unknown features of the population we never see; the right-hand quantities \(\mathbf{r},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},s^2\) are things we compute. Estimation is the act of guessing the first column from the second.

A few words on the content of the assumptions. Assumption G1 says the regressors capture the systematic part of the response, so that the model \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is correct on average; it forces \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\). Assumption G2 is two assumptions in one: equal variances across units (homoskedasticity) and uncorrelated errors. It is what makes ordinary, unweighted least squares the right procedure; when it fails one uses weighted or generalized least squares, and the robust “sandwich” variance of Definition 5.11 repairs the inference. Assumption G3 is needed only for exact, finite-sample tests and intervals; the point estimator and the Gauss-Markov optimality of Section 6.4 require only G1 and G2.

Remark. What homoskedasticity looks like, and when it fails. Homoskedasticity (G2, first half) means every patient’s outcome scatters around the regression line by the same amount. Picture the data points: under homoskedasticity they form a band of constant vertical thickness around the fitted line; heteroskedasticity is a band that fans out, for example wider scatter at higher baseline severity. A health researcher should expect G2 to fail when outcomes are recorded on different scales or in clusters: sicker patients often vary more than healthy ones, and patients within the same hospital are correlated (the second half of G2, no correlation, then also fails). When G2 fails, the point estimate \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is still unbiased, but its reported standard errors are wrong. Two standard repairs exist: weighted or generalized least squares (developed in Exercise 6.8), which models the unequal variances directly, and the robust “sandwich” variance estimator of Definition 5.11, which estimates the uncertainty of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) without assuming the tidy \(\sigma^2 I_n\) covariance structure at all. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reading the coefficients in health terms. The coefficient \(\beta_k\) is the conditional effect on \(\mathbb{E}[y_i]\) of a one-unit change in the \(k\)-th regressor with all others held fixed. The three roles a column can play, which recur throughout the book, are visible already in the running example.

  • The coefficient on the treatment indicator, \(\beta_1\), is the adjusted treatment effect: the expected difference in outcome between the active and control arms for two patients with the same baseline severity. This is the quantity every indirect comparison ultimately targets.
  • The coefficient on baseline severity, \(\beta_2\), is a prognostic effect: severity predicts the outcome regardless of treatment. A covariate that influences the outcome but not the treatment effect is a prognostic variable (Definition 11.5); you do not need the formal definition yet, only the picture of a covariate that shifts everyone’s outcome.
  • If we added a treatment-by-severity product column, its coefficient would measure how the treatment effect itself changes with severity. A covariate that changes the treatment effect is an effect modifier (Definition 11.3). Effect modifiers are the central obstacle in indirect comparison: two trials with different severity mixes can report different treatment effects even when nothing else differs. We meet them properly in Part II.

On the identity link used here, this conditional reading is also the marginal (population-averaged) one, so the distinctions stay simple. For the nonlinear links of Chapter 7 the conditional and marginal effects diverge, a phenomenon named Definition 7.7. The clean behavior of the identity-link linear model is exactly why it is the right place to learn the machinery before meeting its complications. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Throughout Section 6.3 to Section 6.7 we assume full column rank, \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\). In plain terms this means no regressor column can be reconstructed as a combination of the others, so each coefficient is separately identifiable: the data can tell the columns apart. The assumption fails in familiar situations, for example when a categorical predictor is coded with an intercept and a separate indicator for every level, so that the indicators add up to the intercept column. We treat that rank-deficient case fully in Section 6.9, where the geometry survives intact and only the coordinate vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) becomes non-unique; a reader who meets a coding problem of this kind in practice can jump ahead to that section without loss. The following small lemma is the algebraic hinge of the full-rank theory: it is what makes \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) invertible.

Lemma 6.1 (Null space of the Gram matrix) For any \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\), \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\) and \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\); consequently \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\). In particular \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is invertible if and only if \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), in which case it is positive definite.

Proof. We argue in four short steps.

Step 1 (the two null spaces are equal). If \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) then \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\), so \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\). Conversely, if \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) then \[ 0=\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}) =\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\|_2^2, \] whence \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) (a vector of norm zero is the zero vector); thus \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\) and the two null spaces are equal.

Step 2 (the ranks are equal). Apply the rank-nullity theorem (Theorem 1.3) to the \(p\times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) and to \(\mathbf{X}\), both of which have \(p\) columns. Rank-nullity says (number of columns) \(=\) rank \(+\) nullity, so \[ \mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})=p-\dim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}) =p-\dim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}), \] the middle equality being Step 1.

Step 3 (the column spaces are equal). The inclusion \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\) is immediate, because every column of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X})\) is \(\mathbf{X}^\top\) times a column of \(\mathbf{X}\). To upgrade this inclusion to equality it suffices to show the two spaces have the same dimension. The dimension of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\) is \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\) by Step 2, while the dimension of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\) is \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\), and a basic fact of linear algebra is that a matrix and its transpose have the same rank, \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}^\top)=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\) (this is part of the fundamental theorem of the four subspaces, Theorem 1.5; row rank equals column rank). The two dimensions match, so the inclusion is an equality.

Step 4 (definiteness and invertibility). The matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is symmetric with \(\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\|_2^2\ge 0\), so it is positive semidefinite. It is positive definite exactly when \(\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\|_2^2>0\) for every \(\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}\), that is, when \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})=\{\mathbf{0}\}\), equivalently \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\) (Theorem 3.3). A positive definite matrix is invertible. \(\square\)

Reader translation. The Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) inherits exactly the non-redundancy of \(\mathbf{X}\): it is invertible if and only if the regressors are not redundant. This is the single fact that lets us write down the closed-form estimator Equation 6.3 below; when it fails we are in the rank-deficient world of Section 6.9.

6.3 Ordinary least squares: two derivations

We measure the fit of a candidate coefficient vector \(\mathbf{b}\) by the sum of squared residuals.

Definition 6.2 (Residual, total, and explained sums of squares) For a candidate \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) the objective function is \[ S(\mathbf{b}) = \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}). \] At a minimizer \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) write the fitted values \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and residuals \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\). The residual sum of squares is \(\mathrm{RSS}=\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2=S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\). When the model contains an intercept (the vector \(\mathbf{1}_n\) lies in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\)) the total sum of squares is \(\mathrm{TSS}=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\bar y)^2=\|\mathbf{y}-\bar y\,\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2\) with \(\bar y=\tfrac1n\sum_i y_i\), and the explained sum of squares is \(\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\bar y\,\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. \(S(\mathbf{b})\) is the total squared miss if we used the coefficients \(\mathbf{b}\); least squares picks the \(\mathbf{b}\) that makes it smallest. Once we have the winner \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), the RSS is the leftover squared error, the TSS is the total spread of the response around its mean, and the explained sum of squares is the part of that spread the regression accounts for.

Remark. A notation trap. The subscript on \(\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is deliberate. Later chapters use the bare symbol \(\mathrm{ESS}\) for the effective sample size of a weighted sample (central to MAIC in Chapter 18), an entirely different quantity. We always write the subscript here, \(\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}\), for the explained regression sum of squares, to keep the two apart. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

6.3.1 Derivation by calculus

Theorem 6.1 (Normal equations and the least-squares estimator) A vector \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) minimizes \(S\) over \(\mathbb{R}^p\) if and only if it solves the normal equations \[ \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}. \tag{6.2}\] The normal equations are always consistent. When \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\) the minimizer is unique and equals \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}. \tag{6.3}\]

Proof. Expand the objective, using \(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\) (a scalar equals its transpose): \[ S(\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}-2\,\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} +\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{b}. \] This is a quadratic form in \(\mathbf{b}\) (a function built from the squared shape \(\mathbf{b}^\top A\mathbf{b}\) for a symmetric matrix \(A=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\); see Definition 3.6), with gradient and Hessian \[ \nabla S(\mathbf{b})=-2\,\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}+2\,\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{b}, \qquad \nabla^2 S(\mathbf{b})=2\,\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}. \] The gradient is the multivariable analogue of the single-variable derivative: setting it to zero is the same first-order condition as in one-variable calculus, \(\tfrac{d}{db}\sum_i(y_i-b\,x_i)^2=0\), written once for all \(p\) coordinates at once. By Lemma 6.1 the Hessian \(2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is positive semidefinite everywhere, so \(S\) is a convex function on \(\mathbb{R}^p\); for a differentiable convex function a point is a global minimizer if and only if its gradient vanishes there (a fact we now verify directly so as not to lean on convex-analysis machinery). Setting \(\nabla S(\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{0}\) gives exactly the normal equations Equation 6.2. We prove the two directions of the “if and only if” separately.

Forward direction (a solution of the normal equations is a global minimizer). Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) solve Equation 6.2 and let \(\mathbf{b}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\mathbf{d}\) be arbitrary. Then \[ S(\mathbf{b})-S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) =-2\,\mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} +2\,\mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} +\mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{d} =2\,\mathbf{d}^\top\!\big(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\big) +\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{d}\|_2^2 =\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{d}\|_2^2\ge 0, \] the linear term vanishing because \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) solves Equation 6.2. Hence \(S(\mathbf{b})\ge S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\) for every \(\mathbf{b}\), so every solution of the normal equations is a global minimizer.

Reverse direction (a minimizer solves the normal equations). Conversely, if \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is a minimizer then \(\nabla S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\mathbf{0}\) (a necessary first-order condition for an interior minimum of a differentiable function on \(\mathbb{R}^p\)), which is Equation 6.2.

Consistency holds because \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top) =\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\) by Lemma 6.1, so the right-hand side lies in the column space of the coefficient matrix. In plain terms: the normal equations always have at least one solution; they may simply fail to have a unique one when the design is rank deficient (the case of Section 6.9). When \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), Lemma 6.1 makes \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) invertible, so Equation 6.2 has the unique solution Equation 6.3. \(\square\)

Remark. Why “normal”? Here “normal” is the geometric word for perpendicular. The equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\mathbf{0}\) say the residual is perpendicular to every column of \(\mathbf{X}\). The projection derivation below makes this geometry explicit. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We make the formula concrete at once, rather than waiting for the end of the chapter.

Example 6.2 (The running example: computing the estimate) For the trial design of Example 6.1, the Gram matrix is block structured because the centered baseline \(z\) is balanced across arms (its column is orthogonal to both the intercept and the treatment column): \[ \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}6&3&0\\3&3&0\\0&0&4\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac13&-\tfrac13&0\\[2pt]-\tfrac13&\tfrac23&0\\[2pt]0&0&\tfrac14\end{pmatrix}. \] With \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}=(3,0,4)^\top\) (the column sums of \(\mathbf{y}\), of \(T y\), and of \(z y\)), \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} =\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac13(3)-\tfrac13(0)\\[2pt]-\tfrac13(3)+\tfrac23(0)\\[2pt]\tfrac14(4)\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}. \] Reading the three numbers clinically: the control arm’s expected HbA1c change at average baseline is \(\hat\beta_0=1\); the adjusted treatment effect is \(\hat\beta_1=-1\), so the active arm lowers the change by one unit (an improvement); and each one-unit rise in baseline severity adds \(\hat\beta_2=1\) to the expected change. These illustrative values happen to fit exactly, so every residual is zero here; the worked example of Section 6.10 carries a data set with genuine scatter through every variance and testing formula. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Why this matters (ITC). This predict-from-coefficients step is the engine of simulated treatment comparison (Chapter 19). There one fits exactly this kind of outcome model on individual patient data from one trial, then evaluates \(\mathbf{x}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) at the comparator trial’s mean covariates to predict how the treatment would have performed in the other population. The normal equations are precisely what make that prediction well-defined.

6.3.2 Derivation by projection

The calculus derivation produces the formula but hides its meaning. The projection derivation reveals that least squares is geometry: \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is the point of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) closest to \(\mathbf{y}\).

Proof. (Projection derivation of Theorem 6.1.) Minimizing \(S(\mathbf{b})=\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\|_2^2\) over \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) is the same as minimizing \(\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{v}\|_2^2\) over \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), because as \(\mathbf{b}\) ranges over \(\mathbb{R}^p\) the vector \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\) ranges over exactly \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) (Definition 1.10). The orthogonal projection theorem (Theorem 2.5) states that the closed subspace \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) contains a unique closest point \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=P_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})}\mathbf{y}\) to \(\mathbf{y}\), characterized by the orthogonality of the residual to the subspace: \(\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\perp\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\). Orthogonality to \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) means orthogonality to each column of \(\mathbf{X}\), that is, \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}})=\mathbf{0}\). Writing \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) this is \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), the normal equations Equation 6.2 once more. When \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\) the representation \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is unique and Equation 6.3 follows. \(\square\)

The picture behind the projection derivation is the single most useful image in the chapter. The data \(\mathbf{y}\) is a point in \(\mathbb{R}^n\); the model space \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) is a flat subspace (a line, a plane, or higher); and the fitted vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is the foot of the perpendicular dropped from \(\mathbf{y}\) onto that subspace, with the residual \(\mathbf{r}\) as the perpendicular itself.

        y      (the data: a point in R^n)
        |\
        | \    r = y - yhat   (residual: perpendicular to the model space)
        |  \
        |   \
   -----+----*--------------------------  C(X): the model space {Xb : b in R^p}
             yhat = Hy
             (fitted values: the closest point of C(X) to y; right angle at yhat)

Reader translation. “Line of best fit” is made exact: among all predictions the model can produce (every point of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\)), the fitted values are the unique one nearest the data, and “nearest” forces the leftover error to be perpendicular to the model. Everything else in the chapter is a consequence of this right angle.

Remark. The two derivations are the same computation read in two directions. The vanishing gradient \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{0}\) is the statement that the residual is orthogonal to every column of \(\mathbf{X}\). Calculus supplies the formula and convexity certifies it is a global minimum; projection supplies the picture and explains why the minimum is unique on the level of fitted values even when it is not unique on the level of coefficients (the theme of Section 6.9). Numerically one rarely forms \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) and inverts it; the orthogonal factorization \(\mathbf{X}=QR\) from Gram-Schmidt (Theorem 2.3) gives the more stable solution of \(R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=Q^\top\mathbf{y}\). This is what statistical software actually does: the lm() routine in R, and its equivalents, never form \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) explicitly but factor \(\mathbf{X}\) directly, which is numerically more accurate. The closed form Equation 6.3 is for understanding and proof, not for computation. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

6.3.3 The hat matrix and the residual maker

Definition 6.3 (Hat matrix and residual maker) Under \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\) the hat matrix is \[ H=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, \tag{6.4}\] so that the fitted values are \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=H\mathbf{y}\) (it “puts the hat on \(\mathbf{y}\)”). The residual maker is \(M=I_n-H\), so that the residuals are \(\mathbf{r}=M\mathbf{y}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. \(H\) is the single matrix that turns data into fitted values, \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=H \mathbf{y}\), and \(M=I_n-H\) turns data into residuals, \(\mathbf{r}=M\mathbf{y}\). They do all the work of fitting in one multiplication each.

Remark. Why you should care about \(H\): diagnostics. The hat matrix is not a mere bookkeeping device. Its \(i\)-th diagonal entry \(h_{ii}\), called the leverage of observation \(i\), measures how strongly that observation pulls its own fitted value toward itself; a high-leverage patient (one with unusual covariate values) can dominate the fit. Leverage is the basis of every regression diagnostic an applied researcher meets, including Cook’s distance for influential points. We develop the formal properties of leverage in Exercise 6.6. The idempotence \(H^2=H\) proved next also has a plain meaning: fitting is a projection, so once you have projected onto the model space, projecting again changes nothing. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Lemma 6.2 (Properties of H and M) With \(H\) and \(M\) as in Definition 6.3:

  1. \(H\) and \(M\) are symmetric and idempotent: \(H^\top=H\), \(H^2=H\), \(M^\top=M\), \(M^2=M\).
  2. \(HM=MH=0\).
  3. \(H\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}\) and \(M\mathbf{X}=0\); equivalently \(\mathbf{X}^\top H=\mathbf{X}^\top\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top M=0\).
  4. \(H\) is the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) and \(M\) the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\).
  5. \(\mathrm{tr}(H)=p\) and \(\mathrm{tr}(M)=n-p\).

Proof.

  1. Symmetry of \(H\): since \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) is symmetric (the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric), \(H^\top=\mathbf{X}\big((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\big)^\top\mathbf{X}^\top =\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top=H\). Idempotence: \[ H^2=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\underbrace{\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}}_{=I_p}\mathbf{X}^\top =\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top=H. \] Then \(M^\top=(I-H)^\top=I-H=M\) and \(M^2=(I-H)^2=I-2H+H^2=I-2H+H=I-H=M\).

  2. \(HM=H(I-H)=H-H^2=H-H=0\), and \(MH=(I-H)H=H-H^2=0\).

  3. \(H\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}I_p=\mathbf{X}\), hence \(M\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}-H\mathbf{X}=0\). Transposing and using symmetry gives \(\mathbf{X}^\top H=\mathbf{X}^\top\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top M=0\).

  4. A symmetric idempotent matrix is the orthogonal projection onto its column space (Definition 2.5). By (3), \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(H)\); conversely every column of \(H\) is \(\mathbf{X}\) times a vector, so \(\mathcal{C}(H)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\); thus \(\mathcal{C}(H)=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) and \(H=P_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})}\). Since \(M\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}\) exactly when \(H\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\), that is when \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\), and \(M=P^{\perp}_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})}\). The identity \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\) is the fundamental theorem of the four subspaces (Theorem 1.5).

  5. Using the cyclic property of the trace, \(\mathrm{tr}(H)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\big) =\mathrm{tr}\big((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\big)=\mathrm{tr}(I_p)=p\), and \(\mathrm{tr}(M)=\mathrm{tr}(I_n)-\mathrm{tr}(H)=n-p\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. These five properties are the algebraic toolkit reused in almost every proof that follows: idempotence and symmetry drive the distribution of the residual sum of squares (Theorem 6.6), \(HM=0\) drives the independence of the estimate and the variance estimate (Theorem 6.7), and \(\mathrm{tr}(M)=n-p\) is the source of the degrees-of-freedom divisor.

Proposition 6.1 (Geometry of fitted values and residuals) The fitted values and residuals satisfy \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), \(\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^\top\mathbf{r}=0\). Consequently \(\mathbf{y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{r}\) is the orthogonal decomposition of \(\mathbf{y}\) into its component in the model space and its component orthogonal to it, and \[ \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2=\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2+\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2 . \] If the model contains an intercept then in addition \(\sum_{i=1}^n r_i=0\), so the residuals have mean zero and \(\overline{\hat y}=\bar y\).

Proof. \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=H\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(H)=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) and \(\mathbf{r}=M\mathbf{y}\in \mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\) by parts (3) and (4) of Lemma 6.2. Their inner product is \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^\top\mathbf{r}=(H\mathbf{y})^\top(M\mathbf{y}) =\mathbf{y}^\top H M\mathbf{y}=0\) by part (2). Since \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\perp\mathbf{r}\), the Pythagorean theorem gives \(\|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2=\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{r}\|_2^2=\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2+\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2\). If \(\mathbf{1}_n\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) then \(H\mathbf{1}_n=\mathbf{1}_n\), so \(M\mathbf{1}_n=\mathbf{0}\) and \(\sum_i r_i=\mathbf{1}_n^\top\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{1}_n^\top M\mathbf{y}=(M\mathbf{1}_n)^\top\mathbf{y}=0\). Then \(\overline{\hat y}=\bar y-\tfrac1n\sum_i r_i=\bar y\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. The data splits cleanly into “what the model captured” (\(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)) and “what it missed” (\(\mathbf{r}\)), and these two parts are at right angles, so their squared lengths add (Pythagoras). When the model has an intercept the residuals also sum to zero, which is why the fitted line passes through the average point \((\bar x,\bar y)\). This orthogonal split is what the analysis-of-variance decomposition (Section 6.7) and \(R^2\) will measure.

6.4 The Gauss-Markov theorem

We now bring in the assumptions G1 and G2 and study \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) as a random vector. We use repeatedly the two rules of linear transformation of moments: for a fixed matrix \(A\) and random vector \(\mathbf{z}\), \(\mathbb{E}[A\mathbf{z}]=A\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}]\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(A\mathbf{z})=A\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{z})\,A^\top\). The second follows from the definition: \(\mathrm{Cov}(A\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{E}\big[A(\mathbf{z}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{z})(\mathbf{z}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{z})^\top A^\top\big]=A\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{z})\,A^\top\).

Theorem 6.2 (Mean and covariance of the OLS estimator) Under Definition 6.1 with G1, G2 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), the estimator Equation 6.3 is unbiased and has covariance \[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]=\boldsymbol{\beta}, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}. \tag{6.5}\]

Proof. Substitute Equation 6.1 into Equation 6.3 and set \(A=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\): \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=A(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) =(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\boldsymbol{\beta} +A\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} =\boldsymbol{\beta}+A\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \tag{6.6}\] Taking expectations and using G1, \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}] =\boldsymbol{\beta}+A\,\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]=\boldsymbol{\beta}\). For the covariance, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}=A\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), so by G2, \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=A\,\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\,A^\top =A(\sigma^2 I_n)A^\top =\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} =\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}, \] where we used the symmetry of \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. On average the estimator hits the true coefficients (unbiased), and its uncertainty is governed entirely by \(\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\), which depends on the design but not on the data values \(\mathbf{y}\). In the running example (Example 6.2), the \((T,T)\) entry of \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) is \(\tfrac23\), so the variance of the estimated treatment effect is \(\sigma^2\cdot\tfrac23\) and its standard error is \(\sigma\sqrt{2/3}\). A design that estimates the treatment effect more precisely is one that makes this diagonal entry small.

Among unbiased estimators that are linear in \(\mathbf{y}\), none has a smaller variance than OLS. This is the Gauss-Markov theorem, the central optimality result of the chapter. “Best” is meant in the strong matrix sense made precise next.

Definition 6.4 (Loewner order) For symmetric matrices \(A\) and \(B\) of the same size, write \(A\succeq B\), and say \(A\) dominates \(B\) in the Loewner order, when the difference \(A-B\) is positive semidefinite, that is \(\mathbf{z}^\top(A-B) \mathbf{z}\ge 0\) for every vector \(\mathbf{z}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. When \(A\) and \(B\) are the covariance matrices of two estimators, \(A\succeq B\) is a strong statement. Taking \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{c}\) it gives \(\mathbf{c}^\top A\mathbf{c}\ge\mathbf{c}^\top B \mathbf{c}\), that is, every linear combination \(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) of the coefficients has at least as large a variance under \(A\) as under \(B\). A single matrix inequality therefore compares the two estimators on every question at once, not one coefficient at a time.

Theorem 6.3 (Gauss-Markov) Assume Definition 6.1 with G1, G2 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\). Let \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=C\mathbf{y}\) be any estimator linear in \(\mathbf{y}\) that is unbiased for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) for every value of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Then \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\succeq 0, \] with equality if and only if \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) almost surely. In particular, for every fixed \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) the OLS estimator \(\mathbf{c}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators of \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\): \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE).

Roadmap of the proof. Write any rival linear unbiased estimator as \(C\mathbf{y}\). Unbiasedness forces \(C\mathbf{X}=I_p\). We split \(C=A+D\) into the OLS map \(A=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\) plus a remainder \(D\); read \(D\) as whatever extra linear operation the rival performs on top of OLS. Unbiasedness forces that extra operation to ignore the model space, \(D\mathbf{X}=0\), and this single fact makes the cross terms vanish, leaving the rival’s covariance equal to the OLS covariance plus a nonnegative piece \(\sigma^2 DD^\top\). Hence the rival can never beat OLS, and ties it only when \(D=0\).

Proof. Unbiasedness of \(C\mathbf{y}\) for all \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) means \(\mathbb{E}[C\mathbf{y}]=C\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\) for every \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^p\), which forces \[ C\mathbf{X}=I_p. \] Write \(C=A+D\) with \(A=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\) the OLS map, so \(D=C-A\). From \(C\mathbf{X}=I_p\) and \(A\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}=I_p\) we get \[ D\mathbf{X}=C\mathbf{X}-A\mathbf{X}=I_p-I_p=0 . \] Now compute the covariance of \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=C\mathbf{y}\) using \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{y}) =\sigma^2 I_n\): \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sigma^2 C C^\top=\sigma^2 (A+D)(A+D)^\top =\sigma^2\big(AA^\top+AD^\top+DA^\top+DD^\top\big). \] The cross terms vanish: \(AD^\top=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top D^\top =(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}(D\mathbf{X})^\top=0\), and \(DA^\top=(AD^\top)^\top=0\). Since \(AA^\top=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\), we obtain \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) =\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}+\sigma^2 DD^\top =\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})+\sigma^2 DD^\top . \tag{6.7}\] For any \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^p\), \(\mathbf{z}^\top DD^\top\mathbf{z}=\|D^\top\mathbf{z}\|_2^2\ge 0\), so \(DD^\top\succeq 0\) and the difference of covariance matrices is positive semidefinite. Taking \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{c}\) gives the scalar conclusion \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) =\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})+\sigma^2\|D^\top\mathbf{c}\|_2^2 \ge\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\).

Equality in the Loewner order means \(\mathbf{z}^\top DD^\top\mathbf{z}=\|D^\top\mathbf{z}\|_2^2=0\) for all \(\mathbf{z}\), hence \(D^\top=0\), hence \(D=0\) and \(C=A\), so \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=A\mathbf{y}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\). \(\square\)

Why this matters (ITC). In plain terms, when you fit lm(y ~ x) and read off the standard errors, Gauss-Markov says you are getting the best you can do among linear unbiased estimators using only the two assumptions G1 and G2, with no appeal to normality. The result also travels: matching-adjusted indirect comparison (Chapter 18) replaces \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) with a weighted version, and the same Gauss-Markov logic, applied to the weighted (generalized least squares) estimator of Exercise 6.8, is what certifies that the reweighted estimator is optimal under its own assumptions.

Remark. The proof uses only G1 and G2. Normality (G3) is not needed for Gauss-Markov optimality. The price of the restriction to linear estimators is real: under non-normal errors there can be nonlinear unbiased estimators that beat OLS. Under normality, however, no such estimator exists, even nonlinear ones; we prove this stronger statement in Corollary 6.2. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

To use Equation 6.5 in practice we must estimate the unknown \(\sigma^2\). The natural estimator divides the residual sum of squares not by \(n\) but by \(n-p\), the dimension of the residual space; this exact divisor makes the estimator unbiased.

Remark. The trace trick. Two facts from Chapter 1 are used repeatedly below. First, a \(1\times 1\) matrix equals its own trace, so any scalar can be written \(a=\mathrm{tr}(a)\). Second, the trace is linear and cyclic: \(\mathrm{tr}(BC)=\mathrm{tr}(CB)\) whenever both products are defined. Together they let us pull a random vector out of a quadratic form and replace the expectation by a covariance matrix, which is the key maneuver in the next proof and again in Theorem 6.6. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 6.4 (Unbiased estimation of the error variance) Under Definition 6.1 with G1, G2 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\) (and \(n>p\)), the statistic \[ s^2=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-p}=\frac{\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2}{n-p}=\frac{\mathbf{y}^\top M\mathbf{y}}{n-p} \] is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\): \(\mathbb{E}[s^2]=\sigma^2\).

Proof. First, \(\mathrm{RSS}=\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{r}=(M\mathbf{y})^\top(M\mathbf{y}) =\mathbf{y}^\top M^\top M\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}^\top M\mathbf{y}\) by the symmetry and idempotence of \(M\) (Lemma 6.2). Substitute \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) and use \(M\mathbf{X}=0\): \[ \mathbf{y}^\top M\mathbf{y} =(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})^\top M(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) =\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \] the terms involving \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) vanishing because \(M\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{0}\) and \((\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top M=(M\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top=\mathbf{0}^\top\). Now take the expectation of the scalar \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) using the trace trick: a scalar equals its own trace, and the trace is linear and cyclic, so \[ \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] =\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\big] =\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{tr}(M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top)\big] =\mathrm{tr}\big(M\,\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top]\big) =\mathrm{tr}(M\,\sigma^2 I_n)=\sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}(M)=\sigma^2(n-p), \] using \(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top] =\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\sigma^2 I_n\) from G1, G2 and \(\mathrm{tr}(M)=n-p\) from Lemma 6.2. Dividing by \(n-p\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[s^2]=\sigma^2\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. \(s^2\) is the number reported as the residual variance (its square root, the residual standard error, appears in every regression printout), and it feeds every standard error and every \(t\) and \(F\) statistic later in the chapter. Getting its expectation exactly right, via the divisor \(n-p\) rather than \(n\), is what makes those downstream tests unbiased.

Remark. The divisor \(n-p\) has a clean geometric reading: the residual lives in the \((n-p)\)-dimensional space \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\), so although there are \(n\) residuals they satisfy \(p\) linear constraints (the normal equations), leaving \(n-p\) free coordinates. Each contributes one “degree of freedom” of variance. We make this exact in Theorem 6.6. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

6.5 Normal-theory distribution results

To build confidence intervals and tests we need the full sampling distribution of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and \(s^2\), not only their first two moments. Here we invoke G3. We first record two facts about the multivariate normal that we use repeatedly; both are proved with the moment generating function (Definition 4.7), whose value for \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)\) is \(M_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{t})=\exp\!\big(\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}^\top\Sigma \mathbf{t}\big)\), and which determines the distribution uniquely (both facts are established in Chapter 4).

Treat the moment generating function as a fingerprint of a distribution. Two facts from Chapter 4 are all we need, and we do not reprove them. First, the fingerprint determines the distribution: if a random vector’s moment generating function matches that of a known normal, then it is that normal. Second, the joint fingerprint factors into a product of separate fingerprints exactly when the pieces are independent. These are the only two properties of the moment generating function we exploit below.

6.5.1 Two facts about the multivariate normal we will reuse

Lemma 6.3 (Affine closure of the multivariate normal) If \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)\), \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m\), then \(A\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}\sim\mathcal{N}_m(A\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b},A\Sigma A^\top)\).

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{w}=A\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}\). For \(\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^m\), \[ M_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mathbf{s}^\top(A\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b})}\big] =e^{\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{(A^\top\mathbf{s})^\top\mathbf{z}}\big] =e^{\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{b}}\,M_{\mathbf{z}}(A^\top\mathbf{s}) =e^{\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{b}}\exp\!\Big((A^\top\mathbf{s})^\top\boldsymbol{\mu} +\tfrac12(A^\top\mathbf{s})^\top\Sigma(A^\top\mathbf{s})\Big). \] Collecting terms, the exponent equals \(\mathbf{s}^\top(A\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b}) +\tfrac12\mathbf{s}^\top(A\Sigma A^\top)\mathbf{s}\), so \(M_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s})=\exp\!\big(\mathbf{s}^\top(A\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b}) +\tfrac12\mathbf{s}^\top(A\Sigma A^\top)\mathbf{s}\big)\), which is the moment generating function of \(\mathcal{N}_m(A\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b},A\Sigma A^\top)\). By uniqueness of the moment generating function, \(\mathbf{w}\) has that distribution. \(\square\)

Lemma 6.4 (Uncorrelated Gaussian blocks are independent) Partition \(\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2)\) jointly multivariate normal. If \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2)=0\) then \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(\mathbf{u}_2\) are independent. Consequently, measurable functions \(f(\mathbf{u}_1)\) and \(g(\mathbf{u}_2)\) are independent.

Proof. Write \(\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1,\boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\) and, because the cross-covariance is zero, the block-diagonal \(\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{11},\Sigma_{22})\). For \(\mathbf{t}=(\mathbf{t}_1,\mathbf{t}_2)\), \[ M_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{t}) =\exp\!\Big(\mathbf{t}_1^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\mathbf{t}_2^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 +\tfrac12\mathbf{t}_1^\top\Sigma_{11}\mathbf{t}_1+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}_2^\top\Sigma_{22}\mathbf{t}_2\Big) =M_{\mathbf{u}_1}(\mathbf{t}_1)\,M_{\mathbf{u}_2}(\mathbf{t}_2), \] the joint moment generating function factoring into the product of the marginal ones (each marginal is normal by Lemma 6.3 with \(A\) the appropriate coordinate projection). Factorization of the joint moment generating function into the product of the marginals is equivalent to independence (Chapter 4). Independence of \(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\) implies independence of any measurable functions \(f(\mathbf{u}_1),g(\mathbf{u}_2)\). \(\square\)

6.5.2 The three distributional pillars

We can now read off the three pillars of normal regression theory. They are: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is itself normal (the location information), the scaled residual sum of squares is chi-squared (the scale information), and the two are independent (they do not interfere). Each is one line once the two facts above are in hand.

Theorem 6.5 (Normality of the OLS estimator) Under Definition 6.1 with G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sim\mathcal{N}_p\big(\boldsymbol{\beta},\,\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\big). \]

Proof. By G3, \(\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2 I_n)\). The estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=A\mathbf{y}\) with \(A=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\) is an affine (here, linear) function of \(\mathbf{y}\). By Lemma 6.3, \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sim\mathcal{N}_p\big(A\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\,\sigma^2 A A^\top\big). \] We computed \(A\mathbf{X}=I_p\) and \(\sigma^2 AA^\top=\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) in Theorem 6.2, giving the claim. The mean and covariance agree with Equation 6.5, as they must; normality is the new information. \(\square\)

Why this matters. Knowing \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is exactly normal (not merely approximately) is what lets us attach a confidence interval to a treatment effect from a single trial of finite size, without appealing to large-sample approximation. It is the first ingredient of the \(t\) statistic in Theorem 6.8.

The next theorem is the distributional heart of the chapter. Before stating it we isolate the linear-algebra fact that is its true crux, so it has a name we can point to.

Lemma 6.5 (Rank of a symmetric idempotent equals its trace) Let \(K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric and idempotent, \(K=K^\top\) and \(K^2=K\). Then every eigenvalue of \(K\) is \(0\) or \(1\), and the number of unit eigenvalues equals both \(\mathrm{rank}(K)\) and \(\mathrm{tr}(K)\); in particular \(\mathrm{rank}(K)=\mathrm{tr}(K)\).

Proof. By the spectral theorem (Theorem 3.2), \(K=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) for an orthogonal \(Q\) and a diagonal \(\Lambda\) holding the eigenvalues. Idempotence gives \(Q\Lambda Q^\top=K=K^2=Q\Lambda^2 Q^\top\), so \(\Lambda^2=\Lambda\); entry by entry \(\lambda_i^2=\lambda_i\), forcing \(\lambda_i\in\{0,1\}\). The rank of \(K\) equals the number of nonzero eigenvalues (an orthogonal change of basis is invertible and preserves rank), which is the number of ones; the trace is basis-independent and equals the sum of the eigenvalues, again the number of ones. Hence \(\mathrm{rank}(K)=\mathrm{tr}(K)\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. For a projection matrix, counting dimensions and summing diagonal entries give the same number. This is why \(\mathrm{tr}(M)=n-p\) already told us the residual space has dimension \(n-p\), and it is exactly what fixes the chi-squared degrees of freedom below.

The argument now diagonalizes the residual maker by the spectral theorem and reduces the residual sum of squares to a sum of independent squared standard normals.

Roadmap of the proof. The plan has four moves. (1) Write \(\mathrm{RSS}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). (2) Diagonalize the symmetric idempotent \(M\), whose eigenvalues are \(0\) or \(1\) with exactly \(n-p\) ones (Lemma 6.5). (3) Rotate the noise by the orthogonal eigenvector matrix; rotation keeps isotropic Gaussian noise standard normal. (4) In the rotated coordinates the residual sum of squares is just the sum of the \(n-p\) squared coordinates along the unit eigenvalues, which is by definition a \(\chi^2_{n-p}\).

Theorem 6.6 (Chi-squared distribution of the scaled residual sum of squares) Under Definition 6.1 with G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\) (and \(n>p\)), \[ \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\sigma^2}=\frac{(n-p)s^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{\,n-p}. \]

Proof. As in Theorem 6.4, \(\mathrm{RSS}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). The matrix \(M\) is symmetric, so by the spectral theorem (Theorem 3.2) there is an orthogonal matrix \(Q\) (\(Q^\top Q=QQ^\top=I_n\)) and a diagonal matrix \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\) with \(M=Q\Lambda Q^\top\). By Lemma 6.5 each eigenvalue is \(0\) or \(1\), and the number of unit eigenvalues equals \(\mathrm{rank}(M)=\mathrm{tr}(M)=n-p\). This is precisely why there are exactly \(n-p\) ones: the residual maker projects onto the \((n-p)\)-dimensional residual space. Reorder the eigenvalues so that \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(I_{n-p},0)\), with the \(n-p\) ones first.

Define \(\mathbf{z}=\sigma^{-1}Q^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2 I_n)\), Lemma 6.3 gives \[ \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}_n\big(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma^{-2}Q^\top(\sigma^2 I_n)Q\big) =\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{0},Q^\top Q)=\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{0},I_n), \] so the coordinates \(z_1,\dots,z_n\) are independent standard normal. From the definition of \(\mathbf{z}\) we have \(Q^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\sigma\mathbf{z}\), so substituting \(M=Q\Lambda Q^\top\), \[ \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\sigma^2} =\frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\sigma^2} =\frac{(Q^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})^\top\Lambda(Q^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})}{\sigma^2} =\frac{(\sigma\mathbf{z})^\top\Lambda(\sigma\mathbf{z})}{\sigma^2} =\mathbf{z}^\top\Lambda\mathbf{z}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-p} z_i^2 . \] A sum of \(n-p\) independent squared standard normal variables has, by definition, the chi-squared distribution on \(n-p\) degrees of freedom (Chapter 4). \(\square\)

Recap. The rotation \(Q\) turns the noise into \(n\) independent standard-normal coordinates and turns \(M\) into a “keep the first \(n-p\) directions, discard the other \(p\)” operator. The residual sum of squares keeps only the kept coordinates, so it is a sum of \(n-p\) squared independent standard normals.

   R^n  =  C(X)               +   C(X)^perp
           p directions            n-p directions
           (fitted lives here)     (residual lives here)

   rotate by Q:   noise -> n independent N(0,1) coordinates
   RSS / sigma^2  =  sum of the n-p squared coordinates in C(X)^perp  ->  chi^2_{n-p}

Why this matters. This theorem supplies the reference distribution for every variance-based test: the \(n-p\) denominator degrees of freedom in every \(t\) and \(F\) statistic later in the chapter trace back to the dimension of the residual space counted here.

Theorem 6.7 (Independence of the estimator and the variance estimate) Under Definition 6.1 with G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), the random vector \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and the statistic \(s^2\) are independent. More generally \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is independent of the entire residual vector \(\mathbf{r}\).

Proof. Stack the fitted values and residuals into one affine function of \(\mathbf{y}\): \[ \begin{pmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{y}}\\ \mathbf{r}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}H\\ M\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}. \] Since \(\mathbf{y}\) is multivariate normal, Lemma 6.3 makes \((\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{r})\) jointly multivariate normal. Their cross-covariance is \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{r}) =H\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{y})\,M^\top =\sigma^2 H M=\sigma^2 H(I_n-H)=\sigma^2(H-H^2)=0, \] using \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{y})=\sigma^2 I_n\) and \(H^2=H\) (Lemma 6.2). By Lemma 6.4, \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) and \(\mathbf{r}\) are independent. Now \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} =(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top H\mathbf{y} =(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), using \(\mathbf{X}^\top H=\mathbf{X}^\top\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is a function of \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) alone; and \(s^2=\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2/(n-p)\) is a function of \(\mathbf{r}\) alone. Functions of independent random vectors are independent (Lemma 6.4), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and \(s^2\) are independent, as is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) from all of \(\mathbf{r}\). \(\square\)

Remark. The independence is special to the Gaussian case and is what makes exact finite-sample inference possible. Geometrically, the orthogonal decomposition \(\mathbf{y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{r}\) splits the isotropic Gaussian noise into its components in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) and \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\); the rotational symmetry of the isotropic normal makes those components independent. The location information (\(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\)) and the scale information (\(s^2\)) do not interfere. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

6.6 Exact inference: the t and F tests

We assemble the pieces into pivotal quantities: functions of the data and one parameter whose sampling distribution is completely known, free of every unknown parameter. A pivot is what turns an estimate into a test or an interval, because we can tabulate its quantiles once and for all. Recall from Chapter 4 the two constructions: if \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(W\sim\chi^2_m\) are independent then \(Z/\sqrt{W/m}\sim t_m\) (Student’s \(t\) on \(m\) degrees of freedom); and if \(W_1\sim\chi^2_{m_1}\) and \(W_2\sim\chi^2_{m_2}\) are independent then \((W_1/m_1)/(W_2/m_2)\sim F_{m_1,m_2}\) (Snedecor’s \(F\)).

Theorem 6.8 (The t statistic for a linear contrast) Assume Definition 6.1 with G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), \(n>p\). Fix \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^p\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\). Then \[ T=\frac{\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}} {s\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}}}\sim t_{\,n-p}. \tag{6.8}\] In particular, taking \(\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{e}_j\) gives the per-coefficient statistic \(T_j=(\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)/\big(s\sqrt{[(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}\big)\sim t_{\,n-p}\), and a level \(1-\alpha\) confidence interval for \(\beta_j\) is \(\hat\beta_j\pm t_{n-p,1-\alpha/2}\,s\sqrt{[(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}\).

Proof. By Theorem 6.5 and the linear-image rule for the normal (the scalar case of Lemma 6.3), \(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}\big)\). The denominator variance is positive because \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) is positive definite (Lemma 6.1) and \(\mathbf{c}\ne\mathbf{0}\). Standardize: \[ Z=\frac{\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}} {\sigma\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). \] By Theorem 6.6, \(W=\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2=(n-p)s^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-p}\). By Theorem 6.7, \(Z\) (a function of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\)) is independent of \(W\) (a function of \(s^2\)). Therefore \[ \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/(n-p)}}\sim t_{\,n-p}. \] Substituting \(W/(n-p)=s^2/\sigma^2\), the unknown \(\sigma\) cancels: \[ \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/(n-p)}} =\frac{(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta})\big/ \big(\sigma\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}}\big)}{s/\sigma} =\frac{\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}} {s\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}}}=T, \] which is Equation 6.8. The confidence interval is the set of \(\beta_j\) for which \(|T_j|\le t_{n-p,1-\alpha/2}\), obtained by inverting the two-sided test. \(\square\)

Remark. Reading the quantile notation. The symbol \(t_{n-p,1-\alpha/2}\) is the number with probability \(1-\alpha/2\) to its left under the \(t_{n-p}\) curve; for a \(95\%\) interval (\(\alpha=0.05\)) it cuts off the top \(2.5\%\) of the curve, so the interval misses the truth at most \(5\%\) of the time. It is the qt(1 - alpha/2, n - p) of statistical software. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Why this matters (health reading). The per-coefficient statistic \(T_j\) is exactly the \(t\) value a regression table prints for each covariate, and \(\hat\beta_j\pm t_{n-p,1-\alpha/2}\,s\sqrt{[(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}\) is its confidence interval. Choosing \(\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{e}_T\), the treatment column, gives the test and interval for the adjusted treatment effect, the headline number of a trial analysis.

For testing several coefficients at once we compare nested models. Let \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_0)\subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_1)\) be the column spaces of a reduced design \(\mathbf{X}_0\) (with \(p_0\) columns, full rank) and a full design \(\mathbf{X}_1\) (with \(p_1>p_0\) columns, full rank, \(n>p_1\)), with hat matrices \(H_0,H_1\), residual makers \(M_0,M_1\), and residual sums of squares \(\mathrm{RSS}_0,\mathrm{RSS}_1\).

Theorem 6.9 (The F statistic for nested models) Assume G1, G2, G3 and that the reduced model holds, that is, \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_0)\). With \(q=p_1-p_0\), \[ F=\frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_0-\mathrm{RSS}_1)/q}{\mathrm{RSS}_1/(n-p_1)}\sim F_{\,q,\;n-p_1}. \tag{6.9}\]

Roadmap of the proof. Two nested models differ by \(q=p_1-p_0\) extra columns. The drop in residual sum of squares, \(\mathrm{RSS}_0-\mathrm{RSS}_1\), measures what those extra columns explain. We show this drop equals \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) for a projection \(P\) of rank \(q\) onto the “extra” directions, that \(\mathrm{RSS}_1=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M_1 \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) lives on the orthogonal residual directions, and that the two are independent chi-squares; their scaled ratio is then an \(F\) by construction.

Proof. Because \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_0)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_1)\), projecting onto the smaller space and then the larger leaves the smaller projection unchanged: \(H_1H_0=H_0H_1=H_0\). (Indeed \(H_1H_0\) projects each column of \(H_0\), which already lies in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_0)\subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_1)\), onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_1)\), returning it unchanged; symmetry gives the other equality.) Define the difference \(P=H_1-H_0\). Then \(P\) is symmetric and \[ P^2=(H_1-H_0)^2=H_1-H_1H_0-H_0H_1+H_0=H_1-H_0-H_0+H_0=H_1-H_0=P, \] where the four-term expansion used \(H_1^2=H_1\), \(H_0^2=H_0\), and the identity \(H_1H_0=H_0H_1=H_0\) just established. So \(P\) is symmetric and idempotent, hence an orthogonal projection, and by Lemma 6.5 its rank equals its trace, \(\mathrm{rank}(P)=\mathrm{tr}(P)=\mathrm{tr}(H_1)-\mathrm{tr}(H_0)=p_1-p_0=q\). Moreover \(P\) and \(M_1=I-H_1\) are orthogonal projections whose product is zero: \[ PM_1=(H_1-H_0)(I-H_1)=H_1-H_1^2-H_0+H_0H_1=H_1-H_1-H_0+H_0=0 . \] Because \(P\) and \(M_1\) are symmetric projections with \(PM_1=0\), their ranges are orthogonal subspaces of \(\mathbb{R}^n\); this orthogonality is exactly the condition that, applied to Gaussian noise, will make the two pieces \(P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) and \(M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) independent below. Now decompose the numerator. Using \(\mathrm{RSS}_k=\mathbf{y}^\top M_k\mathbf{y}\), \[ \mathrm{RSS}_0-\mathrm{RSS}_1=\mathbf{y}^\top(M_0-M_1)\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}^\top(H_1-H_0)\mathbf{y} =\mathbf{y}^\top P\mathbf{y}. \] Write \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}_0\boldsymbol{\beta}_0+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) under the reduced model. Because \(P\mathbf{X}_0=H_1\mathbf{X}_0-H_0\mathbf{X}_0=\mathbf{X}_0-\mathbf{X}_0=0\) (both projections fix \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}_0)\)), we get \(P\mathbf{y}=P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) and likewise \(M_1\mathbf{y}=M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). Hence \[ \mathrm{RSS}_0-\mathrm{RSS}_1=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \qquad \mathrm{RSS}_1=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \] By the spectral argument of Theorem 6.6 applied to the idempotent \(P\) of rank \(q\) and to \(M_1\) of rank \(n-p_1\), \[ \frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_q, \qquad \frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-p_1}. \] For independence, stack \(P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) and \(M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\); they are jointly normal (Lemma 6.3) with cross-covariance \(\mathrm{Cov}(P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\sigma^2 PM_1=0\), so by Lemma 6.4 the two random vectors are independent, and therefore so are the quadratic forms \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) built from them. The ratio of independent chi-squared variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom, is \[ F=\frac{(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top P\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}/\sigma^2)/q} {(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top M_1\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}/\sigma^2)/(n-p_1)} =\frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_0-\mathrm{RSS}_1)/q}{\mathrm{RSS}_1/(n-p_1)}\sim F_{\,q,\;n-p_1}, \] the unknown \(\sigma^2\) cancelling. \(\square\)

Remark. Taking \(\mathbf{X}_0=\mathbf{1}_n\) (intercept only, \(p_0=1\)) tests the global null that all slopes are zero. Then \(H_0\mathbf{y}=\bar y\,\mathbf{1}_n\), \(H_1\mathbf{y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), and the numerator quadratic form is \(\|(H_1-H_0)\mathbf{y}\|_2^2=\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2 =\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}\), so \[ F=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}/(p-1)}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)}\sim F_{\,p-1,\;n-p} \] under the null. When \(q=1\) the \(F\) statistic equals the square of the corresponding \(t\) statistic, \(F=T^2\), and \(F_{1,m}\) is the law of the square of a \(t_m\) variable; the two tests then agree. The general linear hypothesis \(H_0:R\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{q}\) for a full-row-rank \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}\) gives an algebraically equivalent statistic, which we state and prove as Theorem 6.10 below because it is the form practitioners actually use. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Why this matters (health reading). The global-null \(F\) asks whether the regressors together explain more than chance: in the running trial, whether treatment and baseline severity jointly beat an intercept-only model. The “F-statistic” line of a regression printout is exactly this test. The nested-model view also matches how covariates are added to a model one block at a time, each block tested by the drop in residual sum of squares it produces.

The nested-model \(F\) test of Theorem 6.9 is often more convenient to express directly in terms of a restriction on the coefficients. The next theorem gives that form. It is worth a full statement, not a passing remark, because it is the test computed by routines such as linearHypothesis() in R.

Theorem 6.10 (The general linear hypothesis) Assume Definition 6.1 with G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), \(n>p\). Let \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}\) have full row rank \(q\le p\), and let \(\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^q\). Under the null hypothesis \(H_0:R\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{q}\), \[ F=\frac{(R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{q})^\top\big[R(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}R^\top\big]^{-1} (R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{q})}{q\,s^2}\sim F_{q,\,n-p}. \tag{6.10}\]

Proof. Write \(A=R(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}R^\top\). Since \(R\) has full row rank and \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) is positive definite (Lemma 6.1), \(A\) is a \(q\times q\) positive definite, hence invertible, matrix. By Theorem 6.5 and affine closure (Lemma 6.3), \(R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sim\mathcal{N}_q(R\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2 A)\), so under \(H_0\) the vector \(\mathbf{v}:=R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{q}\sim\mathcal{N}_q(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 A)\).

We now whiten \(\mathbf{v}\), that is, transform it to have the identity covariance. Factor \(A=LL^\top\) (for example the Cholesky factor, or \(A^{1/2}\) from the spectral theorem, Theorem 3.2) and set \(\mathbf{u}=\sigma^{-1}L^{-1}\mathbf{v}\). By Lemma 6.3, \[ \mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{N}_q\big(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma^{-2}L^{-1}(\sigma^2 A)L^{-\top}\big) =\mathcal{N}_q\big(\mathbf{0},\,L^{-1}LL^\top L^{-\top}\big)=\mathcal{N}_q(\mathbf{0},I_q), \] so the coordinates of \(\mathbf{u}\) are independent standard normals and \[ \frac{\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{v}}{\sigma^2}=\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} =\sum_{i=1}^q u_i^2\sim\chi^2_q . \] By Theorem 6.6, \(W:=(n-p)s^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-p}\), and by Theorem 6.7 the numerator (a function of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\)) is independent of \(s^2\). Hence \[ \frac{\big(\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{v}/\sigma^2\big)/q}{W/(n-p)} =\frac{\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{v}}{q\,s^2} =\frac{(R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{q})^\top A^{-1}(R\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathbf{q})}{q\,s^2} \sim F_{q,\,n-p}, \] the unknown \(\sigma^2\) cancelling. This is Equation 6.10. \(\square\)

Why this matters (ITC). This statistic tests any set of \(q\) linear restrictions at once, for example “treatment has no effect at any dose” or “two treatment contrasts are equal.” In network meta-analysis, joint restrictions on treatment contrasts are exactly the hypotheses of interest. Exercise 6.9 asks you to check that Equation 6.10 coincides with the nested-model statistic Equation 6.9.

6.7 Decomposition of variance and the coefficient of determination

The Pythagorean identity of Proposition 6.1, applied to the centered response, splits the total variability of \(\mathbf{y}\) into a part explained by the regression and a residual part. This is the analysis of variance, and it gives the most-quoted summary of fit.

Proposition 6.2 (Analysis-of-variance decomposition) Assume the model contains an intercept, \(\mathbf{1}_n\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\). Then the fitted values and residuals are orthogonal after centering, and \[ \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\bar y)^2}_{\mathrm{TSS}} =\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i-\bar y)^2}_{\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}} +\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat y_i)^2}_{\mathrm{RSS}} . \tag{6.11}\]

Proof. Center the identity \(\mathbf{y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{r}\) by subtracting \(\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\): \[ \mathbf{y}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n=(\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n)+\mathbf{r}, \] where we used \(\overline{\hat y}=\bar y\), valid because the model has an intercept (Proposition 6.1). The two summands on the right are orthogonal: the residual \(\mathbf{r}=M\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\), while \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) because both \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=H\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{1}_n\) lie in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\). Hence \((\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n)^\top\mathbf{r}=0\), and the Pythagorean theorem yields \[ \|\mathbf{y}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2 =\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2+\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2, \] which is Equation 6.11 in vector form. \(\square\)

The decomposition is a right triangle on the centered vectors, with the right angle at \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\).

              y
             /|
            / |
   TSS    /   |  RSS            TSS     = squared length of the hypotenuse
  (hyp)  /    |  (leg)          ESS_reg = squared length of the horizontal leg
        /     |                 RSS     = squared length of the vertical leg
       /______|

Reader translation. Total variation in the response splits into the part the model explains (\(\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}\)) plus the part it cannot (\(\mathrm{RSS}\)), and because the two are at right angles, the split is exact (no cross term). \(R^2\), defined next, is simply the explained fraction of the hypotenuse, so a larger \(R^2\) means the explained leg is the longer share of the triangle.

Definition 6.5 (Coefficient of determination) For a model with intercept and \(\mathrm{TSS}>0\), the coefficient of determination is \[ R^2=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}}{\mathrm{TSS}}=1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}, \] the fraction of the total (centered) variability of the response explained by the regression. The two expressions are equal by Proposition 6.2. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. \(R^2\) is the share of the response’s spread the model accounts for: \(R^2=0.64\) means the regressors explain \(64\%\) of the variability and \(36\%\) is residual scatter. It measures fit, not correctness or causation; a high \(R^2\) does not make a treatment-effect estimate unbiased, and a low one does not make it wrong.

Corollary 6.1 (Range and correlation interpretation of R squared) For a model with intercept, \(0\le R^2\le 1\). The value \(R^2=1\) occurs exactly when all residuals vanish (\(\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\)) and \(R^2=0\) exactly when \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\) (the regressors add nothing beyond the intercept). In simple linear regression (\(\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{1}_n\ \mathbf{x}]\), \(p=2\)), \(R^2=\rho_{xy}^2\), the square of the sample correlation \(\rho_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\big/\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}\).

Proof. From Equation 6.11, \(\mathrm{TSS}=\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}+\mathrm{RSS}\) with both summands nonnegative, so \(0\le\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}\le\mathrm{TSS}\) and dividing by \(\mathrm{TSS}>0\) gives \(0\le R^2\le1\). The boundary cases are read directly from \(R^2=1-\mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\) and \(R^2=\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}/\mathrm{TSS}\).

For simple linear regression, the slope estimator is \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) with \(S_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\) and \(S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\) (the specialization of Equation 6.2 to \(p=2\); see Exercise 6.4). Since \(\hat y_i-\bar y=\hat\beta_1(x_i-\bar x)\), \[ \mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2=\hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx}=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}}, \qquad R^2=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}}{\mathrm{TSS}}=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}=\rho_{xy}^2, \] with \(S_{yy}=\mathrm{TSS}=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2\). The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Theorem 2.1) applied to the centered vectors \(\mathbf{x}-\bar x\mathbf{1}_n\) and \(\mathbf{y}-\bar y\mathbf{1}_n\) gives \(S_{xy}^2\le S_{xx}S_{yy}\), confirming \(0\le\rho_{xy}^2\le 1\) once more. \(\square\)

6.8 Least squares is maximum likelihood

We have introduced least squares as a geometric and decision-theoretic object, and shown it is best in two senses (closest point; BLUE). Under normal errors there is a third, statistical justification that many find the most compelling: minimizing the sum of squares is exactly maximizing the likelihood. This is the answer to “why squares” promised in the introduction: squaring is the price of assuming the noise is Gaussian. Had we instead assumed Laplace-shaped (double-exponential) noise, the likelihood would reward absolute residuals, and the estimator would minimize the sum of absolute deviations. The squared criterion is thus a modeling choice about the shape of the noise, now made explicit.

6.8.1 Ordinary least squares is the maximum likelihood estimator

Theorem 6.11 (Least squares is maximum likelihood under normal errors) Assume Definition 6.1 with G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), \(n>p\), treating \((\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2)\) as unknown parameters. The maximum likelihood estimators are \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{MLE}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} =\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}, \qquad \hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n}. \] That is, the maximum likelihood estimator of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is precisely the ordinary least-squares estimator. (The maximum likelihood estimator of \(\sigma^2\) uses divisor \(n\), not \(n-p\), and is therefore biased downward; compare Theorem 6.4.)

Proof. Under G3 the density of \(\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2 I_n)\) is \((2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\!\big(-\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2/(2\sigma^2)\big)\), so the log-likelihood is \[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) =-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\log\sigma^2 -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 . \tag{6.12}\] For any fixed \(\sigma^2>0\), the only place \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) enters Equation 6.12 is through the term \(-\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2/(2\sigma^2)\), which is increasing in \(-S(\boldsymbol{\beta})\). Maximizing \(\ell\) over \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is therefore identical to minimizing \(S(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2\), whose unique minimizer is the OLS estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\) by Theorem 6.1. Crucially, this minimizing \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is the same for every fixed \(\sigma^2>0\): the factor \(1/(2\sigma^2)\) only rescales the term it multiplies and does not move the location of the minimum. Because the best \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is the same whatever \(\sigma^2\) is, that same \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) must be the \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)-coordinate of the joint maximizer over \((\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2)\). Substituting \(\boldsymbol{\beta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) back into \(\ell\) leaves a function of \(\sigma^2\) alone, the profile log-likelihood (the likelihood with the other parameter concentrated out at its optimal value), \[ \ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^2) =-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\log\sigma^2-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{2\sigma^2}, \] where \(\mathrm{RSS}=\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_2^2>0\) almost surely (it is zero only on the measure-zero event \(\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\)). Differentiate in \(\sigma^2\): \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma^2}\,\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^2) =-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{2\sigma^4} =\frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\big(\mathrm{RSS}-n\sigma^2\big), \] which is positive for \(\sigma^2<\mathrm{RSS}/n\), zero at \(\sigma^2=\mathrm{RSS}/n\), and negative for \(\sigma^2>\mathrm{RSS}/n\). Hence \(\sigma^2\mapsto\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^2)\) increases then decreases, with a unique global maximum at \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\mathrm{RSS}/n\). Therefore \((\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\mathrm{RSS}/n)\) maximizes \(\ell\) jointly. \(\square\)

Why this matters. This is the third of the four optimality principles from the introduction, and the one that justifies the squared-error criterion by name: under Gaussian errors, least squares is the maximum likelihood estimator, not an arbitrary choice. It also hooks regression into the general machinery of Chapter 5, so the large-sample guarantees of maximum likelihood apply to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) automatically.

6.8.2 Cramér-Rao efficiency

Under normality we can say more than Gauss-Markov: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is optimal not merely among linear unbiased estimators but among all unbiased estimators, because it attains the Cramér-Rao lower bound (Theorem 5.7).

Corollary 6.2 (OLS attains the Cramér-Rao bound under normality) Assume G1, G2, G3 and \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=p\), with \(\sigma^2\) known. The Fisher information (Definition 5.7) for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}/\sigma^2\). Consequently, for every fixed \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^p\), any unbiased estimator \(T\) of \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\) satisfies \(\mathrm{Var}(T)\ge\sigma^2\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c} =\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\). The OLS estimator \(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is therefore the uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator of \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

Proof. With \(\sigma^2\) known, drop the additive constants in Equation 6.12 that do not involve \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\); the score is \[ \mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,\ell =\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}), \] with Hessian \(\nabla^2_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ell=-\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}/\sigma^2\), a constant matrix. The Fisher information is minus the expected Hessian, so \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}/\sigma^2\). The Cramér-Rao inequality (Theorem 5.7), in its form for a smooth scalar functional \(\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}) =\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\) with gradient \(\mathbf{c}\), gives for any unbiased \(T\) \[ \mathrm{Var}(T)\ge\mathbf{c}^\top\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{-1}\mathbf{c} =\sigma^2\,\mathbf{c}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{c}. \] By Theorem 6.2 the right-hand side equals \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\), so the bound is attained by OLS, which is unbiased; no unbiased estimator can do better. The same conclusion holds when \(\sigma^2\) is unknown: the information matrix for \((\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2)\) is block diagonal, because \(\partial^2\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\partial\sigma^2 =-\sigma^{-4}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})\) has expectation \(\mathbf{0}\) under G1. A block-diagonal matrix is inverted block by block, so the \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)-block of the inverse information is simply the inverse of the \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)-block of the information, namely \(\sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\). In words: not knowing \(\sigma^2\) costs nothing for estimating \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), so the same lower bound persists. \(\square\)

Why this matters. This is the strongest optimality statement in the chapter, the fourth principle of the introduction. Gauss-Markov ruled out only linear rivals; Cramér-Rao rules out all unbiased rivals, even nonlinear ones, once we assume normality. For a health researcher the practical reading is that under its assumptions there is no cleverer unbiased way to estimate a treatment effect than ordinary least squares; effort is better spent checking the assumptions than seeking a better estimator.

Remark. Three optimality theorems now point to the same estimator. Gauss-Markov (Theorem 6.3) selects OLS among linear unbiased estimators using only the first two moments; the projection theorem selects it as the closest point in the model space using only geometry; Corollary 6.2 selects it among all unbiased estimators under the added assumption of normality. When the errors are heavy-tailed or the model is misspecified these justifications weaken, and the robust sandwich variance of Definition 5.11, or estimators based on other loss functions, become relevant. The large-sample guarantees of Theorem 5.6 apply to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) as a maximum likelihood estimator and extend the normal-theory exact results to settings where G3 holds only approximately. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

6.9 Rank deficiency and the pseudoinverse

When the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly dependent, for example because a categorical predictor is coded with one indicator per level together with an intercept, \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=q<p\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is singular. The formula Equation 6.3 no longer applies, but the geometry survives intact: the fitted values are still the unique projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), and only the coordinate representation \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is non-unique. The clean device for selecting a canonical representative is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse \(\mathbf{X}^{+}\) (Definition 3.11), built from the singular value decomposition (Theorem 3.7).

The four fundamental subspaces (Theorem 1.5) each acquire a regression meaning, summarized here. The two on the left of the table live in observation space \(\mathbb{R}^n\); the two on the right live in coefficient space \(\mathbb{R}^p\) and govern identifiability.

Subspace Where Dimension Regression meaning
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), column space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\) where fitted values \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) live (the model space)
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\) \(\mathbb{R}^n\) \(n-\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\) where residuals \(\mathbf{r}\) live
\(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\), null space \(\mathbb{R}^p\) \(p-\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\) directions in which \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is undetermined (non-identifiable)
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\), row space \(\mathbb{R}^p\) \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\) directions \(\mathbf{c}\) along which \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is estimable

The last row gives a name to the central concept of this section.

Definition 6.6 (Estimable functional) A linear functional \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is estimable if it takes the same value for every least-squares solution \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) of the normal equations Equation 6.2. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. When the design is rank deficient, infinitely many coefficient vectors fit the data equally well, so an individual coefficient can be meaningless on its own: it changes from one solution to another. An estimable functional is a combination that does have a single well-defined value, whichever solution the software returns. Proposition 6.3 shows these are exactly the combinations \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\) with \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\). A treatment contrast is typically estimable even when the separate arm intercepts are not, which is why a difference between treatments is reportable from a design that cannot pin down each arm’s absolute level.

Proposition 6.3 (Rank deficiency and the minimum-norm solution) Let \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})=q\le p\). Then:

  1. The normal equations Equation 6.2 are consistent, and their solution set is the affine subspace \(\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0+\mathbf{v}:\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\}\) for any particular solution \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0\).
  2. The fitted values \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=P_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})}\mathbf{y}\) and the residual \(\mathbf{r}\) are the same for every solution; hence so is \(\mathrm{RSS}\), and a linear functional \(\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is invariant across solutions if and only if \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\) (these are exactly the estimable functionals of Definition 6.6).
  3. Among all minimizers, the one of smallest Euclidean norm is unique and equals \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}=\mathbf{X}^{+}\mathbf{y}\).

Proof.

  1. Consistency was shown in Theorem 6.1: \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\) by Lemma 6.1, so a solution exists. If \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_1\) both solve the normal equations then \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0)=\mathbf{0}\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}) =\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\) (Lemma 6.1); conversely adding any \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\) to a solution gives another solution. This is the stated affine subspace.

  2. The projection theorem (Theorem 2.5) gives a unique closest point \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} =P_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})}\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) regardless of coordinates; any solution \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) because solving the normal equations is equivalent to \(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) being that projection (the orthogonality \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\mathbf{0}\)). Hence \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) and \(\mathrm{RSS}=\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2\) are solution-independent. For a functional, \(\mathbf{c}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is invariant across solutions iff \(\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{v}=0\) for all \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\), that is iff \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})^\perp=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\) (Theorem 1.5).

  3. Take a (thin) singular value decomposition \(\mathbf{X}=U\Sigma V^\top\) (Theorem 3.7) with \(U\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q}\), \(V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\) having orthonormal columns and \(\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_q)\), \(\sigma_i>0\). The pseudoinverse is \(\mathbf{X}^{+}=V\Sigma^{-1}U^\top\), and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}=\mathbf{X}^{+}\mathbf{y}=V\Sigma^{-1}U^\top\mathbf{y} \in\mathcal{C}(V)=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\). One checks \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}\) solves the normal equations: \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}=V\Sigma^2 V^\top V\Sigma^{-1}U^\top\mathbf{y}=V\Sigma U^\top\mathbf{y} =\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\). Any other solution is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}+\mathbf{v}\) with \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})\) by part (1); since \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\perp\mathbf{v}\), Pythagoras gives \(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}+\mathbf{v}\|_2^2=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}\|_2^2+\|\mathbf{v}\|_2^2 \ge\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}\|_2^2\), with equality only at \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\). So \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}\) is the unique minimum-norm minimizer. \(\square\)

Why this matters (ITC). Estimability is the linear-algebra skeleton of identification in indirect comparison. A network of trials defines a design on the treatment contrasts, and which contrasts the network can estimate is exactly the estimability question of part (2). We return to it for network meta-analysis and population adjustment in Part III.

Remark. In the full-rank case \(q=p\) the null space is trivial, every functional is estimable, the affine solution set collapses to the single point Equation 6.3, and \(\mathbf{X}^{+}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top\), so Proposition 6.3 reduces to the earlier theory. The estimability criterion in part (2) is the rigorous statement of which contrasts a rank-deficient design can identify; in network meta-analysis and population adjustment the analogous question, which treatment contrasts a design renders estimable, is central, and reappears in Part III. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

6.10 A fully worked example

We carry a small data set through every formula so the reader can verify each number by hand. To give it a clinical reading, interpret \(x\) as a patient’s baseline disease severity score and \(y\) as the change in a continuous symptom outcome over follow-up, for \(n=5\) patients, fit with intercept (\(p=2\)): \[ \mathbf{x}=(1,2,3,4,5)^\top,\qquad \mathbf{y}=(1,3,2,5,4)^\top,\qquad \mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\\1&4\\1&5\end{pmatrix}. \]

Summary statistics. We have \(\sum x_i=15\), \(\sum x_i^2=55\), \(\sum y_i=15\), \(\sum x_iy_i=53\), hence \(\bar x=\bar y=3\), \(S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=10\), \(S_{yy}=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2=10\), and \(S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=8\).

The Gram matrix and its inverse. \[ \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}n&\sum x_i\\ \sum x_i&\sum x_i^2\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}5&15\\15&55\end{pmatrix},\quad \det(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})=5\cdot55-15^2=50,\quad (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}=\frac{1}{50}\begin{pmatrix}55&-15\\-15&5\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}1.1&-0.3\\-0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix}. \]

The estimate. With \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}=(\sum y_i,\sum x_iy_i)^\top=(15,53)^\top\), \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} =\begin{pmatrix}1.1&-0.3\\-0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}15\\53\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}1.1(15)-0.3(53)\\-0.3(15)+0.1(53)\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}16.5-15.9\\-4.5+5.3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.8\end{pmatrix}. \] Equivalently, by the simple-regression formulas, \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=8/10=0.8\) and \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x=3-0.8\cdot3=0.6\). The fitted line is \(\hat y=0.6+0.8x\). Clinically, the slope \(\hat\beta_1=0.8\) says each additional point of baseline severity is associated with a \(0.8\)-unit larger change in the outcome, and the intercept \(\hat\beta_0=0.6\) is the predicted change at a (hypothetical) severity of zero.

Fitted values and residuals. \[ \hat{\mathbf{y}}=(1.4,\,2.2,\,3.0,\,3.8,\,4.6)^\top,\qquad \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}=(-0.4,\,0.8,\,-1.0,\,1.2,\,-0.6)^\top. \] As Proposition 6.1 predicts, \(\sum_i r_i=0\) and \(\sum_i x_i r_i=0\) (the two normal equations); the reader can check both sums are exactly zero. A residual such as \(r_4=1.2\) means patient \(4\)’s observed change exceeded the model’s prediction by \(1.2\) units.

Sums of squares and \(R^2\). \[ \mathrm{RSS}=\sum r_i^2=0.16+0.64+1.00+1.44+0.36=3.6,\quad \mathrm{TSS}=S_{yy}=10,\quad \mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}=10-3.6=6.4, \] in agreement with \(\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx}=0.64\cdot10=6.4\) and the ANOVA identity Equation 6.11: \(10=6.4+3.6\). Hence \(R^2=6.4/10=0.64\), equal to \(\rho_{xy}^2=(8/\sqrt{10\cdot10})^2 =0.8^2=0.64\) as Corollary 6.1 requires.

Variance estimates. With \(n-p=3\), \[ s^2=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-p}=\frac{3.6}{3}=1.2,\qquad s=\sqrt{1.2}\approx1.0954, \] and the estimated covariance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=s^2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} =1.2\begin{pmatrix}1.1&-0.3\\-0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}1.32&-0.36\\-0.36&0.12\end{pmatrix}. \] Thus the standard errors are \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)=\sqrt{1.32}\approx1.1489\) and \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\sqrt{0.12}\approx0.3464\).

The t tests. By Theorem 6.8, under \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), \[ T_1=\frac{\hat\beta_1}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)}=\frac{0.8}{0.3464}\approx2.309\sim t_3 . \] The two-sided \(p\)-value is \(2P(t_3>2.309)\approx0.104\), and the critical value is \(t_{3,0.975}\approx3.182\), so at level \(0.05\) the slope is not significant. A \(95\%\) confidence interval for \(\beta_1\) is \(0.8\pm3.182\cdot0.3464=0.8\pm1.102=[-0.302,\,1.902]\). For the intercept, \(T_0=0.6/1.1489\approx0.522\), \(p\approx0.638\). In clinical terms, with only five patients the data are consistent with no association between baseline severity and outcome change: the confidence interval for the slope includes zero, even though the point estimate is positive. The study is simply too small to be conclusive, which is exactly what a nonsignificant \(t\) test reports.

The overall F test. By the remark following Theorem 6.9, testing \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) against the full model, \[ F=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_{\mathrm{reg}}/(p-1)}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)}=\frac{6.4/1}{3.6/3}=\frac{6.4}{1.2} =5.333\sim F_{1,3}. \] As promised by the \(q=1\) remark, \(F=T_1^2\): indeed \(2.309^2\approx5.33\). The critical value is \(F_{1,3;0.95}\approx10.13\), so the regression is not significant at the \(0.05\) level, consistent with the \(t\) test (the \(p\)-values agree, both \(\approx0.104\)).

Checking the residual maker. The hat matrix \(H=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\) has \(\mathrm{tr}(H)=2=p\), and \(M=I_5-H\) has \(\mathrm{tr}(M)=3=n-p\). Numerically the eigenvalues of \(M\) are \(\{0,0,1,1,1\}\), exactly two zeros (the dimension of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\)) and three ones (the dimension of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp\)), illustrating the spectral structure used in Theorem 6.6: \(\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2\) is a sum of three independent squared standard normals when G3 holds. This is why the divisor is \(n-p=3\) and the reference distribution has \(3\) degrees of freedom.

Tying it back. The same arithmetic, with the treatment-and-baseline design of Example 6.1 in place of a single severity column, is what an analyst runs on individual patient data before transporting the fit to another population. Every population-adjustment method of Parts IV and V rests on exactly this pipeline: fit a regression, read its coefficients and their uncertainty, and evaluate the fitted model at a new covariate distribution.
