8  Hierarchical Models, Shrinkage, and Bayesian Inference

An indirect treatment comparison never rests on a single study. It assembles effect estimates from a collection of trials that are similar but not identical: different sites, different eligibility criteria, different calendar years, possibly different versions of an outcome scale. Two extreme ways to handle such a collection are both wrong. We could analyze each trial in isolation, treating its effect as an unrelated unknown constant; this throws away the obvious fact that the trials inform one another and leaves each estimate as noisy as its own small sample allows. Or we could pool everything, pretending the trials estimate one common effect; this ignores genuine heterogeneity and reports a false precision. The hierarchical model threads between these extremes. It treats the study-specific true effects as a sample from a common population, so that each study’s estimate is pulled toward the others by an amount that the data themselves calibrate. The resulting estimator, the posterior mean, is a partial pooling that borrows strength across studies; the amount it borrows is the shrinkage factor.

This chapter builds that machinery from the ground up and then develops the Bayesian inference and computation that make it operational. We begin with exchangeability, the symmetry assumption that licenses pooling, and de Finetti’s theorem, which shows that for an exchangeable sequence the hierarchical “conditionally independent given a latent parameter” structure is not a modeling choice but a representation theorem. We then specify the normal-normal hierarchical model, distinguish random effects from fixed effects, and derive the shrinkage estimator as a posterior mean, with the James-Stein phenomenon as its frequentist shadow. Restricted maximum likelihood and empirical Bayes supply the variance components. The second half of the chapter is the Bayesian toolkit on which Bayesian network meta-analysis (Part III) and multilevel network meta-regression (Part V) rest: Bayes’ theorem for parameters, conjugate analysis, the posterior predictive distribution, credible intervals, Markov chain Monte Carlo with a full proof that detailed balance forces the target to be stationary, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and the No-U-Turn Sampler, and the marginal likelihood and Bayes factors. Every load-bearing result is proved in full.

Throughout we follow the notation of Notation: \(\boldsymbol\xi\) is the full parameter vector, \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi)\) a prior density, \(p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\) a likelihood, and \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) a posterior density. In the hierarchical sections we write \(\theta_j\) for the true effect in study \(j\), \(\mu\) and \(\tau^2\) for the population mean and variance of those effects, and \(s_j^2\) for the (known) within-study sampling variance of the estimate \(y_j\).

How to read this chapter. Two kinds of material are interleaved, and it helps to know which is which. The essential thread, which every applied reader should master, is short: what a hierarchical model is, why related trials borrow strength, how shrinkage and the heterogeneity \(\tau^2\) work, what a posterior and a credible interval mean, and what the draws returned by Bayesian software represent. The justification thread proves that this machinery is forced and correct: de Finetti’s representation theorem, the measure-theoretic form of Bayes’ theorem, the James-Stein phenomenon, and the detailed-balance and volume-preservation arguments behind the samplers. Example 8.1 (Three trials we follow all chapter) Three randomized trials each compare the same new treatment against the same control and report a log odds ratio (log OR) for response together with a standard error:

Trial \(j\) log OR \(y_j\) standard error \(s_j\) odds ratio \(e^{y_j}\)
1 \(0.0\) \(0.5\) \(1.00\)
2 \(0.6\) \(0.5\) \(1.82\)
3 \(1.2\) \(0.5\) \(3.32\)

The log OR is the effect on the scale the models use; the last column translates it to the odds ratio a clinician reads, so \(y_2 = 0.6\) means roughly \(1.8\) times the odds of response under treatment. The squared standard error is the within-study sampling variance \(s_j^2 = 0.5^2 = 0.25\), treated as known.

Three instincts compete. No pooling reports each \(y_j\) as is, so trial 3 looks impressively large. Complete pooling averages the three into a single number and denies that the trials differ at all. Partial pooling, the subject of this chapter, pulls each estimate part of the way toward the others by an amount the data choose; we will find (in Section 8.5, then in the consolidated Example 8.5) that if the true effects scatter with standard deviation \(0.5\), each estimate moves halfway, so trial 3’s \(1.2\) becomes \(0.9\). That pull is the shrinkage, and the number that decides how far the trials are allowed to differ is the between-study variance \(\tau^2\), the single most consequential quantity in a random-effects meta-analysis.

Why is pooling even legitimate? Because before we look at the data we have no reason to call trial 1 special and trial 3 typical; relabeling the trials should not change our joint model for their true effects. That symmetry is exchangeability, which we formalize next. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.1 Exchangeability and the rationale for hierarchical models

The premise that several studies “estimate related quantities” must be made precise before it can do any work. The right formalization is a symmetry: before seeing the data, we have no reason to label one study as systematically different from another, so our joint model for their true effects should be invariant to relabeling. That symmetry is exchangeability.

Definition 8.1 (Exchangeability) A finite collection of random variables \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) (each taking values in a measurable space \(\mathcal{S}\)) is exchangeable if for every permutation \(\sigma\) of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\), \[ (X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(n)}) \stackrel{d}{=} (X_1, \dots, X_n), \] that is, the two random vectors have the same joint distribution. An infinite sequence \((X_i)_{i \ge 1}\) is (infinitely) exchangeable if \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) is exchangeable for every \(n \ge 1\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The symbol \(\stackrel{d}{=}\) means “has the same distribution as”: the two random vectors it joins are not equal value by value, but they follow the identical joint law. So exchangeability says that shuffling the labels of the variables leaves their joint distribution untouched. For an indirect comparison this is the precise version of a familiar judgment: the trials of treatment A versus B that we are about to combine are exchangeable if, before seeing any data, we have no reason to single out one trial’s true effect as larger or smaller than another’s. We are not claiming the trials are identical; we are claiming our ignorance about them is symmetric. That is exactly the state of mind in which pooling is defensible. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Exchangeability is strictly weaker than independence and identical distribution. Independent and identically distributed (iid) variables are exchangeable, because the joint density factors into a product that any permutation leaves unchanged. But exchangeable variables need not be independent: the defining symmetry permits the kind of positive dependence we expect among related studies, where learning the effect in one study revises our belief about the others. The following lemma is the source of every example of exchangeable-but-dependent variables we shall use.

Remark. A primer on the measure-theoretic words (skim once, then refer back). The next few proofs use a handful of terms from measure-theoretic probability. They make the statements airtight, but the modeling lessons survive without them, so on a first pass read the plain gloss and move on; nothing later in the chapter requires you to manipulate these objects by hand.

  • A density with respect to a measure generalizes “probability density.” A dominating measure \(\nu\) is the yardstick the density is measured against: Lebesgue measure (ordinary length or area) for continuous data, counting measure for discrete data. “\(f\) is a density with respect to \(\nu\)” just means probabilities are recovered by integrating \(f\) against \(\nu\), which is an ordinary integral for continuous data and a sum for discrete data.
  • \(\sigma\)-finite is a mild technical condition on \(\nu\) (the space is a countable union of pieces of finite measure) that every measure in this book satisfies; you may ignore it.
  • The product measure \(\nu^{\otimes n}\) is the yardstick for \(n\) independent coordinates at once; a joint density “with respect to \(\nu^{\otimes n}\)” is the ordinary joint density of \((x_1,\dots,x_n)\).
  • Integrating against a distribution \(\Pi\), written \(\int \cdots \, d\Pi(\theta)\), is averaging over a random parameter \(\theta \sim \Pi\). It is more general than \(\int \cdots f(\theta)\,d\theta\) because \(\Pi\) need not have a density (it could place point masses), but when \(\Pi\) does have a density it is the familiar weighted average.
  • The Radon-Nikodym theorem is the guarantee that the densities we write down exist; Tonelli’s and Fubini’s theorems are the guarantees that we may swap the order of a double integral (Tonelli for nonnegative integrands, Fubini once absolute integrability is checked). Disintegration is the formal name for “conditional density”: it is what makes \(p(x,y) = p(x \mid y)\,p(y)\) rigorous in general.
  • A martingale (used once, in the de Finetti converse) is a running average or running total that, on average, neither grows nor shrinks given the past; such sequences converge under mild conditions, which is why an empirical frequency settles down to a limit.
  • The kernel of a density is its shape as a function of the unknown, dropping any constant that does not depend on the unknown. Two densities with the same kernel are the same distribution, because the constant is forced by the requirement that the density integrate to \(1\). We use this constantly: recognizing a kernel lets us read off a posterior without computing any normalizing integral.

\(\blacktriangleleft\)

Lemma 8.1 (Mixtures of iid sequences are exchangeable) Let \(\Theta\) be a random parameter with distribution \(\Pi\) on a space \(\mathcal{T}\), and suppose that conditional on \(\Theta = \theta\) the variables \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are iid with density \(f(\cdot \mid \theta)\) with respect to a dominating measure \(\nu\) on \(\mathcal{S}\). Then \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) is exchangeable.

Proof. The joint density of \((X_1, \dots, X_n)\) with respect to \(\nu^{\otimes n}\) is obtained by integrating the conditional joint density against \(\Pi\): \[ p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \int_{\mathcal{T}} \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i \mid \theta) \, d\Pi(\theta). \] For any permutation \(\sigma\), the product \(\prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i \mid \theta)\) is unchanged when the arguments \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) are permuted, since multiplication is commutative; this holds for every fixed \(\theta\), hence after integration \(p(x_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, x_{\sigma(n)}) = p(x_1, \dots, x_n)\). Equal joint densities give equal joint distributions, so the collection is exchangeable. \(\square\)

The hierarchical model is exactly the structure of Lemma 8.1 read as a generative recipe: draw a latent \(\theta\), then draw conditionally iid data. The remarkable content of de Finetti’s theorem is that, for infinite sequences, this is the only structure an exchangeable law can have. We state the binary case, which is cleanest, prove the direction we shall use, and cite the converse.

Before the formal statement, the idea in one sentence: if you genuinely cannot tell the units apart before seeing data, then they must share something, and that “something” can always be written as a single hidden parameter which, once fixed, makes the units independent and identically distributed. The hierarchical “draw \(\theta\), then draw data” recipe is therefore not one modeling option among many; for an exchangeable sequence it is forced. This is precisely the license a health researcher needs for random-effects meta-analysis: treating the study effects as a sample drawn from a population of effects is not a convenience, it is the only coherent way to encode “the trials are exchangeable.” Everything after the hidden parameter is bookkeeping.

Theorem 8.1 (de Finetti representation (binary case)) Let \((X_i)_{i \ge 1}\) be an infinitely exchangeable sequence of \(\{0,1\}\)-valued random variables. Then there is a unique probability measure \(\Pi\) on \([0,1]\) such that for every \(n\) and every \((x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \{0,1\}^n\) with \(s = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\), \[ P(X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_n = x_n) = \int_0^1 \theta^{s}(1-\theta)^{n-s} \, d\Pi(\theta). \tag{8.1}\] Equivalently, there is a random variable \(\Theta \sim \Pi\) such that, conditional on \(\Theta = \theta\), the \(X_i\) are iid \(\mathrm{Ber}(\theta)\); moreover \(\Theta = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) almost surely.

Proof. The direction we use in modeling, that any law of the form Equation 8.1 is exchangeable, is immediate from Lemma 8.1 with \(f(x \mid \theta) = \theta^x (1-\theta)^{1-x}\): the right side of Equation 8.1 depends on \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\) only through \(s\), which is permutation-invariant. The converse, that every infinitely exchangeable binary law admits the representation Equation 8.1 with a unique mixing measure \(\Pi\), is the substantive half of the theorem; it is proved by showing that the empirical frequencies \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\le n} X_i\) form a bounded martingale (a running average that, given the past, neither drifts up nor down) and therefore converge almost surely to a limit \(\Theta\) whose distribution is the required \(\Pi\); the hidden parameter is thus literally the long-run frequency of successes, and the finite-dimensional probabilities match Equation 8.1 in the limit. We cite this converse to the standard literature (Hewitt and Savage 1955; see the work log) rather than reproduce its measure-theoretic apparatus here. \(\square\)

Remark. The general (real-valued) de Finetti theorem of Hewitt and Savage replaces \(\mathrm{Ber}(\theta)\) by an arbitrary sampling distribution and \([0,1]\) by an abstract parameter space: an infinitely exchangeable sequence of random variables is a mixture of iid sequences. This is the precise sense in which “exchangeable units” and “conditionally iid given a latent parameter” coincide for infinite populations. Finite exchangeable sequences are only approximately of this form, which matters little for our applications because the latent parameter is the quantity of scientific interest and the number of studies, though finite, is treated as a sample from a conceptual super-population of studies. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

A two-point mixing distribution makes the representation concrete and the dependence visible by hand.

Example 8.2 (de Finetti with a hand calculation) Imagine binary responses from a population that is half “low responders” and half “high responders”: with probability \(\tfrac12\) the latent rate is \(\theta = 0.3\) and with probability \(\tfrac12\) it is \(\theta = 0.7\), so \(\Pi\) puts mass \(\tfrac12\) at each. Given \(\theta\), responses are iid \(\mathrm{Ber}(\theta)\). For three patients, the probability of any pattern with \(s = 2\) successes is, by Equation 8.1, \[ \int_0^1 \theta^{2}(1-\theta)^{1}\, d\Pi(\theta) = \tfrac12\,(0.3)^2(0.7) + \tfrac12\,(0.7)^2(0.3) = \tfrac12(0.063) + \tfrac12(0.147) = 0.105. \] The answer depends on the data only through \(s = 2\), not on which two patients responded: that is exchangeability, confirmed numerically. The variables are not independent, though. A single response has \(P(X_1 = 1) = \mathbb{E}[\theta] = \tfrac12(0.3) + \tfrac12(0.7) = 0.5\), yet two responses have \(P(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 1) = \mathbb{E}[\theta^2] = \tfrac12(0.09) + \tfrac12(0.49) = 0.29\), which exceeds \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\). Seeing one success raises the chance of a second, because it nudges our belief about \(\theta\) toward \(0.7\). The hidden \(\theta\) is exactly the engine of borrowing strength: data on some units inform the others only through what they reveal about \(\theta\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

For a finite meta-analysis the practical reading is this. de Finetti’s theorem is the idealized justification, stated for an infinite exchangeable population; real evidence synthesis has a finite number of studies, which we treat as exchangeable conditional on shared hyperparameters and as a sample from a conceptual population of possible studies. The infinite theorem tells us the right shape for the model (a latent population parameter with conditionally independent studies); the finite model of Definition 8.2, with its hyperprior, is that shape made operational and honest about uncertainty in the hyperparameters.

We can now define the object the theorem motivates.

Definition 8.2 (Bayesian hierarchical model) A two-level Bayesian hierarchical model for units \(j = 1, \dots, J\) consists of:

  1. a sampling distribution for the data, \(y_j \mid \theta_j \sim p(y_j \mid \theta_j)\), independent across \(j\) given the unit parameters;
  2. a population distribution (second-level prior) for the unit parameters, \(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol\phi \sim p(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol\phi)\), iid across \(j\) given a hyperparameter \(\boldsymbol\phi\); and
  3. a hyperprior \(\boldsymbol\phi \sim p(\boldsymbol\phi)\).

The joint density factors as \[ p(\mathbf y, \boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\phi) = p(\boldsymbol\phi) \prod_{j=1}^{J} p(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol\phi)\, p(y_j \mid \theta_j), \tag{8.2}\] where \(\boldsymbol\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_J)\) and \(\mathbf y = (y_1, \dots, y_J)\). Models with further levels (units nested in groups nested in populations) are defined by iterating the construction. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The three stages name three different things, and keeping them apart is most of the battle. The sampling distribution (stage 1) is what each trial observed: its estimate \(y_j\) given its own true effect \(\theta_j\). The population distribution (stage 2) is what the trials share: the \(\theta_j\) are drawn from one population governed by the hyperparameter \(\boldsymbol\phi\) (for us \(\boldsymbol\phi = (\mu, \tau^2)\)). The hyperprior (stage 3) is our uncertainty about that shared population before any data. In one line: stage 1 is observed, the \(\theta_j\) are latent, and \(\boldsymbol\phi\) is shared. This is the skeleton of every Bayesian network meta-analysis in Part III, where the unit parameters are study-level treatment contrasts and the shared \(\boldsymbol\phi\) is the pooled effect plus the heterogeneity. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Stage 2 of Definition 8.2 encodes exactly the exchangeability of the unit parameters: given \(\boldsymbol\phi\) they are iid, so unconditionally they are exchangeable by Lemma 8.1. The hyperparameter \(\boldsymbol\phi\) is what the units have in common, and the hyperprior expresses our uncertainty about it. Inference for any single \(\theta_j\) now flows through the others, because the data from all units inform \(\boldsymbol\phi\), which in turn informs each \(\theta_j\). This is the mechanism of borrowing strength, and the rest of the chapter makes it quantitative.

8.2 The normal-normal model: random effects versus fixed effects

The canonical instance of Definition 8.2, and the workhorse of meta-analysis, is Gaussian at both levels. Suppose study \(j\) reports an effect estimate \(y_j\) (for concreteness, a log odds ratio) together with its standard error, so that the within-study sampling variance \(s_j^2\) is known to good approximation. The two-level model is \[ y_j \mid \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_j, s_j^2), \qquad \theta_j \mid \mu, \tau^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2), \qquad j = 1, \dots, J, \tag{8.3}\] with hyperparameters \(\boldsymbol\phi = (\mu, \tau^2)\). Here \(\theta_j\) is the true effect in study \(j\), \(\mu\) is the population mean effect, and \(\tau^2\) is the between-study variance that measures heterogeneity. The distinction between the two ways of reading \(\theta_j\) is the distinction between fixed and random effects.

The same five objects, read in the language of the running example of Example 8.1, are the following.

Object Symbol Trial 1 value Reading
observed estimate \(y_j\) \(0.0\) what trial \(j\) reported (a log OR)
standard error \(s_j\) \(0.5\) how precise that report is
within-study variance \(s_j^2\) \(0.25\) squared standard error, treated as known
true study effect \(\theta_j\) unknown what trial \(j\) would report with infinite data
population mean \(\mu\) unknown the average true effect across trials
heterogeneity \(\tau^2\) unknown how widely the \(\theta_j\) scatter around \(\mu\)

A feel for \(\tau^2\) is worth having now, because it governs everything downstream. On the log-OR scale a between-study standard deviation \(\tau = 0.5\) (so \(\tau^2 = 0.25\)) means a typical trial’s true effect lies within roughly \(\pm 1\) of \(\mu\) on the log-OR scale, that is, within a factor of about \(e^{\pm 1} \approx (0.37,\, 2.7)\) on the odds-ratio scale: real, substantial heterogeneity. A value such as \(\tau^2 = 0.01\) (\(\tau = 0.1\)) would mean the trials nearly agree, and \(\tau^2 \to 0\) would force them to share one true effect. The whole job of Section 8.6 is to let the data estimate this number rather than guess it.

Definition 8.3 (Fixed effects and random effects) In a model with unit parameters \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_J\) for the data \(y_j \mid \theta_j \sim p(y_j \mid \theta_j)\):

  • the parameters are fixed effects if they are treated as unrelated unknown constants, each estimated only from its own unit (formally, the joint model places no second-level distribution linking them, so inference for \(\theta_j\) uses \(y_j\) alone); and
  • the parameters are random effects if they are modeled as an exchangeable sample from a common population distribution \(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol\phi \sim p(\cdot \mid \boldsymbol\phi)\), so that information is shared across units through the common hyperparameter \(\boldsymbol\phi\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. A terminology warning you will be glad to have. The words “fixed” and “random effects” mean different things in different fields, and meta-analysis is the worst trap. In regression and mixed-model language, which Definition 8.3 follows, fixed effects are unrelated unknown constants estimated each from its own unit (no link, the \(\tau^2 \to \infty\) extreme). But in meta-analysis software (RevMan, the R package metafor) a fixed-effect model, increasingly called a common-effect model, means the opposite extreme: there is one single true effect shared by every study, \(\theta_1 = \dots = \theta_J = \mu\), which is complete pooling, the \(\tau^2 \to 0\) case. So the meta-analysis “fixed-effect model” is this chapter’s complete pooling, not its fixed effects. The table reconciles the three usages.

Term (and field) Meaning here Limit
fixed effects (regression, mixed models) unrelated unknown constants, each from its own unit \(\tau^2 \to \infty\) (no link)
fixed-effect or common-effect model (meta-analysis) one shared true effect \(\theta_1 = \dots = \theta_J = \mu\) \(\tau^2 = 0\) (complete pooling)
random-effects model (both fields) true effects are an exchangeable sample from a population \(0 < \tau^2 < \infty\) (partial pooling)

The practical choice is simple to state. If you believe every trial estimates the same underlying drug effect, the common-effect (meta-analysis “fixed-effect”) model is appropriate. If you believe each trial has its own true effect drawn from a population of trial effects, use random effects. The random-effects model of Equation 8.3 contains both beliefs as the extremes \(\tau^2 = 0\) and \(\tau^2 \to \infty\), and lets the data choose where between them to sit. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The two readings are genuinely different probability models, and the difference is visible in the marginal law of the data. Under fixed effects the \(y_j\) are independent with means \(\theta_j\) that bear no stated relation. Under random effects, integrating out \(\theta_j\) in Equation 8.3 gives a marginal model in which every observation carries information about the shared \((\mu, \tau^2)\), hence about every other unit; we compute that marginal in Proposition 8.2. The fixed-effects analysis is the \(\tau^2 \to \infty\) limit of the random-effects analysis (an improper flat population that links nothing), and the complete-pooling analysis \(\theta_1 = \dots = \theta_J = \mu\) is the \(\tau^2 \to 0\) limit (a degenerate population that forces equality). The random-effects model contains both as extreme cases and lets the data choose where between them to sit, by estimating \(\tau^2\). Three estimators of the study effects correspond to the three regimes: \[ \underbrace{\hat\theta_j = y_j}_{\text{no pooling}\;(\tau^2 = \infty)}, \qquad \underbrace{\hat\theta_j = \bar y_w}_{\text{complete pooling}\;(\tau^2 = 0)}, \qquad \underbrace{\hat\theta_j = \text{posterior mean}}_{\text{partial pooling}\;(0 < \tau^2 < \infty)}, \] where \(\bar y_w\) is a precision-weighted average. To compute the partial-pooling estimator we need Bayes’ theorem and the normal-normal conjugate update, which we now develop in general before returning to specialize them in Section 8.5.

8.3 Bayes’ theorem for parameters

Bayesian inference treats the parameter as a random variable, assigns it a prior distribution, and updates that distribution by conditioning on the data. The update is Bayes’ theorem. Five distributions recur from here on; they are worth fixing in a table before any algebra.

Distribution Symbol The question it answers
prior \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi)\) what we believe about the parameter before data
likelihood \(p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\) how probable the observed data are for each parameter value
posterior \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) what we believe about the parameter after data
marginal likelihood \(p(\mathbf y)\) how probable the data are overall, averaging the likelihood over the prior
posterior predictive \(p(\tilde{\mathbf y} \mid \mathbf y)\) what future data should look like, given the data so far

The working content of Bayes’ theorem is a single proportionality, posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior, and it needs nothing measure-theoretic. The simplest possible instance uses pure arithmetic.

Example 8.3 (A one-line Bayes update with no calculus) Before a trial we entertain just two possibilities for a treatment’s true response probability: a “modest” rate \(p = 0.3\) and a “strong” rate \(p = 0.6\), judged equally likely, so the prior is \(\pi(0.3) = \pi(0.6) = 0.5\). One patient is treated and responds (\(y = 1\)). The likelihood of this datum is \(p(y = 1 \mid p) = p\), so by posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior, \[ \pi(0.3 \mid y) \propto 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15, \qquad \pi(0.6 \mid y) \propto 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.30. \] Normalizing so the two add to \(1\) (dividing by \(0.15 + 0.30 = 0.45\)) gives \(\pi(0.3 \mid y) = \tfrac13\) and \(\pi(0.6 \mid y) = \tfrac23\). The single success has shifted belief from \(50{:}50\) toward the stronger rate, exactly as intuition demands, and the only operation was “multiply, then rescale to sum to one.” Bayes’ theorem below is this same recipe with a sum replaced by an integral. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The theorem states the recipe in full generality, for parameters living in continuous spaces where the “rescale to one” step is an integral and densities must be handled with care. Its content is interpretive and computational, not analytical: in practice you use the proportional form above. The proof that follows is the measure-theoretic guarantee that the proportional form is legitimate even when the parameter has no density in the elementary sense; a reader meeting measure theory for the first time may take the boxed primer on faith and skim the proof, losing nothing needed for the rest of the chapter.

Theorem 8.2 (Bayes’ theorem (densities)) Let \(\boldsymbol\xi\) take values in a measurable space \((\Xi, \mathcal{G})\) with prior density \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi)\) with respect to a \(\sigma\)-finite dominating measure \(\mu\), and let the data \(\mathbf y\) take values in \((\mathcal{Y}, \mathcal{B})\) with conditional density \(p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\) with respect to a \(\sigma\)-finite measure \(\nu\). Define the marginal likelihood \[ p(\mathbf y) = \int_{\Xi} p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi) \, d\mu(\boldsymbol\xi). \] Then for every \(\mathbf y\) with \(p(\mathbf y) > 0\), a version of the posterior density of \(\boldsymbol\xi\) given \(\mathbf y\) (with respect to \(\mu\)) is \[ \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y) = \frac{p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi)}{p(\mathbf y)}. \tag{8.4}\]

Proof. By construction the pair \((\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf y)\) has joint density \(p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf y) = p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\,\pi(\boldsymbol\xi)\) with respect to the product measure \(\mu \otimes \nu\); the existence of these densities is the Radon-Nikodym theorem applied to the assumed dominated families. The marginal density of \(\mathbf y\) is \(p(\mathbf y) = \int_\Xi p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf y)\, d\mu(\boldsymbol\xi)\), by Tonelli’s theorem applied to the nonnegative integrand. Fix \(\mathbf y\) with \(p(\mathbf y) > 0\) and let \(g\) denote the right side of Equation 8.4, a nonnegative function of \(\boldsymbol\xi\) with \(\int_\Xi g\, d\mu = p(\mathbf y)/p(\mathbf y) = 1\), hence a probability density with respect to \(\mu\). To see that \(g(\cdot \mid \mathbf y)\) is a version of the conditional density, we verify the defining disintegration property: for every bounded measurable \(h: \Xi \to \mathbb{R}\) and every \(B \in \mathcal{B}\), \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[h(\boldsymbol\xi)\,\mathbf 1_B(\mathbf y)\right] = \int_B \left( \int_\Xi h(\boldsymbol\xi)\, g(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\, d\mu(\boldsymbol\xi) \right) p(\mathbf y)\, d\nu(\mathbf y). \] Indeed, writing the left side through the joint density and substituting \(p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf y) = g(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\, p(\mathbf y)\), \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[h(\boldsymbol\xi)\,\mathbf 1_B(\mathbf y)\right] = \int_B \int_\Xi h(\boldsymbol\xi)\, p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf y)\, d\mu(\boldsymbol\xi)\, d\nu(\mathbf y) = \int_B \int_\Xi h(\boldsymbol\xi)\, g(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\, p(\mathbf y)\, d\mu(\boldsymbol\xi)\, d\nu(\mathbf y), \] which is the asserted identity by Fubini’s theorem (the integrand is \(\nu \otimes \mu\)-integrable since \(h\) is bounded and \(p(\boldsymbol\xi,\mathbf y)\) integrates to \(1\)). The disintegration identity characterizes the conditional density up to \(\mu\)-null sets, so \(g(\cdot \mid \mathbf y) = \pi(\cdot \mid \mathbf y)\) is the posterior density. \(\square\)

Remark. Three points organize everything that follows. First, the denominator \(p(\mathbf y)\) does not depend on \(\boldsymbol\xi\), so up to a normalizing constant Equation 8.4 reads “posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior,” and every method that needs only the shape of the posterior (conjugate updating, Markov chain Monte Carlo) can ignore \(p(\mathbf y)\). Second, the same \(p(\mathbf y)\) is the central object of model comparison (Section 8.10), where its dependence on the model, not on \(\boldsymbol\xi\), is exactly the point. Third, Bayes’ theorem is a statement about densities; its use is a modeling decision, namely to regard \(\boldsymbol\xi\) as random and to report \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) as the inference. (Corresponds to L.1 in the proof catalogue.) \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why this matters. Every Bayesian network meta-analysis in this book is this one update applied to study-level treatment contrasts: a prior on the pooled effects and heterogeneity, a binomial or normal likelihood from the trials, and a posterior that the software reports. The proportional form posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior is all that the Markov chain Monte Carlo of Section 8.8 ever needs, because the unknown constant \(p(\mathbf y)\) cancels in every ratio it computes. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.4 Conjugate analysis

A prior family is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior stays in the family, with updated hyperparameters. Conjugacy turns Equation 8.4 into algebra, gives closed-form posteriors that we can read and check, and supplies the building blocks of both the shrinkage estimator and the Gibbs updates inside samplers. We prove the two pairs we use in full and state the third.

8.4.1 Normal-normal conjugacy

Theorem 8.3 (Normal-normal conjugacy) Let \(y \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2 > 0\) known, and let the prior be \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)\). Then the posterior is \[ \theta \mid y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \tau_1^2), \qquad \frac{1}{\tau_1^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu_1 = \tau_1^2 \left( \frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{y}{\sigma^2} \right). \tag{8.5}\] If instead we observe an iid sample \(y_1, \dots, y_n \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)\), the same conclusion holds with \(y\) replaced by \(\bar y = \frac1n \sum_i y_i\) and \(\sigma^2\) replaced by \(\sigma^2/n\), so that \(\tau_1^{-2} = \tau_0^{-2} + n\sigma^{-2}\).

Proof. By Bayes’ theorem in proportional form (Theorem 8.2), the posterior density is proportional in \(\theta\) to the product of the likelihood and the prior: \[ \pi(\theta \mid y) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y - \theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\theta - \mu_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac12 Q(\theta)\right), \] where, collecting the two quadratics, \[ Q(\theta) = \frac{(\theta - y)^2}{\sigma^2} + \frac{(\theta - \mu_0)^2}{\tau_0^2} = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau_0^2}\right)\theta^2 - 2\theta\left(\frac{y}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2}\right) + \text{const}. \] Write \(\tau_1^{-2} = \sigma^{-2} + \tau_0^{-2}\) for the coefficient of \(\theta^2\) and \(\mu_1 = \tau_1^2( y\sigma^{-2} + \mu_0\tau_0^{-2})\). Completing the square, \[ Q(\theta) = \frac{1}{\tau_1^2}\bigl(\theta^2 - 2\theta\mu_1\bigr) + \text{const} = \frac{(\theta - \mu_1)^2}{\tau_1^2} + \text{const}, \] where the constant absorbs all terms free of \(\theta\). Hence \(\pi(\theta \mid y) \propto \exp(-(\theta - \mu_1)^2/(2\tau_1^2))\), which is the kernel of the \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \tau_1^2)\) density; since a density is determined by its kernel (Proposition 4.2), the posterior is \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \tau_1^2)\). For the iid sample, the likelihood factor is \(\prod_i \exp(-(y_i - \theta)^2/(2\sigma^2)) = \exp(-\sum_i (y_i - \theta)^2/(2\sigma^2))\), and \(\sum_i (y_i - \theta)^2 = n(\bar y - \theta)^2 + \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2\); the second term is free of \(\theta\), so the sample enters exactly as a single observation \(\bar y\) with variance \(\sigma^2/n\). \(\square\)

Remark. Equation Equation 8.5 is the rule “precisions add, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of prior mean and data.” Writing the precision \(\tau_1^{-2}\) as the sum of the prior precision \(\tau_0^{-2}\) and the data precision \(\sigma^{-2}\) makes the update additive in information, and exhibits the posterior mean \(\mu_1 = (\tau_0^{-2}\mu_0 + \sigma^{-2} y)/(\tau_0^{-2} + \sigma^{-2})\) as a weighted average with weights proportional to those precisions. This is the analytic heart of shrinkage. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why this matters. This single update is what a Bayesian network meta-analysis performs for each study-level treatment contrast: the prior mean \(\mu_0\) is the pooled effect, the datum \(y\) is one trial’s log odds ratio, and the posterior mean \(\mu_1\) is that trial’s shrunken contrast. Read the update with \(\mu_0\) as the network’s pooled effect and you are looking at the engine of Part III. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The same computation runs in any dimension and is the bridge to the multivariate normal of Chapter 4. We need the multivariate version because a network meta-analysis estimates a whole vector of pooled contrasts at once (treatment \(B\) versus \(A\), \(C\) versus \(A\), and so on), correlated through the studies they share; the update below is the joint version of the scalar shrinkage above, applied to that vector of effects.

Corollary 8.1 (Multivariate normal-normal conjugacy) Let \(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\theta \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma \succ 0\) known, and let \(\boldsymbol\theta \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\mathbf m_0, \boldsymbol\Omega_0)\) with \(\boldsymbol\Omega_0 \succ 0\). Then \[ \boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf y \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\mathbf m_1, \boldsymbol\Omega_1), \qquad \boldsymbol\Omega_1^{-1} = \boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1} + \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}, \qquad \mathbf m_1 = \boldsymbol\Omega_1\bigl(\boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1}\mathbf m_0 + \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf y\bigr). \]

Proof. The posterior is proportional in \(\boldsymbol\theta\) to \(\exp(-\tfrac12 Q(\boldsymbol\theta))\) with \[ Q(\boldsymbol\theta) = (\mathbf y - \boldsymbol\theta)^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y - \boldsymbol\theta) + (\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf m_0)^\top \boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1}(\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf m_0) = \boldsymbol\theta^\top \boldsymbol\Omega_1^{-1}\boldsymbol\theta - 2\boldsymbol\theta^\top \bigl(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf y + \boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1}\mathbf m_0\bigr) + \text{const}, \] using symmetry of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) and \(\boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1}\) to combine the cross terms. The matrix \(\boldsymbol\Omega_1^{-1} = \boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1} + \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) is the sum of two positive definite matrices, hence positive definite and invertible (Theorem 3.3), so \(\boldsymbol\Omega_1\) is well defined and \(\succ 0\). With \(\mathbf m_1 = \boldsymbol\Omega_1( \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf y + \boldsymbol\Omega_0^{-1}\mathbf m_0)\), completing the square in the \(\boldsymbol\Omega_1^{-1}\) quadratic form (Definition 3.6) gives \(Q(\boldsymbol\theta) = (\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf m_1)^\top \boldsymbol\Omega_1^{-1}(\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf m_1) + \text{const}\), the kernel of \(\mathcal{N}_p(\mathbf m_1, \boldsymbol\Omega_1)\). \(\square\)

Remark. The same posterior can be read off without algebra by stacking \((\boldsymbol\theta, \mathbf y)\) into a joint Gaussian and applying the conditioning formula Theorem 4.15: with \(\boldsymbol\theta \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\mathbf m_0, \boldsymbol\Omega_0)\) and \(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\theta \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\), the pair is jointly normal, and the conditional of \(\boldsymbol\theta\) given \(\mathbf y\) is normal with the stated mean and covariance. The two routes agree, which is a useful check on Theorem 4.15. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.4.2 Beta-binomial conjugacy

Theorem 8.4 (Beta-binomial conjugacy) Let \(y \mid p \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)\) and let the prior be \(p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a, b)\) with \(a, b > 0\). Then \[ p \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a + y, \, b + n - y), \tag{8.6}\] and the posterior mean is the convex combination \[ \mathbb{E}[p \mid y] = \frac{a + y}{a + b + n} = \frac{a + b}{a + b + n}\cdot \frac{a}{a + b} + \frac{n}{a + b + n}\cdot \frac{y}{n}. \]

Proof. The likelihood is \(p(y \mid p) = \binom{n}{y} p^y (1 - p)^{n - y} \propto p^y (1 - p)^{n - y}\) as a function of \(p\), and the prior density is \(\pi(p) = \frac{1}{B(a,b)} p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1} \propto p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1}\) on \((0,1)\), where \(B(a,b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)\) is the beta function. By Theorem 8.2, \[ \pi(p \mid y) \propto p^{y}(1 - p)^{n - y} \cdot p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1} = p^{(a + y) - 1}(1 - p)^{(b + n - y) - 1}, \] which is the kernel of the \(\mathrm{Beta}(a + y, b + n - y)\) density. A density is determined by its kernel and its total mass \(1\); the unique normalizing constant making the displayed kernel integrate to \(1\) is \(1/B(a + y, b + n - y)\), so the posterior is \(\mathrm{Beta}(a + y, b + n - y)\). The mean of a \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) variable is \(\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)\); substituting \(\alpha = a + y\) and \(\beta = b + n - y\) gives \((a + y)/(a + b + n)\), and splitting the numerator as \(a + y\) and writing the denominator as \((a + b) + n\) produces the stated convex combination of the prior mean \(a/(a+b)\) and the maximum likelihood estimate \(y/n\). \(\square\)

Remark. The posterior mean again shrinks the data estimate \(y/n\) toward the prior mean \(a/(a + b)\), with weight \(n/(a + b + n)\) on the data growing to \(1\) as \(n \to \infty\). The hyperparameters \(a\) and \(b\) act as prior “pseudo-counts” of successes and failures; this is the discrete-data analogue of the precision-weighting in Equation 8.5. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

A number makes the update tangible, and event counts are the data a health researcher actually holds.

Example 8.4 (A treatment-response update by hand) One trial arm treats \(n = 8\) patients and \(y = 7\) respond, a raw response rate of \(7/8 = 0.875\). Before the trial we hold a weakly informative prior \(p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)\), centered at \(0.5\) and worth two pseudo-successes and two pseudo-failures. By Theorem 8.4 the posterior is \[ p \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2 + 7, \; 2 + 1) = \mathrm{Beta}(9, 3), \] with posterior mean \(9/12 = 0.75\). The observed \(0.875\) has been pulled toward the prior mean \(0.5\); the pull is exactly the “four prior pseudo-patients out of twelve total” weight, \(4/12 = \tfrac13\) toward \(0.5\) and \(8/12 = \tfrac23\) toward the data. Only two integers changed, \(a \mapsto a + y\) and \(b \mapsto b + (n - y)\): Bayesian updating with event counts is bookkeeping on responders and non-responders. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.4.3 Gamma-Poisson conjugacy

We state the third standard pair, with its one-line proof, for completeness; it is the conjugate analysis behind count-data meta-analysis.

Proposition 8.1 (Gamma-Poisson conjugacy) Let \(y_1, \dots, y_n \mid \lambda \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\) iid, and let \(\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)\) with shape \(a > 0\) and rate \(b > 0\) (so \(\mathbb{E}[\lambda] = a/b\)). Then \[ \lambda \mid \mathbf y \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a + \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n y_i, \; b + n\right). \]

Proof. The likelihood is \(\prod_i e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{y_i}/y_i! \propto \lambda^{\sum_i y_i} e^{-n\lambda}\), and the prior density is \(\propto \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}\) on \(\lambda > 0\). Their product is \(\propto \lambda^{(a + \sum_i y_i) - 1} e^{-(b + n)\lambda}\), the kernel of \(\mathrm{Gamma}(a + \sum_i y_i, \, b + n)\). \(\square\)

8.5 Partial pooling and the shrinkage estimator

We now apply conjugate analysis to the normal-normal model Equation 8.3. Fix the hyperparameters \((\mu, \tau^2)\) for the moment (we estimate them in Section 8.6) and compute the posterior of each study effect \(\theta_j\) given its estimate \(y_j\). The result is the partial-pooling, or shrinkage, estimator, and it is the central object of this chapter.

Theorem 8.5 (Shrinkage as a posterior mean) In the normal-normal model \(y_j \mid \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_j, s_j^2)\) and \(\theta_j \mid \mu, \tau^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2)\) with \(\mu, \tau^2\) and \(s_j^2 > 0\) known, the posterior of \(\theta_j\) given \(y_j\) is normal, \[ \theta_j \mid y_j \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat\theta_j, \; V_j\right), \] with posterior mean and variance \[ \hat\theta_j = B_j\, \mu + (1 - B_j)\, y_j, \qquad V_j = (1 - B_j)\, s_j^2, \qquad B_j = \frac{s_j^2}{s_j^2 + \tau^2} = \frac{1/\tau^2}{\,1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2\,}. \tag{8.7}\] The shrinkage factor \(B_j\) lies in \((0, 1)\), so the posterior mean is a strict convex combination of the population mean \(\mu\) and the study estimate \(y_j\), and the posterior variance \(V_j = (1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2)^{-1}\) is smaller than both \(s_j^2\) and \(\tau^2\).

Proof. The model is the single-observation normal-normal pair of Theorem 8.3 with prior mean \(\mu_0 = \mu\), prior variance \(\tau_0^2 = \tau^2\), and sampling variance \(\sigma^2 = s_j^2\). By Equation 8.5 the posterior is \(\mathcal{N}(\hat\theta_j, V_j)\) with \[ \frac{1}{V_j} = \frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{1}{s_j^2}, \qquad \hat\theta_j = V_j\left(\frac{\mu}{\tau^2} + \frac{y_j}{s_j^2}\right). \] Define \(B_j = V_j/\tau^2\) and note \(1 - B_j = V_j/s_j^2\), because \(V_j/\tau^2 + V_j/s_j^2 = V_j(1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2) = V_j \cdot V_j^{-1} = 1\). Substituting, \[ \hat\theta_j = V_j\cdot\frac{\mu}{\tau^2} + V_j\cdot\frac{y_j}{s_j^2} = B_j\,\mu + (1 - B_j)\,y_j, \] which is the stated convex combination. From \(1/V_j = 1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2\) we get \(V_j = (1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2)^{-1}\) and, multiplying \(B_j = V_j/\tau^2\) through, \[ B_j = \frac{V_j}{\tau^2} = \frac{1}{\tau^2}\cdot\frac{1}{1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2} = \frac{1/\tau^2}{1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2} = \frac{s_j^2}{s_j^2 + \tau^2}, \] the last equality by multiplying numerator and denominator by \(\tau^2 s_j^2\). Finally \(V_j = (1 - B_j) s_j^2\) since \(1 - B_j = V_j/s_j^2\). Because \(s_j^2 > 0\) and \(\tau^2 > 0\), the factor \(B_j = s_j^2/(s_j^2 + \tau^2)\) is strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), and \(V_j = (1/\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2)^{-1} < (1/\tau^2)^{-1} = \tau^2\) and likewise \(V_j < s_j^2\). \(\square\)

Remark. The estimator Equation 8.7 interpolates exactly the three regimes of Section 8.2. As \(\tau^2 \to \infty\) (a diffuse population, units unrelated) we have \(B_j \to 0\) and \(\hat\theta_j \to y_j\): no pooling. As \(\tau^2 \to 0\) (a degenerate population, units identical) we have \(B_j \to 1\) and \(\hat\theta_j \to \mu\): complete pooling. For intermediate \(\tau^2\), the study is shrunk toward the population mean by the fraction \(B_j\), and a noisier study (larger \(s_j^2\)) is shrunk more, because its own estimate is less trustworthy: \(B_j\) increases in \(s_j^2\). This is borrowing strength made precise. The posterior variance \(V_j\) is smaller than the naive \(s_j^2\), which quantifies the information each study gains from the others through the shared \(\mu\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Put numbers to it with the three trials of Example 8.1. Suppose for the moment the population mean is \(\mu = 0.6\) and the between-study standard deviation is \(\tau = 0.5\) (so \(\tau^2 = 0.25\)), equal to the common \(s_j^2 = 0.25\). Then Equation 8.7 gives the same shrinkage factor \(B_j = 0.25/(0.25 + 0.25) = \tfrac12\) for every trial, so each estimate moves exactly halfway toward \(0.6\):

Trial \(j\) raw \(y_j\) raw OR \(e^{y_j}\) shrunk \(\hat\theta_j\) shrunk OR \(e^{\hat\theta_j}\)
1 \(0.0\) \(1.00\) \(0.30\) \(1.35\)
2 \(0.6\) \(1.82\) \(0.60\) \(1.82\)
3 \(1.2\) \(3.32\) \(0.90\) \(2.46\)

This table is a forest plot in words: the two extreme, equally imprecise trials are drawn inward toward the center while the already-central trial 2 does not move, and the odds ratios follow. Trial 3’s eye-catching raw odds ratio of \(3.32\) becomes a soberer \(2.46\) once we admit it is one noisy trial among three. The full calculation, with credible intervals and a \(\tau^2\) estimated from the data, is carried out in Example 8.5.

Remark. Why this matters. The shrinkage estimator Equation 8.7 is the point estimate a random-effects meta-analysis reports for each study, and its network generalization (Section 8.12) is what a Bayesian NMA reports for each study-level contrast. When a paper says a network model “borrows strength across trials,” this convex combination, with \(\tau^2\) setting how far each estimate travels, is the precise mechanism. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The shrinkage estimator has a celebrated frequentist counterpart. Stein discovered that shrinkage improves estimation of a vector of normal means even with no prior and under squared-error loss, a result that overturned the presumption that the obvious estimator “use each \(y_j\) for \(\theta_j\)” is optimal. We prove it, because it is the rigorous justification for pooling when one is reluctant to commit to a prior, and because it explains why empirical Bayes works. The engine is an integration-by- parts identity for the normal distribution.

Lemma 8.2 (Stein’s lemma) Let \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)\) and let \(h: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) be absolutely continuous with \(\mathbb{E}\,|h'(Z)| < \infty\). Then \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(Z - \theta)\, h(Z)\bigr] = \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}\bigl[h'(Z)\bigr]. \]

Proof. Write \(\varphi(z) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}\exp(-(z - \theta)^2/(2\sigma^2))\) for the density of \(Z\). It satisfies the identity \(\varphi'(z) = -\frac{z - \theta}{\sigma^2}\varphi(z)\), equivalently \((z - \theta)\varphi(z) = -\sigma^2 \varphi'(z)\). Hence \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(Z - \theta) h(Z)\bigr] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(z)(z - \theta)\varphi(z)\, dz = -\sigma^2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(z)\varphi'(z)\, dz. \] Integrating by parts, and using that \(h(z)\varphi(z) \to 0\) as \(z \to \pm\infty\) (which holds because \(\mathbb{E}|h'(Z)| < \infty\) forces \(h\) to grow slower than the Gaussian tail decays), \[ -\sigma^2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(z)\varphi'(z)\, dz = -\sigma^2\Bigl[ h(z)\varphi(z)\Bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \sigma^2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h'(z)\varphi(z)\, dz = \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}\bigl[h'(Z)\bigr]. \qquad \square \]

Theorem 8.6 (James-Stein dominance) Let \(\mathbf Y \sim \mathcal{N}_J(\boldsymbol\theta, \sigma^2 I_J)\) with \(\sigma^2\) known and \(J \ge 3\), and define the James-Stein estimator \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS}} = \left(1 - \frac{(J - 2)\sigma^2}{\|\mathbf Y\|^2}\right)\mathbf Y. \] Then for every \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \mathbb{R}^J\), \[ \mathbb{E}\,\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS}} - \boldsymbol\theta\bigr\|^2 = J\sigma^2 - (J - 2)^2 \sigma^4\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf Y\|^2}\right] < J\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}\,\bigl\|\mathbf Y - \boldsymbol\theta\bigr\|^2. \] That is, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS}}\) has strictly smaller mean squared error than the maximum likelihood estimator \(\mathbf Y\), uniformly in \(\boldsymbol\theta\).

Proof. Write \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS}} = \mathbf Y + \mathbf g(\mathbf Y)\) with \(g_i(\mathbf y) = -(J - 2)\sigma^2\, y_i/\|\mathbf y\|^2\). Then \[ \|\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS}} - \boldsymbol\theta\|^2 = \|\mathbf Y - \boldsymbol\theta\|^2 + 2\sum_{i=1}^J (Y_i - \theta_i) g_i(\mathbf Y) + \|\mathbf g(\mathbf Y)\|^2. \] Taking expectations, the first term is \(J\sigma^2\). For the cross term, apply Stein’s lemma (Lemma 8.2) one coordinate at a time. Fix every coordinate except the \(i\)-th; then \(g_i(\mathbf Y)\) depends on the single scalar \(Y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_i, \sigma^2)\) through the one-variable function \(h(y_i) := g_i(Y_1, \dots, y_i, \dots, Y_J)\), with the other \(Y_k\) riding along as constants, so Stein’s lemma in that one variable gives \(\mathbb{E}[(Y_i - \theta_i) g_i(\mathbf Y) \mid Y_k, k \ne i] = \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}[\partial g_i/\partial y_i \mid Y_k, k \ne i]\). Averaging over the remaining coordinates (the tower property) removes the conditioning: \(\mathbb{E}[(Y_i - \theta_i) g_i(\mathbf Y)] = \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}[\partial g_i/\partial y_i]\). Summing, \[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_i (Y_i - \theta_i) g_i(\mathbf Y)\Bigr] = \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}\bigl[\nabla \cdot \mathbf g(\mathbf Y)\bigr]. \] Differentiate: with \(\|\mathbf y\|^2 = \sum_k y_k^2\), \[ \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial y_i} = -(J - 2)\sigma^2\left(\frac{1}{\|\mathbf y\|^2} - \frac{2 y_i^2}{\|\mathbf y\|^4}\right), \qquad \nabla\cdot \mathbf g = -(J - 2)\sigma^2\left(\frac{J}{\|\mathbf y\|^2} - \frac{2}{\|\mathbf y\|^2}\right) = -\frac{(J - 2)^2 \sigma^2}{\|\mathbf y\|^2}, \] using \(\sum_i y_i^2 = \|\mathbf y\|^2\). Also \(\|\mathbf g(\mathbf y)\|^2 = (J - 2)^2\sigma^4\,\|\mathbf y\|^2/\|\mathbf y\|^4 = (J - 2)^2\sigma^4/\|\mathbf y\|^2\). Combining, \[ \mathbb{E}\,\|\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS}} - \boldsymbol\theta\|^2 = J\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\!\left(-(J-2)^2\sigma^2\,\mathbb{E}\tfrac{1}{\|\mathbf Y\|^2}\right) + (J - 2)^2\sigma^4\,\mathbb{E}\tfrac{1}{\|\mathbf Y\|^2} = J\sigma^2 - (J - 2)^2\sigma^4\,\mathbb{E}\tfrac{1}{\|\mathbf Y\|^2}. \] The inverse moment \(\mathbb{E}[1/\|\mathbf Y\|^2]\) is finite and strictly positive precisely when \(J \ge 3\): \(\|\mathbf Y\|^2/\sigma^2\) is a noncentral chi-square with \(J\) degrees of freedom (the distribution of a sum of \(J\) squared independent unit-variance normals with possibly nonzero means; it reduces to the ordinary chi-square when \(\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf 0\)), whose density behaves like \(r^{J/2 - 1}\) near \(0\), so \(\int_0^1 r^{-1} r^{J/2 - 1}\, dr\) converges iff \(J/2 - 1 > 0\), that is \(J \ge 3\). Therefore the subtracted term is strictly positive and the risk is strictly below \(J\sigma^2\) for every \(\boldsymbol\theta\). \(\square\)

Remark. The James-Stein estimator is exactly the shrinkage estimator Equation 8.7 with the population mean \(\mu = 0\) and the shrinkage factor \(B = (J - 2)\sigma^2/\|\mathbf Y\|^2\) estimated from the data rather than supplied by a prior. Writing \(\hat B = (J - 2)\sigma^2/\|\mathbf Y\|^2\) as an estimate of \(B = \sigma^2/(\sigma^2 + \tau^2)\) is precisely the empirical-Bayes recipe of Section 8.6: the marginal variance of each \(Y_i\) is \(\sigma^2 + \tau^2\), so \(\|\mathbf Y\|^2/(J - 2)\) is (almost) an unbiased estimate of \(\sigma^2 + \tau^2\) and \(\hat B\) estimates \(B\). Stein’s paradox is thus the statement that hierarchical shrinkage helps even when no genuine prior is believed, provided one estimates three or more means at once; this is the deepest justification for the partial pooling of meta-analysis.

Why this matters. If you are reluctant to place a prior on the between-study variance \(\tau^2\), James-Stein still licenses pooling. With three or more study effects, shrinking every raw estimate toward a common center has strictly smaller total mean squared error than reporting the raw estimates, whatever the true effects are, and with no prior at all. That is the frequentist permission slip for borrowing strength: partial pooling is not merely a Bayesian taste but a provable improvement, which is why it is the default even for analysts who never write down a hyperprior. The result is due to Stein and to James and Stein (1961), with the empirical-Bayes reading due to Efron and Morris (1975); see the work log for the bibliography. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.6 Variance components: REML and empirical Bayes

The shrinkage estimator of Section 8.5 assumed the hyperparameters \((\mu, \tau^2)\) known. In practice the between-study variance \(\tau^2\) must be estimated, and this is delicate: ordinary maximum likelihood underestimates variance components because it does not pay for the degrees of freedom spent estimating the mean. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) corrects this; empirical Bayes plugs the estimated hyperparameters back into the shrinkage formula.

8.6.1 The bias of maximum likelihood and the idea of REML

The pathology is visible in the simplest model. For \(y_1, \dots, y_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) iid, the maximum likelihood estimator of \(\sigma^2\) is \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac1n \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2\), with \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}] = \frac{n - 1}{n}\sigma^2\), biased downward by the factor \((n - 1)/n\). The reason is that \(\bar y\) is estimated from the same data, consuming one degree of freedom that the maximum likelihood denominator \(n\) ignores. REML removes the dependence on the mean by basing inference for the variance on linear combinations of the data that are orthogonal to the mean, the error contrasts, whose distribution does not involve the fixed effects at all.

Definition 8.4 (Restricted maximum likelihood) Consider the Gaussian linear model \(\mathbf y \sim \mathcal{N}_n(\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta, \mathbf V(\boldsymbol\vartheta))\), where \(\mathbf X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) has full column rank \(p\), \(\boldsymbol\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p\) are fixed effects, and \(\mathbf V(\boldsymbol\vartheta) \succ 0\) depends on variance parameters \(\boldsymbol\vartheta\). An error contrast is a linear combination \(\mathbf a^\top \mathbf y\) with \(\mathbf X^\top \mathbf a = \mathbf 0\), that is \(\mathbf a \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp\). Let \(\mathbf K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n - p)}\) have full column rank with \(\mathbf K^\top \mathbf X = \mathbf 0\), so its columns form a basis of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp\). The restricted maximum likelihood estimator of \(\boldsymbol\vartheta\) maximizes the likelihood of the transformed data \(\mathbf K^\top \mathbf y\), which is \[ \mathbf K^\top \mathbf y \sim \mathcal{N}_{n - p}\bigl(\mathbf 0, \; \mathbf K^\top \mathbf V(\boldsymbol\vartheta)\mathbf K\bigr), \] a distribution free of \(\boldsymbol\beta\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. An error contrast \(\mathbf a^\top\mathbf y\) with \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf a = \mathbf 0\) is a combination of the data that the fixed effects cannot touch: it lives in the residual space \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp\), the directions left over after the model’s fitted part is removed. Looking only at these contrasts is looking only at the residuals, and residuals carry no information about where the mean sits. That is the whole trick of REML: estimate the variance from the part of the data that says nothing about the mean, so you are not charged twice for the mean you had to estimate. The matrix \(\mathbf K\) merely collects a basis of that residual space, and the theorem below says the particular basis does not matter. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Two questions arise immediately: does the estimator depend on the arbitrary choice of contrast matrix \(\mathbf K\), and what does maximizing the contrast likelihood give in a concrete model. We answer both.

Theorem 8.7 (REML is invariant to the choice of error contrasts) The REML estimator of Definition 8.4 does not depend on the choice of full-rank contrast matrix \(\mathbf K\) with \(\mathbf K^\top \mathbf X = \mathbf 0\).

Proof. Let \(\mathbf K_1\) and \(\mathbf K_2\) be two such matrices. Each has \(n - p\) independent columns lying in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp\), and \(\dim \mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp = n - \mathrm{rank}(\mathbf X) = n - p\) by the four-subspaces theorem (Theorem 1.5) and rank-nullity (Theorem 1.3). Hence the columns of each \(\mathbf K_i\) are a basis of the same space \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf X)^\perp\), so there is an invertible \((n - p) \times (n - p)\) matrix \(\mathbf T\) with \(\mathbf K_2 = \mathbf K_1 \mathbf T\). Then \(\mathbf K_2^\top \mathbf y = \mathbf T^\top (\mathbf K_1^\top \mathbf y)\) is an invertible linear transformation of \(\mathbf K_1^\top \mathbf y\). By the change-of-variables formula, the density of \(\mathbf K_2^\top \mathbf y\) at any point equals the density of \(\mathbf K_1^\top \mathbf y\) at the corresponding point times the constant Jacobian factor \(|\det \mathbf T|^{-1}\), which does not depend on \(\boldsymbol\vartheta\). Therefore the two contrast log-likelihoods differ by an additive constant \(-\log|\det \mathbf T|\), and they are maximized at the same \(\boldsymbol\vartheta\). \(\square\)

In the balanced normal case we can carry the maximization out explicitly and see REML restore the correct denominator.

Theorem 8.8 (REML in the one-sample normal model) Let \(y_i = \mu + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) iid, \(i = 1, \dots, n \ge 2\), so \(\mathbf X = \mathbf 1_n\), \(p = 1\), and \(\mathbf V = \sigma^2 I_n\). The REML estimator of the variance is \[ \hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{REML}} = \frac{1}{n - 1}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2 = s^2, \] the usual unbiased sample variance, in contrast to \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac1n \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2\).

Proof. Choose \(\mathbf K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n - 1)}\) with orthonormal columns spanning \(\mathbf 1_n^\perp\), so \(\mathbf K^\top \mathbf 1_n = \mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbf K^\top \mathbf K = I_{n-1}\). Such a \(\mathbf K\) exists by Gram-Schmidt (Theorem 2.3) applied to any basis of \(\mathbf 1_n^\perp\). Then \(\mathbf K^\top \mathbf y \sim \mathcal{N}_{n-1}(\mathbf 0, \sigma^2 \mathbf K^\top \mathbf K) = \mathcal{N}_{n-1}(\mathbf 0, \sigma^2 I_{n-1})\), and its log-likelihood in \(\sigma^2\) is \[ \ell_R(\sigma^2) = -\frac{n - 1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\|\mathbf K^\top \mathbf y\|^2. \] Now \(\|\mathbf K^\top \mathbf y\|^2 = \mathbf y^\top \mathbf K \mathbf K^\top \mathbf y\), and because the columns of \(\mathbf K\) are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbf 1_n^\perp\), the matrix \(\mathbf K \mathbf K^\top\) is the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathbf 1_n^\perp\) (Definition 2.5), namely \(\mathbf K \mathbf K^\top = I_n - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf 1_n \mathbf 1_n^\top = I_n - \frac1n J_n\). Hence \[ \|\mathbf K^\top \mathbf y\|^2 = \mathbf y^\top\!\left(I_n - \tfrac1n J_n\right)\mathbf y = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i^2 - n\bar y^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2 =: S. \] Maximizing \(\ell_R\) over \(\sigma^2 > 0\): setting \(d\ell_R/d\sigma^2 = -\frac{n - 1}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{S}{2\sigma^4} = 0\) gives \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{REML}} = S/(n - 1)\), and the second derivative is negative there, confirming a maximum. Since \(\mathbb{E}[S] = (n - 1)\sigma^2\), the REML estimator is unbiased, whereas the maximum likelihood estimator \(S/n\) has expectation \(\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\). \(\square\)

Remark. The same construction, applied to the balanced one-way layout \(y_{ij} = \mu + \theta_i + \varepsilon_{ij}\) with \(\theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\) and \(\varepsilon_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), yields the analysis-of-variance estimators of the variance components \((\tau^2, \sigma^2)\), with the between-groups denominator reduced by one for the estimated grand mean; the general restricted log-likelihood is, up to an additive constant, \[ \ell_R(\boldsymbol\vartheta) = -\tfrac12 \log\det \mathbf V - \tfrac12 \log\det(\mathbf X^\top \mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf X) - \tfrac12 (\mathbf y - \mathbf X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})^\top \mathbf V^{-1}(\mathbf y - \mathbf X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}), \] with \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf X^\top \mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top \mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf y\) the generalized least squares estimator; the extra log-determinant term, absent from ordinary maximum likelihood, is the penalty for estimating \(\boldsymbol\beta\). REML is the default for variance-component estimation in mixed models and in meta-analysis software (Patterson and Thompson 1971; Harville 1977; Dias et al. (2018)). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

It is worth instantiating this general formula on the meta-analysis model itself, since that is the case a health researcher actually faces. The normal-normal model Equation 8.3 is the Gaussian linear model of Definition 8.4 with a single fixed effect, \(\mathbf X = \mathbf 1_J\) (so \(\boldsymbol\beta = \mu\)), and diagonal covariance \(\mathbf V(\tau^2) = \mathrm{diag}(\tau^2 + s_1^2, \dots, \tau^2 + s_J^2)\). Writing \(w_j(\tau^2) = 1/(\tau^2 + s_j^2)\) and \(\hat\mu(\tau^2) = \sum_j w_j(\tau^2)\, y_j / \sum_j w_j(\tau^2)\), the restricted log-likelihood specializes to a function of the one unknown \(\tau^2\), \[ \ell_R(\tau^2) = -\tfrac12 \sum_{j=1}^J \log(\tau^2 + s_j^2) - \tfrac12 \log\!\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_j w_j(\tau^2)\Bigr) - \tfrac12 \sum_{j=1}^J w_j(\tau^2)\,\bigl(y_j - \hat\mu(\tau^2)\bigr)^2 . \] The middle log term, absent from ordinary maximum likelihood, is the penalty for having estimated \(\mu\); it is what stops \(\hat\tau^2\) from being biased toward \(0\). Unlike the one-sample case of Theorem 8.8 there is no closed form, so software maximizes \(\ell_R(\tau^2)\) numerically over \(\tau^2 \ge 0\). The DerSimonian-Laird estimator Equation 8.8 is the quick method-of-moments approximation to the same target; REML is the likelihood-based default.

Remark. Why this matters. When metafor, the multinma package, or a hand-written Stan model reports a heterogeneity estimate \(\hat\tau^2\) for your meta-analysis or network, it is this restricted likelihood (or its moment shortcut) that produced the number. REML is preferred precisely because, as Theorem 8.8 shows in miniature, plain maximum likelihood underestimates \(\tau^2\) by ignoring the degree of freedom spent on the pooled effect; with the handful of studies typical of an indirect comparison, that downward bias is not negligible and would make the pooled effect look falsely precise. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.6.2 Empirical Bayes

Empirical Bayes estimates the hyperparameters from the marginal distribution of the data and then proceeds as if they were known. The marginal is itself a normal model, obtained by integrating out the study effects.

Proposition 8.2 (The marginal model) In the normal-normal model Equation 8.3, integrating out \(\theta_j\) gives, independently across \(j\), \[ y_j \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\mu, \; \tau^2 + s_j^2\bigr). \]

Proof. Write \(y_j = \theta_j + e_j\) with \(\theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2)\) and \(e_j \mid \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s_j^2)\), where \(e_j\) is independent of \(\theta_j\). The pair \((\theta_j, e_j)\) is jointly normal with independent components, so the linear combination \(y_j = \theta_j + e_j\) is normal by the affine-image property of the multivariate normal (Theorem 4.14), with mean \(\mathbb{E}[\theta_j] + \mathbb{E}[e_j] = \mu\) and variance \(\mathrm{Var}(\theta_j) + \mathrm{Var}(e_j) = \tau^2 + s_j^2\) by independence. Independence across \(j\) follows from the independence of the pairs \((\theta_j, e_j)\) across \(j\). \(\square\)

Given Proposition 8.2, empirical Bayes estimates \((\mu, \tau^2)\) by maximizing the marginal likelihood \(\prod_j \mathcal{N}(y_j; \mu, \tau^2 + s_j^2)\) over \((\mu, \tau^2)\), or by the method of moments, and then substitutes \((\hat\mu, \hat\tau^2)\) into the shrinkage formula Equation 8.7.

The most widely used moment estimator is due to DerSimonian and Laird (DerSimonian and Laird (1986)), and it is the single most common heterogeneity estimate a health researcher meets, so it is worth deriving rather than displaying. Its ingredients are the precision weights \(w_j = 1/s_j^2\) (a precise study earns a large weight), the precision-weighted mean \(\bar y_w = \sum_j w_j y_j / \sum_j w_j\), and Cochran’s heterogeneity statistic \[ Q = \sum_{j=1}^J w_j (y_j - \bar y_w)^2 , \] which measures how far the study estimates fall from their weighted mean, each squared discrepancy scaled by that study’s own precision. The key fact is that if there were no heterogeneity (\(\tau^2 = 0\)) then \(Q\) would average to exactly \(J - 1\); any systematic excess of \(Q\) over \(J - 1\) is therefore evidence of between-study variance, and the estimator below turns that excess into a number.

Proposition 8.3 (The DerSimonian-Laird estimator is a method-of-moments estimator) In the marginal model \(y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2 + s_j^2)\) of Proposition 8.2, with weights \(w_j = 1/s_j^2\) and Cochran’s \(Q\) as above, \[ \mathbb{E}[Q] = (J - 1) + \tau^2\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_j w_j - \sum_j w_j^2 / \sum_j w_j\Bigr). \] Equating the observed \(Q\) to this expectation, solving for \(\tau^2\), and truncating at \(0\) to keep the estimate nonnegative gives \[ \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \max\!\left\{0, \; \frac{Q - (J - 1)}{\sum_j w_j - \sum_j w_j^2 / \sum_j w_j}\right\}, \qquad \hat\mu = \bar y_w . \tag{8.8}\]

Proof. Write \(W = \sum_j w_j\) and \(v_j = \tau^2 + s_j^2 = \mathrm{Var}(y_j)\), and note the identity \(w_j v_j = 1 + w_j\tau^2\) (since \(w_j s_j^2 = 1\)). Expand \(Q = \sum_j w_j y_j^2 - W\bar y_w^2\). For the first sum, using \(\mathbb{E}[y_j^2] = \mu^2 + v_j\), \[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_j w_j y_j^2\Bigr] = \sum_j w_j(\mu^2 + v_j) = \mu^2 W + \sum_j (1 + w_j\tau^2) = \mu^2 W + J + \tau^2 W . \] For the second, \(\bar y_w = W^{-1}\sum_j w_j y_j\) has mean \(\mu\) and, by independence of the \(y_j\), \[ \mathrm{Var}(\bar y_w) = W^{-2}\sum_j w_j^2 v_j = W^{-2}\sum_j\bigl(w_j + \tau^2 w_j^2\bigr) = W^{-1} + \tau^2 W^{-2}\textstyle\sum_j w_j^2 , \] using \(w_j^2 s_j^2 = w_j\). Hence \(\mathbb{E}[W\bar y_w^2] = W\bigl(\mathrm{Var}(\bar y_w) + \mu^2\bigr) = 1 + \tau^2 W^{-1}\sum_j w_j^2 + \mu^2 W\). Subtracting the two expectations, the \(\mu^2 W\) terms cancel and \[ \mathbb{E}[Q] = \bigl(J + \tau^2 W\bigr) - \bigl(1 + \tau^2 W^{-1}\textstyle\sum_j w_j^2\bigr) = (J - 1) + \tau^2\Bigl(W - \textstyle\sum_j w_j^2 / W\Bigr), \] which is the claimed identity since \(W = \sum_j w_j\). Replacing \(\mathbb{E}[Q]\) by the observed \(Q\) and solving the resulting linear equation for \(\tau^2\) produces the displayed estimator; the truncation enforces \(\tau^2 \ge 0\). \(\square\)

The truncation at \(0\) is exactly why a random-effects analysis sometimes collapses to complete pooling: when the studies agree more closely than their standard errors alone would predict, \(Q < J - 1\), the numerator is negative, and \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = 0\), so every \(B_j \to 1\) and the estimates fuse at the common mean \(\bar y_w\).

Remark. Empirical Bayes is a computational shortcut to full Bayes: it replaces the hyperprior and the integration over \((\mu, \tau^2)\) by a point estimate \((\hat\mu, \hat\tau^2)\). The cost is that it ignores the uncertainty in \((\hat\mu, \hat\tau^2)\), so the empirical-Bayes posterior variance \(V_j = (1/\hat\tau^2 + 1/s_j^2)^{-1}\) understates the true posterior variance, the more so when \(J\) is small and \(\tau^2\) is poorly identified. Full Bayes places a hyperprior \(p(\mu, \tau^2)\) and integrates over it, propagating that uncertainty into the credible intervals for \(\theta_j\); this integral is rarely closed-form and is computed by the Markov chain Monte Carlo of Section 8.8. The James-Stein estimator of Theorem 8.6 is the empirical-Bayes estimator for \(\mu = 0\) with \(\hat B = (J - 2)\sigma^2/\|\mathbf y\|^2\), which is why it dominates the maximum likelihood estimator: estimating the shrinkage factor from the ensemble is exactly what borrowing strength means. As a rule of thumb, empirical Bayes is safe when \(J\) is large (a dozen studies or more) and misleads most when \(J\) is small: with three or four trials the credible intervals it reports for each \(\theta_j\) can be substantially too narrow, because a point estimate \(\hat\tau^2\) hides how uncertain the heterogeneity really is. Full Bayes, or a small-sample correction of the Hartung-Knapp type, is then worth the extra computation. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.7 The posterior predictive distribution and credible intervals

Two further objects complete the inferential toolkit: the distribution of future data, and the Bayesian analogue of a confidence interval.

Definition 8.5 (Posterior predictive distribution) Let \(\mathbf y\) be observed data and \(\tilde{\mathbf y}\) future data that is conditionally independent of \(\mathbf y\) given the parameter, \(\tilde{\mathbf y} \perp \mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi\). The posterior predictive distribution of \(\tilde{\mathbf y}\) given \(\mathbf y\) has density \[ p(\tilde{\mathbf y} \mid \mathbf y) = \int_\Xi p(\tilde{\mathbf y} \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\, d\boldsymbol\xi. \tag{8.9}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proof. We verify Equation 8.9. By the conditional independence assumption (Definition 4.6), the joint density of \((\tilde{\mathbf y}, \boldsymbol\xi)\) given \(\mathbf y\) factors as \[ p(\tilde{\mathbf y}, \boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y) = p(\tilde{\mathbf y} \mid \boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf y)\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y) = p(\tilde{\mathbf y} \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y), \] the last step using \(\tilde{\mathbf y} \perp \mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi\). Marginalizing over \(\boldsymbol\xi\) gives Equation 8.9. \(\square\)

Remark. The integral Equation 8.9 is computed by sampling: draw \(\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)} \sim \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) from the posterior and then \(\tilde{\mathbf y}^{(s)} \sim p(\cdot \mid \boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\) from the sampling model, so that the \(\tilde{\mathbf y}^{(s)}\) are a sample from the posterior predictive. This drives posterior predictive checks (compare statistics of the \(\tilde{\mathbf y}^{(s)}\) to the observed data; systematic discrepancy signals misfit) and out-of-sample prediction.

Reader translation, and why it matters for ITC. The posterior predictive answers “what would a new observation look like, given everything we have learned?” by averaging the sampling model over the posterior, so it carries both the natural variability of data and our remaining uncertainty about the parameters. This is exactly the shape of an indirect-comparison target estimand. A population-adjusted effect asks what a treatment contrast would be in a new target population, one we did not run a trial in; predicting that effect means drawing parameters from the posterior and pushing them through the target population’s covariate distribution, which is a posterior predictive computation. So the marginal effect reported in Part V is not a single plug-in number but a posterior predictive quantity, and its credible interval inherits parameter uncertainty through this very integral. (Corresponds to L.4 in the proof catalogue.) \(\blacktriangleleft\)

A Bayesian reports uncertainty as a region of high posterior probability.

Definition 8.6 (Credible set, equal-tailed and highest posterior density) A \((1 - \alpha)\) credible set for \(\boldsymbol\xi\) is a measurable set \(C \subseteq \Xi\) with \(\Pi(\boldsymbol\xi \in C \mid \mathbf y) = 1 - \alpha\). In one dimension, the equal-tailed interval is \((q_{\alpha/2}, q_{1 - \alpha/2})\), the interval between the \(\alpha/2\) and \(1 - \alpha/2\) posterior quantiles. The highest posterior density (HPD) set at level \(1 - \alpha\) is \(C_\lambda = \{\boldsymbol\xi : \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y) \ge \lambda\}\), with \(\lambda\) chosen so that \(\Pi(C_\lambda \mid \mathbf y) = 1 - \alpha\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The HPD set is the most economical credible region, in the precise sense of minimal volume.

Proposition 8.4 (The HPD set has minimal volume) Let the posterior density \(\pi(\cdot \mid \mathbf y)\) be defined with respect to Lebesgue measure, and let \(C = C_\lambda = \{\boldsymbol\xi : \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y) \ge \lambda\}\) be an HPD set of posterior probability \(1 - \alpha\). Then for any measurable set \(D\) with \(\Pi(D \mid \mathbf y) \ge 1 - \alpha\), we have \(\mathrm{vol}(C) \le \mathrm{vol}(D)\).

Proof. Write \(\pi\) for \(\pi(\cdot \mid \mathbf y)\) and \(\mathrm{vol}\) for Lebesgue measure. From \(\Pi(D) \ge \Pi(C)\), \[ 0 \le \Pi(D) - \Pi(C) = \int_{D \setminus C}\pi - \int_{C \setminus D}\pi. \] On \(D \setminus C\) the point is outside \(C\), so \(\pi < \lambda\) there, giving \(\int_{D \setminus C}\pi \le \lambda\,\mathrm{vol}(D \setminus C)\). On \(C \setminus D\) the point is inside \(C\), so \(\pi \ge \lambda\) there, giving \(\int_{C \setminus D}\pi \ge \lambda\,\mathrm{vol}(C \setminus D)\). Combining, \[ 0 \le \lambda\,\mathrm{vol}(D \setminus C) - \lambda\,\mathrm{vol}(C \setminus D), \] and since \(\lambda > 0\), \(\mathrm{vol}(C \setminus D) \le \mathrm{vol}(D \setminus C)\). Adding \(\mathrm{vol}(C \cap D)\) to both sides, \[ \mathrm{vol}(C) = \mathrm{vol}(C \cap D) + \mathrm{vol}(C \setminus D) \le \mathrm{vol}(C \cap D) + \mathrm{vol}(D \setminus C) = \mathrm{vol}(D). \qquad \square \]

Remark. A credible interval is a direct probability statement about the parameter given the data, \(\Pi(\theta \in C \mid \mathbf y) = 1 - \alpha\), which is the interpretation practitioners often wrongly attach to a frequentist confidence interval. For a symmetric unimodal posterior the equal-tailed and HPD intervals coincide; for skewed posteriors the HPD interval is shorter but can be harder to compute from samples, so equal-tailed intervals (posterior sample quantiles) are the common default. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.8 Markov chain Monte Carlo

Outside the conjugate families the posterior Equation 8.4 is known only up to the intractable normalizing constant \(p(\mathbf y)\), and posterior expectations \(\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol\xi) \mid \mathbf y]\) are high-dimensional integrals with no closed form. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sidesteps both problems by constructing a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior; running the chain produces dependent draws whose ergodic averages estimate posterior expectations. The construction needs only the ratio of posterior densities, in which \(p(\mathbf y)\) cancels. We develop the general theory on an abstract state space and then specialize to the posterior.

Definition 8.7 (Transition kernel and stationary distribution) A transition kernel on a measurable state space \((\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{B})\) is a map \(P: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{B} \to [0,1]\) such that \(P(x, \cdot)\) is a probability measure for each \(x\) and \(P(\cdot, B)\) is measurable for each \(B\). A probability measure \(\pi\) on \(\mathcal{X}\) is stationary (or invariant) for \(P\) if \[ \int_{\mathcal{X}} P(x, B)\, \pi(dx) = \pi(B) \qquad \text{for every } B \in \mathcal{B}. \] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Stationarity is the property that, if the chain’s current state is distributed as \(\pi\), then so is its next state; a chain started anywhere and run long enough (under further ergodicity conditions) forgets its start and produces draws from \(\pi\). The practical way to guarantee a prescribed stationary distribution is the stronger and easier-to-check condition of detailed balance.

Definition 8.8 (Detailed balance (reversibility)) A transition kernel \(P\) is reversible with respect to a probability measure \(\pi\), or satisfies detailed balance, if the two measures on \(\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X}\) defined by \(\pi(dx)P(x, dy)\) and \(\pi(dy)P(y, dx)\) are equal: \[ \pi(dx)\, P(x, dy) = \pi(dy)\, P(y, dx). \tag{8.10}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Detailed balance says the long-run flow of probability from \(x\) to \(y\) exactly matches the flow from \(y\) back to \(x\), for every pair of states. Nothing circulates on net, so once the chain is distributed as \(\pi\) it stays that way: \(\pi\) is held in place. It is a stronger condition than stationarity but far easier to check (a pointwise equation instead of an integral one), which is why every sampler in this chapter is engineered to satisfy it. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Lemma 8.3 (Detailed balance implies stationarity) If \(P\) satisfies detailed balance with respect to \(\pi\) (Definition 8.8), then \(\pi\) is stationary for \(P\).

Proof. Fix \(B \in \mathcal{B}\). Integrate the detailed-balance identity Equation 8.10 over \(x \in \mathcal{X}\) and \(y \in B\). The left side is \[ \int_{\mathcal{X}} \int_{B} \pi(dx)\, P(x, dy) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} P(x, B)\, \pi(dx), \] which is the quantity in Definition 8.7. The right side is \[ \int_{\mathcal{X}} \int_{B} \pi(dy)\, P(y, dx) = \int_{B}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}} P(y, dx)\right)\pi(dy) = \int_{B} P(y, \mathcal{X})\, \pi(dy) = \int_{B} 1 \cdot \pi(dy) = \pi(B), \] using \(P(y, \mathcal{X}) = 1\) because \(P(y, \cdot)\) is a probability measure, and Tonelli’s theorem to exchange the order of integration of the nonnegative kernel. Equating the two sides gives \(\int_{\mathcal{X}} P(x, B)\, \pi(dx) = \pi(B)\) for every \(B\), which is stationarity. \(\square\)

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm builds a reversible kernel for any target \(\pi\) from an arbitrary proposal mechanism, correcting the proposal by an accept-reject step.

Theorem 8.9 (Metropolis-Hastings has the target as stationary distribution) Let \(\pi\) be a target density on \(\mathcal{X}\) with respect to a reference measure, and let \(q(x, y)\) be a proposal density (so \(q(x, \cdot)\) is a density for each \(x\)). Define the acceptance probability \[ \alpha(x, y) = \min\!\left\{1, \; \frac{\pi(y)\, q(y, x)}{\pi(x)\, q(x, y)}\right\} \tag{8.11}\] for \(x, y\) with \(\pi(x)q(x, y) > 0\), and the Metropolis-Hastings kernel \[ P(x, dy) = \alpha(x, y)\, q(x, y)\, dy + r(x)\, \delta_x(dy), \qquad r(x) = 1 - \int \alpha(x, y)\, q(x, y)\, dy, \] where \(\delta_x\) is the point mass at \(x\) (the probability of rejecting and staying). Then \(P\) satisfies detailed balance with respect to \(\pi\), and hence (by Lemma 8.3) \(\pi\) is stationary for \(P\).

Proof. The kernel has two parts: a “move” part with density \(p(x, y) = \alpha(x, y)\, q(x, y)\) for \(y \ne x\), and a “stay” part, the atom \(r(x)\delta_x\). We check the detailed-balance identity Equation 8.10 for each.

Move part. We must show \(\pi(x)\, p(x, y) = \pi(y)\, p(y, x)\) for all \(x \ne y\), that is \[ \pi(x)\, q(x, y)\, \alpha(x, y) = \pi(y)\, q(y, x)\, \alpha(y, x). \] Fix \(x \ne y\). If either \(\pi(x)q(x,y) = 0\) or \(\pi(y)q(y,x) = 0\) the identity holds trivially (both sides are \(0\), since \(\alpha\) multiplies the vanishing factor). Otherwise both products are positive, and the ratio \(\rho = \pi(y)q(y, x)/(\pi(x)q(x, y))\) is a positive real with \(\alpha(x, y) = \min\{1, \rho\}\) and \(\alpha(y, x) = \min\{1, 1/\rho\}\). Suppose \(\rho \le 1\) (the case \(\rho > 1\) is symmetric by swapping \(x\) and \(y\)). Then \(\alpha(x, y) = \rho\) and \(\alpha(y, x) = 1\), so \[ \pi(x)\, q(x, y)\, \alpha(x, y) = \pi(x)\, q(x, y)\, \rho = \pi(x)\, q(x, y)\cdot \frac{\pi(y)\, q(y, x)}{\pi(x)\, q(x, y)} = \pi(y)\, q(y, x) = \pi(y)\, q(y, x)\, \alpha(y, x), \] as required. Thus \(\pi(x)p(x, y)\, dx\, dy\) is symmetric in \((x, y)\).

Stay part. The atomic contribution to \(\pi(dx)P(x, dy)\) is \(\pi(x)\, r(x)\, \delta_x(dy)\, dx\), a measure concentrated on the diagonal \(\{x = y\}\). The diagonal is invariant under the coordinate swap \((x, y) \mapsto (y, x)\), and on it \(\pi(x)r(x)\delta_x(dy)\,dx = \pi(y)r(y)\delta_y(dx)\,dy\) because both describe the same mass \(\pi(x)r(x)\) placed at \(x = y\). Hence the stay part also satisfies detailed balance.

Adding the move and stay parts, \(\pi(dx)P(x, dy) = \pi(dy)P(y, dx)\), so \(P\) is reversible with respect to \(\pi\). By Lemma 8.3, \(\pi\) is stationary. \(\square\)

Remark. Two practical points. First, the acceptance ratio Equation 8.11 involves \(\pi\) only through the ratio \(\pi(y)/\pi(x)\), in which the normalizing constant \(p(\mathbf y)\) cancels; this is what makes MCMC applicable to the unnormalized posterior. Second, stationarity alone does not guarantee that the time average \(\frac1N \sum_{t=1}^N g(\boldsymbol\xi^{(t)})\) converges to \(\mathbb{E}_\pi[g]\); that requires the chain to be \(\pi\)-irreducible (it can reach any set of positive \(\pi\)-measure) and aperiodic, under which a law of large numbers for Markov chains gives almost-sure convergence of ergodic averages and a central limit theorem governs the Monte Carlo error. We state this ergodic theorem as a cited result (Tierney 1994; see the work log) and use it to justify Monte Carlo estimation; its proof belongs to the general theory of Harris-recurrent chains. The original symmetric-proposal algorithm is due to Metropolis et al. (1953) and the general asymmetric form to Hastings (1970). (Corresponds to the Metropolis-Hastings content of L.3 in the proof catalogue.) \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What this means in practice, and what to check. The theory above guarantees only that the chain eventually produces draws from the posterior; it says nothing about any particular finite run. It helps to picture what the software hands back. When you fit a Bayesian meta-analysis or NMA, the sampler returns a long list of draws \(\boldsymbol\xi^{(1)}, \boldsymbol\xi^{(2)}, \dots\), and each draw is one complete, jointly plausible setting of every parameter at once: each pooled effect \(d_{1k}\), the heterogeneity \(\tau^2\), and every study-level contrast. A posterior mean is then just the average of one column of that list, a \(95\%\) credible interval is a pair of sample quantiles, and a treatment ranking is computed draw by draw and tallied. Plotted against iteration, a single parameter’s trace should look like a fuzzy horizontal band (a “grassy” caterpillar), not a slow drift or a path that sticks in one place for long stretches. Before trusting any of these numbers, check four things: (i) convergence, that several chains started from different points overlap, summarized by the potential scale reduction factor \(\hat R\) lying near \(1\); (ii) the effective sample size, the number of effectively independent draws after discounting autocorrelation, which must be large enough that the Monte Carlo error (the sampler’s own noise, distinct from genuine posterior uncertainty) is negligible; (iii) divergent transitions, warnings that the sampler could not follow the posterior geometry and that estimates may be biased; and (iv) prior sensitivity, that the conclusions do not swing with an arbitrary choice of prior. Theoretical correctness (detailed balance) and a trustworthy run (clean diagnostics) are separate matters, and an ITC result needs both. These diagnostics are developed in full in the Bayesian-computation chapter of Part V. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.9 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and the No-U-Turn Sampler

Random-walk Metropolis explores by undirected local steps and mixes slowly in high dimensions, because the proposal must be small to be accepted yet small steps decorrelate slowly. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) replaces the random walk by deterministic trajectories that follow the gradient of the log posterior, allowing distant, high-acceptance proposals. It is the sampler inside Stan and the engine of the ML-NMR software of Part V.

The physical picture is worth carrying through the algebra that follows. Imagine the negative log posterior \(-\log\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) as a landscape, with deep valleys where the posterior is high. HMC gives the current parameter value a random shove (a momentum) and lets it roll across this landscape under Newtonian mechanics for a short while, then proposes wherever the roll ends. Because the motion follows the gradient, it travels along a valley rather than diffusing across it, so one proposal can land far from the start yet still in a plausible region, which is exactly what random-walk Metropolis cannot do in high dimensions. The kinetic energy \(K(\mathbf r)\) is the cost of moving, the potential energy \(U(\boldsymbol\xi)\) is the negative log posterior, and the ideal dynamics conserve their sum; the small error from simulating the roll on a computer is what the final accept-reject step corrects. In one line: this is what runs when you call Stan on a network meta-analysis, and everything below proves that the roll-and-correct recipe targets the right distribution.

Definition 8.9 (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) To sample \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) on \(\boldsymbol\xi \in \mathbb{R}^d\), augment the state with an auxiliary momentum \(\mathbf r \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and define the Hamiltonian \[ H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) = U(\boldsymbol\xi) + K(\mathbf r), \qquad U(\boldsymbol\xi) = -\log \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y), \qquad K(\mathbf r) = \tfrac12 \mathbf r^\top M^{-1}\mathbf r, \tag{8.12}\] with a symmetric positive definite mass matrix \(M\). The joint density \(p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) \propto e^{-H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r)} = \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf r; \mathbf 0, M)\) has the target as its \(\boldsymbol\xi\)-marginal. One HMC transition from \((\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r)\) is:

  1. Momentum refresh: draw \(\mathbf r \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0, M)\).
  2. Simulate dynamics: approximate the Hamiltonian flow of \[ \dot{\boldsymbol\xi} = \nabla_{\mathbf r} H = M^{-1}\mathbf r, \qquad \dot{\mathbf r} = -\nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi} H = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi} \log \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y), \] for \(L\) steps of size \(\epsilon\) using the leapfrog integrator \[ \mathbf r^{+} = \mathbf r + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla\log\pi(\boldsymbol\xi), \quad \boldsymbol\xi' = \boldsymbol\xi + \epsilon M^{-1}\mathbf r^{+}, \quad \mathbf r' = \mathbf r^{+} + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla\log\pi(\boldsymbol\xi'), \] producing a proposal \((\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*})\) (after negating the final momentum).
  3. Metropolis correction: accept \((\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*})\) with probability \(\min\{1, \exp(H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) - H(\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*}))\}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The correctness of HMC rests on three facts about the Hamiltonian flow and its leapfrog discretization, which we prove, after which the Metropolis correction delivers the target by the detailed-balance argument of Section 8.8.

Lemma 8.4 (The Hamiltonian flow conserves energy) Along any solution of Hamilton’s equations \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi} = \nabla_{\mathbf r}H\), \(\dot{\mathbf r} = -\nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}H\), the Hamiltonian \(H\) is constant in time.

Proof. By the chain rule and Hamilton’s equations, \[ \frac{dH}{dt} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}H \cdot \dot{\boldsymbol\xi} + \nabla_{\mathbf r}H \cdot \dot{\mathbf r} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}H \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf r}H + \nabla_{\mathbf r}H \cdot (-\nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}H) = 0. \qquad \square \]

Lemma 8.5 (The leapfrog map preserves volume and is reversible) The leapfrog map \((\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) \mapsto (\boldsymbol\xi', \mathbf r')\) of Definition 8.9 has Jacobian determinant equal to \(1\), and is reversible: negating the momentum, applying the map, and negating the momentum again recovers the inverse map.

Proof. The map is the composition of three shears. The first, \((\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) \mapsto (\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r^{+})\) with \(\mathbf r^{+} = \mathbf r + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla\log\pi( \boldsymbol\xi)\), leaves \(\boldsymbol\xi\) fixed and updates \(\mathbf r\) by a function of \(\boldsymbol\xi\) alone; its Jacobian in block form is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} I & 0 \\ * & I \end{smallmatrix}\right)\), lower triangular with unit diagonal, so its determinant is \(1\). The second, \((\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r^{+}) \mapsto (\boldsymbol\xi', \mathbf r^{+})\) with \(\boldsymbol\xi' = \boldsymbol\xi + \epsilon M^{-1}\mathbf r^{+}\), updates \(\boldsymbol\xi\) by a function of \(\mathbf r^{+}\) alone; its Jacobian is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} I & * \\ 0 & I \end{smallmatrix}\right)\), again unit determinant. The third is another momentum shear, determinant \(1\). The determinant of the composition is the product, \(1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1\), so the leapfrog map preserves Lebesgue volume. Reversibility is direct: each substep is inverted by running it with \(\epsilon \mapsto -\epsilon\), and negating \(\mathbf r\) turns the forward half-steps into the corresponding backward half-steps, so the momentum-flip conjugate of the leapfrog map is its own inverse. \(\square\)

Proposition 8.5 (HMC leaves the joint distribution invariant) The HMC transition of Definition 8.9 is reversible with respect to the joint density \(p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) \propto e^{-H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r)}\), and therefore leaves the target posterior \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\) invariant as the \(\boldsymbol\xi\)-marginal.

Proof. The momentum refresh draws \(\mathbf r \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0, M)\), which is exactly the conditional \(p(\mathbf r \mid \boldsymbol\xi)\) under the joint \(e^{-H}\), so it leaves the joint invariant. The leapfrog proposal is a deterministic, volume-preserving (Lemma 8.5) involution after the momentum flip. For such a proposal the two ingredients of the general acceptance ratio Equation 8.11 each simplify: the involution makes the forward map and its reverse identical after the momentum flip, so the proposal is symmetric and the factor \(q(y,x)/q(x,y)\) equals \(1\); and volume preservation means the change-of-variables Jacobian relating the forward and reverse densities is also \(1\), so no Jacobian factor survives. The acceptance ratio therefore collapses to the pure density ratio \[ \frac{p(\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r)} = \frac{e^{-H(\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*})}}{e^{-H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r)}} = \exp\!\bigl(H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r) - H(\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*})\bigr), \] because the proposal Jacobian is \(1\) and the involution makes the forward and reverse proposal densities equal; this is the acceptance probability in step 3. By the detailed-balance computation of Theorem 8.9 applied to the joint target \(e^{-H}\), the accept-reject step is reversible with respect to \(e^{-H}\), so the joint distribution is invariant. (If the leapfrog flow were exact, energy conservation Lemma 8.4 would make \(H(\boldsymbol\xi^{*}, \mathbf r^{*}) = H(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf r)\) and every proposal would be accepted; finite \(\epsilon\) introduces an energy error that the Metropolis step corrects exactly.) Marginalizing the invariant joint over the Gaussian momentum returns \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathbf y)\). \(\square\)

Remark. HMC still requires the user to choose the trajectory length \(L\) and step size \(\epsilon\). The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) of Hoffman and Gelman (2014) eliminates the choice of \(L\) by building the leapfrog trajectory forward and backward in time, doubling its length until it begins to double back on itself (formally, until the running trajectory would make a “U-turn,” detected when the momentum becomes anti-aligned with the position displacement), then selecting the next state from the trajectory by a slice or multinomial rule that preserves detailed balance. The step size \(\epsilon\) is tuned during a warmup phase by dual averaging to hit a target acceptance rate near \(0.8\), and the mass matrix \(M\) is adapted to the posterior covariance. NUTS is the default in Stan (Carpenter et al. (2017)) and underlies the multilevel models of Part V; the geometric foundations are surveyed by Betancourt (2017) and Neal (2011). A hierarchical-model caution connects back to this chapter, and it is the single most common practical failure in Bayesian meta-analysis. In the centered parameterization \(\theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2)\), the joint posterior of \((\theta_j, \log\tau)\) has a funnel geometry: when \(\tau\) is large the study effects \(\theta_j\) are free to spread out, but when \(\tau\) is small they are squeezed into a narrow neck around \(\mu\). Picture a funnel or a trumpet bell, wide at the top and pinched at the bottom. A single leapfrog step size cannot fit both regions at once; a step tuned for the wide mouth overshoots the narrow neck, the simulated energy blows up, and the proposal is rejected as a divergent transition. The sampler then silently fails to explore small \(\tau\), biasing \(\hat\tau^2\) upward and understating heterogeneity. The remedy is the non-centered parameterization \(\theta_j = \mu + \tau\,\tilde\theta_j\) with \(\tilde\theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), which factors the dependence out so that the geometry the sampler actually traverses is a fixed unit Gaussian regardless of \(\tau\); this is the standard fix and the reason production NMA code is written in the non-centered form. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.10 Marginal likelihood and Bayes factors

Model comparison uses the same marginal likelihood that normalizes the posterior, now read as a function of the model.

Definition 8.10 (Marginal likelihood and Bayes factor) For a model \(\mathcal{M}\) with parameter \(\boldsymbol\xi\), prior \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathcal{M})\), and likelihood \(p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi, \mathcal{M})\), the marginal likelihood (or evidence) is \[ p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}) = \int p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi, \mathcal{M})\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi \mid \mathcal{M})\, d\boldsymbol\xi. \] The Bayes factor comparing models \(\mathcal{M}_1\) and \(\mathcal{M}_0\) is the ratio of their marginal likelihoods, \[ \mathrm{BF}_{10} = \frac{p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}_0)}. \] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proposition 8.6 (Posterior odds equal the Bayes factor times prior odds) Given prior model probabilities \(\pi(\mathcal{M}_0), \pi(\mathcal{M}_1) > 0\), \[ \frac{P(\mathcal{M}_1 \mid \mathbf y)}{P(\mathcal{M}_0 \mid \mathbf y)} = \mathrm{BF}_{10} \cdot \frac{\pi(\mathcal{M}_1)}{\pi(\mathcal{M}_0)}. \]

Proof. Treat the model index \(\mathcal{M}\) as a discrete parameter and apply Bayes’ theorem (Theorem 8.2) at the model level: \(P(\mathcal{M}_k \mid \mathbf y) = p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}_k)\,\pi(\mathcal{M}_k)/ p(\mathbf y)\), where \(p(\mathbf y) = \sum_k p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}_k)\pi(\mathcal{M}_k)\) is common to both. Taking the ratio for \(k = 1\) and \(k = 0\) cancels \(p(\mathbf y)\): \[ \frac{P(\mathcal{M}_1 \mid \mathbf y)}{P(\mathcal{M}_0 \mid \mathbf y)} = \frac{p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}_1)\,\pi(\mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}_0)\,\pi(\mathcal{M}_0)} = \mathrm{BF}_{10}\cdot \frac{\pi(\mathcal{M}_1)}{\pi(\mathcal{M}_0)}. \qquad \square \]

Remark. The Bayes factor is the factor by which the data update the prior odds, and it is the principled Bayesian model-comparison criterion. It is, however, sensitive to the prior on \(\boldsymbol\xi\) within each model, because \(p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M}) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\xi)]\) averages the likelihood over the prior and so depends on prior mass placed in regions of low likelihood; a diffuse prior can drive the marginal likelihood toward zero and favor the simpler model regardless of the data, the phenomenon known as Lindley’s paradox. The mechanism is spreading too thin: a prior stretched over a very wide range places only a sliver of its mass near the parameter values the data support, so the average of the likelihood over the prior, which is precisely what \(p(\mathbf y \mid \mathcal{M})\) computes, comes out small even when the best-fitting parameter value fits the data well. For this reason applied Bayesian model comparison often prefers predictive criteria such as the widely applicable information criterion or leave-one-out cross-validation, which are developed in the Bayesian-computation chapter of Part V. ( Corresponds to L.5 in the proof catalogue.) \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.11 A fully worked example

We compute a three-study normal-normal meta-analysis entirely by hand: the shrinkage estimates, their credible intervals, the empirical-Bayes heterogeneity, a direct conjugacy check, and one Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability.

Example 8.5 (Partial pooling of three studies) Three trials report log-odds-ratio estimates \[ y_1 = 0.0, \qquad y_2 = 0.6, \qquad y_3 = 1.2, \] each with the same known sampling variance \(s_j^2 = 0.25\) (standard error \(0.5\)).

Shrinkage with given hyperparameters. Take the population mean \(\mu = 0.6\) and between-study variance \(\tau^2 = 0.25\). The shrinkage factor of Equation 8.7 is the same for all three studies, \[ B_j = \frac{s_j^2}{s_j^2 + \tau^2} = \frac{0.25}{0.25 + 0.25} = \frac12, \] so \(\hat\theta_j = \tfrac12\mu + \tfrac12 y_j\). The partial-pooling estimates are \[ \hat\theta_1 = \tfrac12(0.6) + \tfrac12(0.0) = 0.30, \quad \hat\theta_2 = \tfrac12(0.6) + \tfrac12(0.6) = 0.60, \quad \hat\theta_3 = \tfrac12(0.6) + \tfrac12(1.2) = 0.90, \] each pulled halfway from its raw value toward the population mean \(0.6\). The common posterior variance is \(V_j = (1 - B_j)s_j^2 = \tfrac12 \times 0.25 = 0.125\), with posterior standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.125} = 0.35355\). The \(95\%\) credible interval for \(\theta_1\) is therefore \[ 0.30 \pm 1.96 \times 0.35355 = 0.30 \pm 0.6930 = (-0.393,\; 0.993). \]

Reading the result on the odds-ratio scale. Translating back to the scale a clinician reads makes the effect of shrinkage concrete. Trial 3’s raw log odds ratio of \(1.2\) (an odds ratio of \(e^{1.2} = 3.32\)) shrinks to \(0.90\) (an odds ratio of \(e^{0.90} = 2.46\)): partial pooling has walked the most eye-catching trial back from roughly a tripling of the odds to about a \(2.5\)-fold effect, because that trial is one imprecise result among three. For trial 1 the shrunken estimate \(0.30\) corresponds to an odds ratio of \(e^{0.30} = 1.35\), and its credible interval \((-0.393,\; 0.993)\) becomes \((e^{-0.393},\; e^{0.993}) = (0.67,\; 2.70)\) on the odds-ratio scale. That interval straddles \(1\), so even after borrowing strength the evidence for trial 1’s population remains consistent with no effect: shrinkage has changed the point estimate without manufacturing certainty.

Direct conjugacy check. Verify \(\hat\theta_1\) from first principles by Theorem 8.3. The prior is \(\theta_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0.6, 0.25)\) and the likelihood is \(y_1 = 0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_1, 0.25)\). Posterior precision \(= 1/0.25 + 1/0.25 = 4 + 4 = 8\), so \(V_1 = 1/8 = 0.125\), matching above. Posterior mean \(= V_1(\mu/\tau^2 + y_1/s_1^2) = 0.125\,(0.6/0.25 + 0/0.25) = 0.125 \times 2.4 = 0.30\), matching \(\hat\theta_1\).

Empirical Bayes by the DerSimonian-Laird estimator. Now estimate \((\mu, \tau^2)\) from the data via Equation 8.8. The weights are \(w_j = 1/s_j^2 = 4\), so \(\sum_j w_j = 12\), the weighted mean is \(\bar y_w = (4 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 0.6 + 4 \cdot 1.2)/12 = 7.2/12 = 0.60\), and \[ Q = \sum_j w_j (y_j - \bar y_w)^2 = 4\bigl[(0 - 0.6)^2 + 0 + (1.2 - 0.6)^2\bigr] = 4(0.36 + 0.36) = 2.88. \] With \(J - 1 = 2\), \(\sum_j w_j^2 = 3 \times 16 = 48\), and \(\sum_j w_j^2/\sum_j w_j = 48/12 = 4\), the denominator is \(12 - 4 = 8\), so \[ \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \frac{2.88 - 2}{8} = \frac{0.88}{8} = 0.11. \] The estimated heterogeneity is smaller than the \(\tau^2 = 0.25\) assumed above, so empirical Bayes shrinks more aggressively: \(\hat B_j = 0.25/(0.25 + 0.11) = 0.25/0.36 = 0.6944\), giving \[ \hat\theta_1 = 0.6944(0.6) + 0.3056(0.0) = 0.4167, \quad \hat\theta_2 = 0.60, \quad \hat\theta_3 = 0.6944(0.6) + 0.3056(1.2) = 0.7833. \]

One Metropolis-Hastings step. Suppose we sample the posterior \(\theta_1 \mid y_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0.30, 0.125)\) of the first display by random-walk Metropolis with a symmetric Gaussian proposal, so \(q(x, y) = q(y, x)\) and the acceptance probability Equation 8.11 reduces to \(\min\{1, \pi(y)/\pi(x)\}\). From the current state \(\theta = 0.30\) (the posterior mode) propose \(\theta' = 0.70\). Then \[ \frac{\pi(\theta')}{\pi(\theta)} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(0.70 - 0.30)^2 - (0.30 - 0.30)^2}{2 \times 0.125}\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{0.16}{0.25}\right) = e^{-0.64} = 0.5273, \] so the move is accepted with probability \(0.5273\). A draw below this threshold accepts \(\theta' = 0.70\); otherwise the chain stays at \(0.30\). Iterating such steps produces a sample from \(\mathcal{N}(0.30, 0.125)\) by Theorem 8.9. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example 8.6 (Beta-binomial shrinkage) A binary endpoint yields \(y = 8\) successes in \(n = 10\) patients, with a weakly informative prior \(p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)\) (prior mean \(0.5\), two pseudo-successes and two pseudo-failures). By Theorem 8.4 the posterior is \(\mathrm{Beta}(2 + 8, 2 + 2) = \mathrm{Beta}(10, 4)\), with posterior mean \[ \frac{10}{14} = 0.7143 = \frac{4}{14}\cdot \frac{2}{4} + \frac{10}{14}\cdot \frac{8}{10}, \] a convex combination shrinking the maximum likelihood estimate \(0.80\) toward the prior mean \(0.50\), with weight \(10/14\) on the data. The posterior standard deviation is \(\sqrt{10 \cdot 4/(14^2 \cdot 15)} = \sqrt{40/2940} = 0.1167\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

8.12 The bridge to Bayesian network meta-analysis

The random-effects meta-analysis model Equation 8.3 is the one-comparison special case of the hierarchical models that drive the rest of this book. In a network of trials comparing several treatments, each study \(j\) contributes one or more relative-effect estimates, and the study-specific true contrasts \(\delta_{j,1k}\) (treatment \(k\) versus the reference) are modeled as exchangeable draws from a common distribution, \[ \delta_{j,1k} \mid d_{1k}, \tau^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(d_{1k}, \tau^2), \] where \(d_{1k}\) is the population (pooled) relative effect to be estimated and \(\tau^2\) is the between-study heterogeneity (Dias et al. (2018)). This is precisely Definition 8.2 with the study contrasts as unit parameters; the pooled effects \(d_{1k}\) play the role of \(\mu\), and the consistency equations of the network tie the contrasts together algebraically. Because the link is typically the logit and the likelihood binomial, the posterior is non-conjugate, and estimation proceeds by the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo of Section 8.9, as implemented in the Bayesian NMA software of Part III and the multilevel network meta-regression of Part V. The shrinkage, REML, empirical-Bayes, and MCMC machinery built here is the computational substrate of every Bayesian estimate reported in those chapters; the non-collapsibility of the logit link (Definition 7.7) is the reason the population and study-level effects must be handled with the care this chapter develops.

8.14 Exercises

A starred exercise (\(\star\)) has a full worked solution in Appendix F. The first two exercises are plain-language warm-ups; the rest build up to the computations and proofs.

Exercise 8.1 (Warm-up: reading shrinkage in words (interpretation)) No calculation is required. (a) Two trials estimate the same treatment contrast; trial A has a small standard error and trial B a large one. In a random-effects meta-analysis, which of the two raw estimates is pulled harder toward the pooled mean, and why? (b) Explain in one sentence each what it means for the between-study variance to be \(\tau^2 = 0\) and for \(\tau^2\) to be very large, in terms of how much the trials borrow strength. (c) A colleague reports a shrinkage factor \(B_j = 0.8\) for a study and says “good, that means we trust the study a lot.” Correct the misunderstanding: what does \(B_j = 0.8\) actually say about that study, and which direction does it pull the estimate?

Exercise 8.2 (Warm-up: from log odds ratios to a clinical statement (interpretation)) A Bayesian meta-analysis reports, for a treatment contrast on the log-odds-ratio scale, a posterior mean of \(0.55\) with a \(95\%\) credible interval \((0.10,\; 1.00)\). (a) Convert the point estimate and both interval endpoints to the odds-ratio scale. (b) State in one plain sentence what the credible interval says, and contrast that with the (incorrect) confidence-interval interpretation a reader might reach for. (c) Does the interval provide evidence of a treatment effect on the odds scale? Answer by reference to the value \(\mathrm{OR} = 1\).

Exercise 8.3 (Shrinkage with unequal variances) Two studies report \(y_1 = 1.0\) with \(s_1^2 = 0.04\) and \(y_2 = 0.0\) with \(s_2^2 = 0.36\), and the hyperparameters are \(\mu = 0.4\), \(\tau^2 = 0.16\). (a) Compute the shrinkage factors \(B_1, B_2\), the partial-pooling estimates \(\hat\theta_1, \hat\theta_2\), and the posterior variances \(V_1, V_2\) from Equation 8.7. (b) Explain, by comparing \(B_1\) and \(B_2\), why the less precise study is shrunk more. (c) Give the \(95\%\) credible interval for each \(\theta_j\).

Exercise 8.4 (The \(n\)-sample normal update and complete pooling) For \(y_1, \dots, y_n \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)\) iid with prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)\), prove from Theorem 8.3 that the posterior precision is \(\tau_0^{-2} + n\sigma^{-2}\) and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of \(\mu_0\) and \(\bar y\). Deduce that as \(n \to \infty\) the posterior concentrates at \(\bar y\) regardless of the prior, and interpret this as the data overwhelming the prior.

Exercise 8.5 (Gamma-Poisson posterior mean as shrinkage) Using Proposition 8.1, show that the posterior mean of \(\lambda\) given \(y_1, \dots, y_n\) is \[ \mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid \mathbf y] = \frac{a + \sum_i y_i}{b + n} = \frac{b}{b + n}\cdot \frac{a}{b} + \frac{n}{b + n}\cdot \bar y, \] a convex combination of the prior mean \(a/b\) and the sample mean \(\bar y\). Identify the data weight and its limit as \(n \to \infty\).

Exercise 8.6 (Detailed balance on a finite chain) Let \(\mathcal{X} = \{1, 2, 3\}\) and let \(\pi = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)\) be a target. Take the symmetric proposal that picks one of the other two states with probability \(1/2\) each, and apply Metropolis-Hastings. (a) Write down the full \(3 \times 3\) transition matrix \(P\) with entries \(P_{xy} = q_{xy}\alpha(x, y)\) off the diagonal and the rejection mass on the diagonal. (b) Verify directly that \(\pi_x P_{xy} = \pi_y P_{yx}\) for all \(x \ne y\). (c) Confirm \(\pi P = \pi\), illustrating Lemma 8.3.

Exercise 8.7 (Equal-tailed and HPD intervals) For a posterior \(\theta \mid \mathbf y \sim \mathcal{N}(m, v)\), show that the \(95\%\) equal-tailed and \(95\%\) HPD intervals coincide and both equal \(m \pm 1.96\sqrt{v}\), and explain using Proposition 8.4 why the coincidence is special to symmetric unimodal posteriors. Then for a \(\mathrm{Gamma}(2, 1)\) posterior, argue qualitatively that the HPD interval is shifted toward smaller values relative to the equal-tailed interval.

Exercise 8.8 (Leapfrog volume preservation in one dimension) For \(d = 1\) and \(M = 1\), write the leapfrog map \((\xi, r) \mapsto (\xi', r')\) of Definition 8.9 explicitly as a function of \((\xi, r)\) for a smooth log target \(\log\pi(\xi)\), compute the \(2 \times 2\) Jacobian matrix, and verify its determinant equals \(1\), confirming Lemma 8.5 in this case.

Exercise 8.9 (Recomputing the worked example) Redo Example 8.5 with \(y = (0.2, 0.5, 1.1)\) and common \(s_j^2 = 0.20\). (a) Using \(\mu = 0.6\), \(\tau^2 = 0.20\), compute the shrinkage factor, the three partial-pooling estimates, and the posterior standard deviation. (b) Compute the DerSimonian-Laird estimate \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}\) from Equation 8.8 and the corresponding empirical-Bayes estimates. (c) Carry out one Metropolis-Hastings acceptance-probability calculation for the posterior of \(\theta_3\) with a proposed move of your choice.

Exercise 8.10 (James-Stein dominance via the unbiased risk estimate (\(\star\))) Let \(\mathbf Y \sim \mathcal{N}_J(\boldsymbol\theta, \sigma^2 I_J)\) with \(J \ge 3\) and \(\sigma^2\) known. (a) Prove Stein’s unbiased risk estimate: for \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = \mathbf Y + \mathbf g(\mathbf Y)\) with \(\mathbf g\) weakly differentiable and suitably integrable, \(\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} - \boldsymbol\theta\|^2 = \mathbb{E}[J\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf g(\mathbf Y) + \|\mathbf g(\mathbf Y)\|^2]\), using Lemma 8.2 coordinatewise. (b) Apply it to the James-Stein choice \(\mathbf g(\mathbf y) = -(J - 2)\sigma^2\mathbf y/\|\mathbf y\|^2\) to recover the risk identity of Theorem 8.6, and identify the positive-part estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{\mathrm{JS+}} = (1 - (J-2)\sigma^2/\|\mathbf Y\|^2)_{+}\mathbf Y\) as a further improvement. (c) Explain in one paragraph how this justifies the partial pooling of meta-analysis without invoking a subjective prior. (Solution in Appendix F.)

Exercise 8.11 (REML for the balanced one-way model (\(\star\))) Consider the balanced one-way random-effects model \(y_{ij} = \mu + \theta_i + \varepsilon_{ij}\), \(i = 1, \dots, I\), \(j = 1, \dots, n\), with \(\theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\) and \(\varepsilon_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), all independent. (a) Using a maximal set of error contrasts orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1_{In}\), or otherwise, derive the REML estimators of \((\sigma^2, \tau^2)\) in terms of the within-group and between-group mean squares \(\mathrm{MS}_{\mathrm{W}}\) and \(\mathrm{MS}_{\mathrm{B}}\). (b) Show that the REML between-groups computation uses the denominator \(I - 1\) (correcting for the estimated grand mean), whereas the corresponding maximum likelihood computation uses \(I\), and confirm that REML reproduces the analysis-of-variance estimators. (c) State the condition under which the REML estimate of \(\tau^2\) is truncated at zero, and relate it to Equation 8.8. (Solution in Appendix F.)
