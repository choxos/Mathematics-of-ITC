3  Linear Algebra III: Spectral Theory and Quadratic Forms

3.1 Why spectral theory matters for indirect comparison

The first two linear-algebra chapters built the stage: vector spaces, linear maps, rank, the four fundamental subspaces (Theorem 1.5), orthogonality, and the projection theorem (Theorem 2.5). Those chapters answer questions about where vectors live and how to project onto a subspace. The present chapter answers a different and deeper question: given a linear map represented by a matrix, are there special directions along which the map acts simply, by mere scaling? The answer, for the symmetric matrices that dominate statistics, is an emphatic yes, and the resulting spectral theorem is arguably the single most useful structural result in applied mathematics.

Every quantity that controls the precision of an indirect treatment comparison is, at bottom, an eigenvalue or a singular value of some matrix.

  • The covariance matrix of an estimator, for instance \(\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\) for a vector of regression coefficients, is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Its eigenvalues are the variances of the estimator along its principal axes, and the variance of any linear contrast \(\mathbf{a}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), which is exactly the form an indirect-comparison estimand takes, is the quadratic form \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\,\mathbf{a}\).
  • The Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) of a design must be positive definite for the least-squares and maximum-likelihood problems of later chapters to have a unique well-conditioned solution. When it is only positive semidefinite, because covariates are collinear or because a study reports fewer data points than parameters, the singular value decomposition and the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of this chapter supply the minimum-norm solution that the inverse can no longer provide.
  • The matrix square root, also constructed here, whitens correlated data: it turns a general covariance into the identity. This is the algebraic backbone of standardization, of the Mahalanobis distance used by the matching and distance methods of Part V, and of the Gaussian-copula constructions used for numerical integration.

We develop the theory in dependency order. We begin with eigenvalues, eigenvectors, the characteristic polynomial, and diagonalization for a general square matrix. We then specialize to symmetric matrices, proving the spectral theorem in full. From the spectral theorem we read off the theory of quadratic forms and positive definiteness, the variational (Rayleigh) characterization of eigenvalues, the singular value decomposition, the pseudoinverse, and the matrix square root. We close by noting that covariance matrices are symmetric and positive semidefinite, which is the bridge into the probability chapter (Theorem 4.9). Throughout, the reference for standard linear-algebra background is Strang (2016).

3.1.1 A running example we will reuse at every step

Before any abstraction, here is a small, concrete object to keep in mind. We will return to it after almost every definition and theorem, so that each piece of machinery has an immediate meaning you can check by hand.

Example 3.1 (Two correlated treatment-effect estimates) Two trials share a common comparator \(A\). The first reports the log odds ratio of treatment \(B\) versus \(A\), with estimate \(\hat y_{AB}\); the second reports the log odds ratio of \(C\) versus \(A\), with estimate \(\hat y_{AC}\). When the two relative effects are estimated together in a network, the estimates are correlated. Suppose their joint covariance matrix, in convenient units chosen to keep the arithmetic clean, is \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat y_{AB}\\ \hat y_{AC}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}. \] The Bucher indirect comparison of \(C\) versus \(B\) is the contrast \(\hat d_{BC}=\hat y_{AB}-\hat y_{AC}\), which is \(\mathbf{a}^\top(\hat y_{AB},\hat y_{AC})^\top\) for the contrast vector \(\mathbf{a}=(1,-1)^\top\). Everything an indirect comparison cares about, namely the uncertainty of \(\hat d_{BC}\) and the directions of large and small uncertainty in \((\hat y_{AB},\hat y_{AC})\), will turn out to be an eigenvalue, an eigenvector, or a quadratic form of this one matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This is exactly the matrix carried through the fully worked example at the end of the chapter (Section 3.11), so the threads tie together there.

3.2 Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and the characteristic polynomial

Why care about eigenvalues at all. When a matrix \(A\) describes how some system transforms vectors, most directions get both rotated and stretched, which is hard to picture. An eigenvector is a special direction that the matrix only stretches, never rotates; the stretch factor is its eigenvalue. In statistics the “system” is a covariance or information matrix, and its eigenvectors are the independent axes of variation, with the eigenvalues telling us how much variance lies along each axis. For the running covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) of Example 3.1, we will find one axis along which the two estimates vary a lot together and one along which they vary little; those axes are the eigenvectors and the amounts are the eigenvalues.

Because a real matrix can fail to have real eigenvalues, the cleanest setting in which to define eigenvalues is the complex one. We work with \(\mathbb{C}^n\), equipped with the conjugate-linear inner product \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle = \mathbf{x}^*\mathbf{y}\), where \(\mathbf{x}^* = \bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top\) is the conjugate transpose. Every real matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is in particular a complex matrix, so the definitions below apply to it.

Remark. A one-paragraph complex-number refresher (you can skip the details). A complex number \(z=a+bi\) has conjugate \(\bar z=a-bi\), and \(z\bar z=a^2+b^2\ge 0\) is its squared magnitude. For a complex vector \(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)^\top\), the conjugate transpose \(\mathbf{x}^*=\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top\) is the row vector of conjugates. We use it instead of the plain transpose because \(\mathbf{x}^*\mathbf{x}=\sum_i \bar{x}_i x_i=\sum_i |x_i|^2\ge 0\) is a genuine squared length, whereas \(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}=\sum_i x_i^2\) could be negative or even complex. That is the only reason the conjugate appears. The good news for a health researcher: the matrices we actually care about, namely symmetric covariance and Fisher information matrices, always have real eigenvalues and real eigenvectors (we prove this in Lemma 3.6), so the complex numbers are a temporary technical device that disappears the moment we specialize to symmetric matrices. For a real matrix the conjugate transpose \(A^*\) is just the ordinary transpose \(A^\top\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 3.1 (Eigenvalue and eigenvector) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\). A scalar \(\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if there exists a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^n\) with \[ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}. \] Any such \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated with \(\lambda\). For a fixed eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the set \[ E_\lambda = \{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^n : A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\} = \mathcal{N}(A-\lambda I) \] is the eigenspace of \(\lambda\); it is a subspace. The set of all eigenvalues is the spectrum of \(A\), written \(\mathrm{spec}(A)\).

The dimension of the eigenspace is itself a named quantity that we will use repeatedly. To avoid burying it, we state it on its own.

Definition 3.2 (Geometric multiplicity) The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is the dimension \(\dim E_\lambda\ge 1\) of its eigenspace, that is, the number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with \(\lambda\).

Reader translation. An eigenvector is a direction on which \(A\) acts as plain multiplication by a number, the eigenvalue. The eigenspace collects every such direction for one fixed eigenvalue, and the geometric multiplicity counts how many genuinely independent such directions there are. For a covariance matrix, an eigenvalue is a variance and its eigenvector is the direction in which that variance is felt.

To see the definition work, take the running matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) and the candidate direction \(\mathbf{v}=(1,1)^\top\). Then \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix}=3\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=3\,\mathbf{v}, \] so \((1,1)^\top\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(3\): along the direction where both estimates move together, the variance is large. We will find the other eigenvalue, \(1\), along \((1,-1)^\top\), which is exactly the indirect-contrast direction.

The defining equation \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) rearranges to \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\). A nonzero solution \(\mathbf{v}\) exists if and only if \(A-\lambda I\) is singular, that is, if and only if \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). This turns the search for eigenvalues into a problem about a single polynomial.

Definition 3.3 (Characteristic polynomial) The characteristic polynomial of \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is \[ \chi_A(t) = \det(tI - A). \tag{3.1}\]

Reader translation. Finding eigenvalues by hand would mean searching all directions for one that \(A\) merely scales. The characteristic polynomial turns that geometric search into a single algebra problem: its roots are exactly the eigenvalues. For a \(2\times 2\) matrix this is just a quadratic, which is why our running example is solvable on paper.

By the Leibniz expansion of the determinant, \(\chi_A\) is a monic polynomial in \(t\) of degree exactly \(n\), with real coefficients when \(A\) is real. The next lemma is the bridge between the algebraic object \(\chi_A\) and the geometric object \(E_\lambda\).

Lemma 3.1 (Eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial) For \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and \(\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\), the scalar \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if and only if \(\chi_A(\lambda)=0\). Consequently \(A\) has at least one and at most \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, and counted with the multiplicities defined by the factorization \(\chi_A(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n (t-\lambda_i)\) it has exactly \(n\) eigenvalues in \(\mathbb{C}\).

Proof. By Definition 3.1, \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue if and only if \(\mathcal{N}(A-\lambda I)\ne\{\mathbf{0}\}\), that is, if and only if \(A-\lambda I\) is not invertible. A square matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is nonzero (a standard fact about determinants; see Strang (2016)). Hence \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue if and only if \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). Since \(\det(A-\lambda I) = (-1)^n\det(\lambda I - A) = (-1)^n\chi_A(\lambda)\), this is equivalent to \(\chi_A(\lambda)=0\).

The polynomial \(\chi_A\) has degree \(n\), so it has at most \(n\) distinct roots. By the fundamental theorem of algebra it factors over \(\mathbb{C}\) as \(\chi_A(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n (t-\lambda_i)\) with the \(\lambda_i\in\mathbb{C}\) repeated according to multiplicity, giving exactly \(n\) eigenvalues counted this way. \(\square\)

Why this matters. This lemma is what makes eigenvalues computable: every variance axis of a covariance matrix, every curvature of a log-likelihood, is a root of one polynomial. An \(n\times n\) matrix has exactly \(n\) eigenvalues (with repeats), so an estimator with \(n\) parameters has exactly \(n\) principal variance directions to account for.

The multiplicity of \(\lambda\) as a root of \(\chi_A\) is the algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda\). The geometric and algebraic multiplicities need not agree, but they are ordered.

Lemma 3.2 (Geometric multiplicity does not exceed algebraic multiplicity) For every eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\) of \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\), \[ 1 \le \dim E_{\lambda_0} \le (\text{algebraic multiplicity of } \lambda_0). \]

Proof. Write \(g=\dim E_{\lambda_0}\ge 1\) and choose a basis \(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_g\) of \(E_{\lambda_0}\). Extend it to a basis \(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\) of \(\mathbb{C}^n\) (every linearly independent set extends to a basis; see Definition 1.5 and Strang (2016)) and assemble the columns into an invertible matrix \(S=[\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n]\). Because \(A\mathbf{v}_i=\lambda_0\mathbf{v}_i\) for \(i\le g\), the matrix \(S^{-1}AS\) is block upper triangular, \[ S^{-1}AS = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_0 I_g & B \\ \mathbf{0} & C \end{pmatrix}, \] for some blocks \(B\) and \(C\). To see why the lower-left block is zero, read \(S^{-1}AS\) column by column. Its first \(g\) columns are \(S^{-1}(A\mathbf{v}_i)=S^{-1}(\lambda_0\mathbf{v}_i)=\lambda_0\,S^{-1}\mathbf{v}_i =\lambda_0\mathbf{e}_i\) for \(i\le g\), because \(S^{-1}\mathbf{v}_i=\mathbf{e}_i\) (the \(i\)-th column of \(S^{-1}S=I\)). The vector \(\lambda_0\mathbf{e}_i\) has zeros in rows \(g+1,\dots,n\), so the first \(g\) columns of \(S^{-1}AS\) are exactly the first \(g\) columns of \(\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_0 I_g, C)\) with \(B\) sitting on top; that is the displayed block form. Similar matrices share a characteristic polynomial, since \(\det(tI - S^{-1}AS) = \det\!\big(S^{-1}(tI-A)S\big)=\det(tI-A)\) by multiplicativity of the determinant. The determinant of a block upper-triangular matrix is the product of the determinants of its diagonal blocks, so \[ \chi_A(t) = \det(tI - S^{-1}AS) = (t-\lambda_0)^g\,\det(tI_{n-g}-C). \] Thus \((t-\lambda_0)^g\) divides \(\chi_A\), so the algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda_0\) is at least \(g\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. The gap between the two multiplicities is exactly the trouble. When the geometric multiplicity is strictly smaller than the algebraic one, a repeated eigenvalue does not supply enough independent directions to build a coordinate system, and the matrix cannot be diagonalized. The spectral theorem (Theorem 3.2) will show that this pathology never occurs for symmetric matrices, which is why covariance and information matrices are always so well behaved.

Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues carry independent information. The following lemma is the engine behind diagonalization.

Lemma 3.3 (Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent) Let \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k\) be distinct eigenvalues of \(A\) with associated eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_k\). Then \(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_k\) are linearly independent.

Proof. We argue by induction on \(k\). For \(k=1\) the single eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_1\) is nonzero, hence independent. Assume the claim for \(k-1\) and suppose \[ c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}. \tag{$\ast$} \] Apply \(A\) to (\(\ast\)) and use \(A\mathbf{v}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i\): \[ c_1\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\lambda_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}. \] Subtract \(\lambda_k\) times (\(\ast\)) from this equation; the \(\mathbf{v}_k\) term cancels, leaving \[ c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_k)\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_{k-1}(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_k)\mathbf{v}_{k-1} = \mathbf{0}. \] By the inductive hypothesis \(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\) are independent, so each coefficient vanishes: \(c_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_k)=0\) for \(i\le k-1\). Since the eigenvalues are distinct, \(\lambda_i-\lambda_k\ne 0\), forcing \(c_i=0\) for \(i\le k-1\). Then (\(\ast\)) reduces to \(c_k\mathbf{v}_k=\mathbf{0}\), and \(\mathbf{v}_k\ne\mathbf{0}\) gives \(c_k=0\). All coefficients vanish, so the eigenvectors are independent. \(\square\)

3.3 Diagonalization

Definition 3.4 (Diagonalizable and similar matrices) Two matrices \(A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) are similar if \(B=S^{-1}AS\) for some invertible \(S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) (or \(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\)). The matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix, that is, if there exist an invertible \(S\) and a diagonal \(D\) with \(S^{-1}AS=D\).

Reader translation. Two similar matrices are the same linear map written in two coordinate systems; \(S\) is the dictionary that translates between them. (Similarity is an equivalence relation, and similar matrices share rank, determinant, trace, and spectrum, so these quantities describe the map, not the coordinates.) To diagonalize is to find a coordinate system in which the map acts independently along each axis: no axis leaks into another, and the map is just a list of scalings. That is the cleanest possible description of a matrix, and the rest of the chapter is about when it is achievable.

Theorem 3.1 (Diagonalization criterion) A matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is diagonalizable if and only if \(\mathbb{C}^n\) has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of \(A\), equivalently if and only if \(A\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors. When \(S=[\mathbf{s}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{s}_n]\) has these eigenvectors as columns, then \(S^{-1}AS=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\), where \(A\mathbf{s}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{s}_i\).

Proof. Suppose \(A\) is diagonalizable, with \(S^{-1}AS=D=\mathrm{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)\) for an invertible \(S\) with columns \(\mathbf{s}_1,\dots,\mathbf{s}_n\). Multiplying on the left by \(S\) gives \(AS=SD\). Reading this equality column by column, \(A\mathbf{s}_i=d_i\mathbf{s}_i\) for each \(i\). Because \(S\) is invertible its columns are linearly independent and nonzero, so each \(\mathbf{s}_i\) is an eigenvector and they form a basis of \(\mathbb{C}^n\).

Conversely, suppose \(\mathbf{s}_1,\dots,\mathbf{s}_n\) are linearly independent eigenvectors, \(A\mathbf{s}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{s}_i\). Form \(S=[\mathbf{s}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{s}_n]\), which is invertible because its columns are independent. Then the \(i\)-th column of \(AS\) is \(A\mathbf{s}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{s}_i\), which is the \(i\)-th column of \(S\Lambda\) with \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\). Hence \(AS=S\Lambda\), so \(S^{-1}AS=\Lambda\) is diagonal. \(\square\)

Why this matters. Diagonalizability is not symbol manipulation; it is the existence of a coordinate system in which the matrix becomes a diagonal list of eigenvalues. Once we reach symmetric matrices, this coordinate system will be an orthonormal one (a pure rotation), which is what lets us interpret a covariance matrix as independent variances along perpendicular axes.

Corollary 3.1 (Distinct eigenvalues force diagonalizability) If \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, then \(A\) is diagonalizable.

Proof. Choose one eigenvector for each of the \(n\) distinct eigenvalues. By Lemma 3.3 these \(n\) eigenvectors are linearly independent, so they form a basis of \(\mathbb{C}^n\). Apply Theorem 3.1. \(\square\)

The converse of the corollary is false, and not every matrix is diagonalizable.

Definition 3.5 (Defective matrix) A matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is defective if it does not have \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors, equivalently if some eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity strictly less than its algebraic multiplicity. By Theorem 3.1 a defective matrix is not diagonalizable.

Reader translation. A defective matrix has a repeated eigenvalue that did not bring enough independent directions with it, so there is no full set of axes to diagonalize in. The next example shows the smallest one. The payoff of the spectral theorem is that symmetric matrices, and therefore all covariance and information matrices, are never defective.

Remark. Distinct eigenvalues are sufficient but not necessary: the identity \(I_n\) has the single eigenvalue \(1\) with multiplicity \(n\) yet is already diagonal. Diagonalizability can also fail outright. The matrix \[ N=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \] has \(\chi_N(t)=t^2\), so its only eigenvalue is \(0\) with algebraic multiplicity \(2\), but \(E_0=\mathcal{N}(N)=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{e}_1\}\) has dimension \(1\). The geometric multiplicity is strictly less than the algebraic multiplicity, so \(N\) has only one independent eigenvector and, by Theorem 3.1, is not diagonalizable; it is defective in the sense of Definition 3.5. The spectral theorem below shows that symmetric matrices are never defective. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

3.4 Trace and determinant as symmetric functions of the eigenvalues

Two scalar summaries of a matrix, the trace \(\mathrm{tr}(A)=\sum_i a_{ii}\) and the determinant \(\det(A)\), turn out to be the simplest symmetric functions of the eigenvalues. These two numbers are not idle curiosities for a statistician. For a covariance matrix the trace is the total variance (the sum of the variances along the principal axes), and the determinant is the generalized variance, which is the squared volume of the confidence ellipsoid. The results below say both are read directly off the eigenvalues, which is exactly why principal component analysis can speak of the share of total variance “explained” by each axis. We first record a small but heavily used fact.

Lemma 3.4 (Cyclic invariance of the trace) For \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) and \(C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\), \(\mathrm{tr}(BC)=\mathrm{tr}(CB)\). In particular \(\mathrm{tr}(S^{-1}AS)=\mathrm{tr}(A)\), so the trace is a similarity invariant.

Proof. Compute directly: \[ \mathrm{tr}(BC)=\sum_{i=1}^m (BC)_{ii}=\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n B_{ij}C_{ji} =\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{i=1}^m C_{ji}B_{ij}=\sum_{j=1}^n (CB)_{jj}=\mathrm{tr}(CB). \] Applying this with \(B=S^{-1}A\) and \(C=S\) gives \(\mathrm{tr}(S^{-1}AS)=\mathrm{tr}(ASS^{-1})=\mathrm{tr}(A)\). \(\square\)

Lemma 3.5 (Trace and determinant from the spectrum) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) have eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\in\mathbb{C}\) listed with algebraic multiplicity. Then \[ \mathrm{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i, \qquad \det(A)=\prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i . \]

Proof. By Lemma 3.1 we may factor \(\chi_A(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n (t-\lambda_i)\). Expanding, \[ \prod_{i=1}^n (t-\lambda_i) = t^n - \Big(\sum_i\lambda_i\Big)t^{n-1} + \cdots + (-1)^n\prod_i\lambda_i . \tag{1} \] We now compute the same coefficients from \(\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)\) using the Leibniz expansion \[ \det(tI-A)=\sum_{\pi\in S_n}\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^n\big(t\,\delta_{i,\pi(i)}-a_{i,\pi(i)}\big), \] where \(S_n\) is the set of permutations of \(\{1,\dots,n\}\). A factor \(t\,\delta_{i,\pi(i)}-a_{i,\pi(i)}\) contains the variable \(t\) only when \(\pi(i)=i\), so the degree in \(t\) of the term indexed by \(\pi\) equals the number of fixed points of \(\pi\) (a permutation \(\pi\) contributes one factor of \(t\) for each index it leaves fixed).

It helps to see the pattern for \(n=2\) before the general count. There the two permutations are the identity, contributing \((t-a_{11})(t-a_{22})=t^2-(a_{11}+a_{22})t+a_{11}a_{22}\), and the swap, with no fixed points, contributing \(-a_{12}a_{21}\) (degree \(0\) in \(t\)). So \(\chi_A(t)=t^2-\mathrm{tr}(A)\,t+ \det(A)\): the identity alone produces the \(t^{n-1}\) coefficient, exactly the claim we now prove in general.

Constant term. Set \(t=0\): \(\chi_A(0)=\det(-A)=(-1)^n\det(A)\). Comparing with (1) evaluated at \(t=0\), namely \((-1)^n\prod_i\lambda_i\), gives \(\det(A)=\prod_i\lambda_i\).

Coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\). A permutation contributes to \(t^{n}\) or \(t^{n-1}\) only if it has at least \(n-1\) fixed points. No permutation has exactly \(n-1\) fixed points, because fixing \(n-1\) of the \(n\) symbols forces the last one to be fixed as well (a permutation is a bijection, so once \(n-1\) outputs are pinned the remaining input has only one possible output left). Hence the only contributor is the identity permutation, whose term is \(\prod_{i=1}^n (t-a_{ii})\). The coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\) in this product is \(-\sum_i a_{ii}=-\mathrm{tr}(A)\). Comparing with (1), \(\mathrm{tr}(A)=\sum_i\lambda_i\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. Read for a covariance matrix, this lemma says total variance \(\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\sum_i\lambda_i\) is the sum of the principal-axis variances, and generalized variance \(\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\prod_i\lambda_i\) is their product. A single zero eigenvalue makes the determinant zero, the signature of a perfectly collinear direction with no variation; principal component analysis “explains” variance precisely by reporting each \(\lambda_i\) as a share of \(\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\).

For the symmetric matrices treated next, both identities have a one-line proof from the spectral decomposition \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\): by Lemma 3.4, \(\mathrm{tr}(A)=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda Q^\top Q)=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda)=\sum_i\lambda_i\), and by multiplicativity of the determinant together with \(\det(Q)^2=\det(Q^\top Q)=\det(I)=1\), \(\det(A)=\det(Q)\det(\Lambda)\det(Q^\top)=\det(\Lambda)=\prod_i\lambda_i\).

3.5 Symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem

Recall from Chapter 2 that a matrix \(Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is orthogonal if \(Q^\top Q=I_n\), equivalently \(QQ^\top=I_n\), equivalently the columns of \(Q\) form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and norms: \(\langle Q\mathbf{x},Q\mathbf{y}\rangle = \mathbf{x}^\top Q^\top Q\mathbf{y}=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\). A matrix \(A\) is symmetric if \(A^\top=A\).

Symmetric matrices are the best-behaved matrices in all of linear algebra. We prove three facts in sequence: their eigenvalues are real, eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal, and there is always a full orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. The last statement is the spectral theorem.

Why this matters, stated up front. It is worth knowing the payoff before grinding through the proof. The spectral theorem is the reason every covariance matrix can be rotated into independent variances (this is principal component analysis), the reason a covariance matrix has a well-defined square root (this is whitening and the Mahalanobis distance), and the reason the quadratic forms of the next section reduce to simple weighted sums of squares. Because \(A^\top=A\) describes covariance, correlation, Gram, Hessian, and Fisher information matrices alike, this one theorem underwrites almost every precision calculation in the book. (A reminder of two symbols from the Notation chapter: \(\mathcal{N}(A)\) is the null space of \(A\) and \(\mathcal{C}(A)\) is its column space.)

Lemma 3.6 (Real eigenvalues and a real eigenvector) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric. Then every eigenvalue of \(A\) is real, and every eigenvalue has a real eigenvector.

Proof. Let \(\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\) be an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^n\), \(\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}\). Consider the scalar \(\mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v}\), where \(\mathbf{v}^*= \bar{\mathbf{v}}^\top\). Because \(A\) is real and symmetric, \(A^*=\bar A^\top=A^\top=A\) (for a real matrix the conjugate transpose is just the transpose), so, using that the conjugate transpose of a product reverses the order, \((\mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v})^* = \mathbf{v}^*A^*(\mathbf{v}^*)^*\) with \((\mathbf{v}^*)^*=\mathbf{v}\), giving \[ (\mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v})^* = \mathbf{v}^*A^*\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v}. \] A scalar equal to its own conjugate is real, so \(\mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}\). Likewise \(\mathbf{v}^*\mathbf{v}=\sum_i|v_i|^2=\|\mathbf{v}\|^2\) is a positive real number. From \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) we get \(\mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\,\mathbf{v}^*\mathbf{v}\), so \[ \lambda = \frac{\mathbf{v}^*A\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}^*\mathbf{v}} \] is a ratio of two real numbers, hence real.

Since \(\lambda\) is real and \(A\) is real, the matrix \(A-\lambda I\) is a real matrix that is singular (by Lemma 3.1 its determinant is zero). A real singular matrix has a nonzero real vector in its null space, namely a real solution of the real homogeneous system \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\). That vector is a real eigenvector for \(\lambda\). \(\square\)

Lemma 3.7 (Orthogonality of eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric, and let \(A\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{u}\), \(A\mathbf{w}=\mu\mathbf{w}\) with \(\lambda\ne\mu\). Then \(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{w}=0\).

Proof. Using \(A^\top=A\), \[ \lambda\,\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{w}=(\lambda\mathbf{u})^\top\mathbf{w}=(A\mathbf{u})^\top\mathbf{w} =\mathbf{u}^\top A^\top\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}^\top A\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}^\top(\mu\mathbf{w}) =\mu\,\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{w}. \] Hence \((\lambda-\mu)\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{w}=0\), and since \(\lambda\ne\mu\) we conclude \(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{w}=0\). \(\square\)

We now prove the central theorem of the chapter. The proof is by induction on \(n\) and uses only the existence of one real eigenvector (Lemma 3.6), the invariance of the orthogonal complement, and the ability to extend an orthonormal set to an orthonormal basis (Gram-Schmidt, Theorem 2.3).

Theorem 3.2 (Spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric. Then there exist an orthogonal matrix \(Q=[\mathbf{q}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{q}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and a real diagonal matrix \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\) such that \[ A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top . \tag{3.2}\] The columns \(\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\) form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(A\), with \(A\mathbf{q}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{q}_i\), and the \(\lambda_i\) are exactly the eigenvalues of \(A\) repeated with multiplicity. In particular every real symmetric matrix is diagonalizable, by an orthogonal change of basis, and is never defective.

The proof is the longest in the chapter, so here is the plan before the details. It is an induction on the size \(n\), peeling off one eigenvector at a time.

Step What happens
Base case a \(1\times 1\) matrix is already diagonal
Step 1: first eigenvector a real unit eigenvector \(\mathbf{q}_1\) exists by Lemma 3.6
Step 2: invariant complement the hyperplane \(\mathbf{q}_1^\perp\) is mapped into itself by \(A\)
Step 3: shrink the problem \(A\) restricted to that hyperplane is a smaller symmetric matrix; apply the inductive hypothesis
Step 4: assemble glue \(\mathbf{q}_1\) to the smaller orthonormal eigenbasis to get the full one

Proof. Base case. We induct on \(n\). For \(n=1\) the matrix \(A=(a_{11})\) is already diagonal, with \(Q=(1)\) and \(\Lambda=(a_{11})\).

Step 1: the first eigenvector. Assume the theorem for all symmetric matrices of size \(n-1\), and let \(A\) be symmetric of size \(n\). By Lemma 3.6, \(A\) has a real eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) with a real eigenvector, which we scale to unit length: \(A\mathbf{q}_1=\lambda_1\mathbf{q}_1\), \(\|\mathbf{q}_1\|=1\).

Step 2: the invariant complement. Let \[ W=\mathbf{q}_1^\perp=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n:\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{x}=0\}, \] a subspace of dimension \(n-1\) by Theorem 1.3. The subspace \(W\) is \(A\)-invariant, meaning that once a vector lies in \(W\), applying \(A\) keeps it in \(W\): if \(\mathbf{x}\in W\) then, using symmetry, \[ \mathbf{q}_1^\top(A\mathbf{x})=(A\mathbf{q}_1)^\top\mathbf{x} =\lambda_1\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{x}=0, \] so \(A\mathbf{x}\in W\). This is what lets us reduce the problem to one dimension lower.

Step 3: shrink the problem. Recall \(W\) has dimension \(n-1\). By Theorem 2.3 choose an orthonormal basis of \(W\) and assemble it as the columns of a matrix \(Q_1\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-1)}\), so that \(Q_1^\top Q_1=I_{n-1}\), the columns of \(Q_1\) span \(W\), and \(Q_1^\top\mathbf{q}_1=\mathbf{0}\) because the columns of \(Q_1\) lie in \(W=\mathbf{q}_1^\perp\). Define the \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) matrix \[ B=Q_1^\top A Q_1 . \] It is symmetric, since \(B^\top=Q_1^\top A^\top Q_1=Q_1^\top A Q_1=B\). By the inductive hypothesis there are an orthogonal \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}\) and a real diagonal \(\Lambda'=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n)\) with \(B=R\Lambda'R^\top\).

Step 4: assemble. Set \(\tilde Q=[\mathbf{q}_1\ \mid\ Q_1R]\). Its columns are orthonormal: \(\mathbf{q}_1\) has unit norm, the columns of \(Q_1R\) have the form \(Q_1\mathbf{r}\) for orthonormal \(\mathbf{r}\) and satisfy \((Q_1R)^\top(Q_1R)=R^\top Q_1^\top Q_1 R=R^\top R=I_{n-1}\), and they are orthogonal to \(\mathbf{q}_1\) because \((Q_1R)^\top\mathbf{q}_1=R^\top Q_1^\top\mathbf{q}_1=\mathbf{0}\). Hence \(\tilde Q\) is orthogonal. Compute \[ \tilde Q^\top A\tilde Q =\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_1^\top A\mathbf{q}_1 & \mathbf{q}_1^\top A Q_1 R\\ (Q_1R)^\top A\mathbf{q}_1 & (Q_1R)^\top A Q_1 R\end{pmatrix}. \] The diagonal scalar is \(\mathbf{q}_1^\top A\mathbf{q}_1=\lambda_1\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{q}_1=\lambda_1\). The off-diagonal blocks vanish: since \(A\mathbf{q}_1=\lambda_1\mathbf{q}_1\) and \(Q_1^\top\mathbf{q}_1= \mathbf{0}\), \[ (Q_1R)^\top A\mathbf{q}_1=R^\top Q_1^\top(\lambda_1\mathbf{q}_1)=\lambda_1 R^\top Q_1^\top\mathbf{q}_1 =\mathbf{0}, \] and the upper block is its transpose, also zero. The lower-right block is \((Q_1R)^\top A(Q_1R)=R^\top(Q_1^\top A Q_1)R=R^\top B R=\Lambda'\). Therefore \[ \tilde Q^\top A\tilde Q=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1 & \mathbf{0}^\top\\\mathbf{0}&\Lambda'\end{pmatrix} =\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n)=:\Lambda, \] which rearranges to \(A=\tilde Q\Lambda\tilde Q^\top\). Taking \(Q=\tilde Q\) completes the induction. The identity \(A=\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top\) is the column-wise expansion of \(Q\Lambda Q^\top\), and reading \(AQ=Q\Lambda\) column by column shows \(A\mathbf{q}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{q}_i\), so each \(\mathbf{q}_i\) is an eigenvector and each \(\lambda_i\) an eigenvalue. That the listed \(\lambda_i\) are all the eigenvalues with multiplicity follows because \(\chi_A=\chi_\Lambda=\prod_i(t-\lambda_i)\) by similarity. \(\square\)

The expansion Equation 3.2 deserves a second reading, and the interpretation is important enough to record as a proposition.

Proposition 3.1 (Spectral decomposition as a sum of orthogonal projections) Let \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) be a spectral decomposition of a symmetric matrix, with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\). Each rank-one matrix \(P_i=\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top\) is the orthogonal projection onto the eigenline \(\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{q}_i\}\), and \[ A=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i P_i=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(A)}\lambda\,P_{E_\lambda}, \] where \(P_{E_\lambda}\) is the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace \(E_\lambda\). The eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal, and every vector of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) splits uniquely into one piece in each eigenspace.

Proof. Each \(P_i\) is symmetric and idempotent, \(P_i^2=\mathbf{q}_i(\mathbf{q}_i^\top\mathbf{q}_i) \mathbf{q}_i^\top=P_i\), so by the projection theorem (Theorem 2.5) and the orthonormal-basis formula for an orthogonal projection matrix (Definition 2.5) it is exactly the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{q}_i\}\). Summing the rank-one expansion Equation 3.2 gives \(A=\sum_i\lambda_i P_i\). Grouping the terms with a common eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the corresponding \(\mathbf{q}_i\) span \(E_\lambda\) and their rank-one projections add to \(P_{E_\lambda}\), yielding the grouped form. Distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal by Lemma 3.7, and since the \(n\) eigenvectors form a basis, every vector decomposes uniquely across the eigenspaces. (In the language of Section 3.5, this last property is summarized by saying the eigenspaces form a direct sum equal to \(\mathbb{R}^n\), written with the direct-sum symbol as \(\mathbb{R}^n=\bigoplus_\lambda E_\lambda\): a direct sum is a sum of subspaces in which each vector has exactly one decomposition.) \(\square\)

Why this matters. This is the bridge from abstract algebra to geometry. A symmetric matrix is nothing more than a set of perpendicular directions, each with a number attached: it scales the part of any vector lying along \(\mathbf{q}_i\) by \(\lambda_i\) and adds the results. For a covariance matrix the \(\mathbf{q}_i\) are the principal axes of the data cloud and the \(\lambda_i\) are the variances along them.

Remark. Reader checkpoint. If you retain one thing from this section, retain this: a symmetric matrix can be rotated into a set of independent perpendicular axes; the eigenvalues tell you the strength, or variance, along each axis; and nothing is lost in the rotation. This is precisely why covariance matrices, however large, are manageable, and it is the engine of principal component analysis. The proofs above are there for rigor; on a first reading you may use the result and move on. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

For the running covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) the proposition reads, with \(\mathbf{q}_+=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{q}_-=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^\top\), \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=3\,\mathbf{q}_+\mathbf{q}_+^\top+1\,\mathbf{q}_-\mathbf{q}_-^\top =3\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}+1\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}. \] The “both estimates move together” axis carries variance \(3\); the indirect-contrast axis \((1,-1)\) carries variance \(1\) per unit length.

Remark. Functions of a symmetric matrix. Once \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) is in hand, any function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) defined on the eigenvalues extends to \(A\) by acting on the spectrum: \[ f(A):=Q\,f(\Lambda)\,Q^\top,\qquad f(\Lambda)=\mathrm{diag}\big(f(\lambda_1),\dots,f(\lambda_n)\big). \] The idea is that \(A\) does nothing but scale along its axes, so to apply a function to \(A\) we apply the function to those scalings and leave the axes alone. The smallest instance: if \(A=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(4,9)\,Q^\top\), then its square root is \(A^{1/2}=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(2,3)\,Q^\top\) and its inverse is \(A^{-1}=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac14,\tfrac19)\,Q^\top\); one simply takes \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) or \(1/(\cdot)\) of each eigenvalue. This is legitimate, and not just a convenient definition: it agrees with the algebraic meaning whenever \(f\) is a polynomial, because \(A^k=Q\Lambda^k Q^\top\) (the \(Q^\top Q=I\) in the middle collapses), and it is independent of the choice of \(Q\) because \(f(A)\) acts as multiplication by \(f(\lambda)\) on each eigenspace \(E_\lambda\). We use the cases \(f(t)=t^{-1}\) (the inverse, when all \(\lambda_i\ne 0\)) and \(f(t)=\sqrt{t}\) (the square root, when all \(\lambda_i\ge 0\)) below. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

3.6 Quadratic forms and definiteness

Definition 3.6 (Quadratic form) A quadratic form on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a function \(q:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) of the shape \[ q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}x_i x_j, \] where \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\). The matrix \(A\) may always be taken symmetric, and the symmetric matrix representing a given \(q\) is unique.

Proof. We justify the two claims in the definition. For any \(M\), since \(\mathbf{x}^\top M\mathbf{x}\) is a scalar it equals its own transpose, \(\mathbf{x}^\top M\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top M^\top\mathbf{x}\), so \[ \mathbf{x}^\top M\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top\Big(\tfrac{M+M^\top}{2}\Big)\mathbf{x}, \] and the symmetric part \(\tfrac{1}{2}(M+M^\top)\) represents the same form. For uniqueness, suppose symmetric matrices \(A\) and \(A'\) satisfy \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top A'\mathbf{x}\) for all \(\mathbf{x}\), and set \(D=A-A'\), which is symmetric with \(\mathbf{x}^\top D\mathbf{x}=0\) for all \(\mathbf{x}\). Taking \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_i\) gives \(D_{ii}=0\); taking \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_i+\mathbf{e}_j\) gives \(D_{ii}+2D_{ij}+D_{jj}=0\), hence \(D_{ij}=0\). So \(D=0\) and \(A=A'\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. A quadratic form is a weighted sum of squares and cross-products of the coordinates; the matrix \(A\) just stores the weights. The statistical instance to keep in mind is \(A=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\): the quadratic form \(\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a}\) is the variance of the linear combination \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}\) (we prove this in Section 3.10). For our running example with \(\mathbf{a}=(1,-1)^\top\), \[ \mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a} =\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} =2-1-1+2=2, \] which is the variance of the indirect comparison \(\hat d_{BC}=\hat y_{AB}-\hat y_{AC}\). Quadratic forms are how every variance in this book is computed.

From now on the matrix of a quadratic form is symmetric. Definiteness classifies a symmetric matrix by the sign of its quadratic form.

Definition 3.7 (Definiteness) A symmetric matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is

  • positive definite, written \(A\succ 0\), if \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}>0\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\);
  • positive semidefinite, written \(A\succeq 0\), if \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\ge 0\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\);
  • negative definite (\(A\prec 0\)) or negative semidefinite (\(A\preceq 0\)) if \(-A\) is positive definite or positive semidefinite;
  • indefinite if \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\) takes both signs.

For symmetric \(A,B\) we write \(A\succeq B\) to mean \(A-B\succeq 0\), and likewise \(A\succ B\).

Reader translation. Definiteness is the sign of the quadratic form, read as a verdict on every direction at once. Positive definite means every direction has strictly positive value: for a covariance matrix, every contrast has strictly positive variance, and for a curvature (Hessian) matrix, the surface curves up in every direction. Positive semidefinite weakens this to “nonnegative”, allowing a flat direction; a flat direction in a covariance matrix is a linear combination with zero variance, that is, a perfectly collinear direction. Indefinite means the form goes up in some directions and down in others, a saddle. The smallest indefinite matrix is \[ B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{x}^\top B\mathbf{x}=x_1^2-x_2^2, \] with eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\); the form is positive along \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and negative along \(\mathbf{e}_2\). The notation \(A\succeq B\) extends the symbol \(\succeq 0\) from the Notation chapter: read \(A\succeq B\) as “\(A-B\) is positive semidefinite”, which for covariance matrices means \(A\) is at least as variable as \(B\) in every direction.

The spectral theorem reduces every question about a quadratic form to a question about signs of eigenvalues, because an orthogonal change of variables turns the form into a weighted sum of squares.

Theorem 3.3 (Eigenvalue characterization of definiteness) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\) (all real by Lemma 3.6). Then

  1. \(A\succ 0\) if and only if \(\lambda_i>0\) for every \(i\);
  2. \(A\succeq 0\) if and only if \(\lambda_i\ge 0\) for every \(i\);
  3. \(A\) is indefinite if and only if \(A\) has both a positive and a negative eigenvalue.

Consequently a positive definite matrix is invertible with \(\det(A)=\prod_i\lambda_i>0\), and a positive semidefinite matrix has \(\det(A)\ge 0\) and \(\mathrm{tr}(A)\ge 0\).

Proof. Write \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) by Theorem 3.2 and change variables \(\mathbf{y}=Q^\top\mathbf{x}\). Because \(Q\) is orthogonal, the map \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{y}\) is a bijection of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(\mathbf{x}=Q\mathbf{y}\), and \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) if and only if \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}\). Then \[ \mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top\mathbf{x} =\mathbf{y}^\top\Lambda\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i y_i^2 . \tag{3.3}\]

Claim 1. If every \(\lambda_i>0\) and \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\), then \(\mathbf{y}\ne\mathbf{0}\), so \(\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2>0\). Conversely, if some \(\lambda_k\le 0\), take \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{q}_k\), the \(k\)-th column of \(Q\), so that \(\mathbf{y}=Q^\top\mathbf{q}_k=\mathbf{e}_k\) and \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\lambda_k\le 0\) with \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\), contradicting positive definiteness.

Claim 2. If every \(\lambda_i\ge 0\) then \(\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2\ge 0\) for all \(\mathbf{y}\). Conversely, if some \(\lambda_k<0\) then \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{q}_k\) gives \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\lambda_k<0\), contradicting positive semidefiniteness.

Claim 3. By Equation 3.3 the range of \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\) over \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\) is contained in \([\min_i\lambda_i,\ \max_i\lambda_i]\) and contains both endpoints (attained at the corresponding \(\mathbf{q}_i\)). It takes both signs if and only if \(\min_i\lambda_i<0<\max_i\lambda_i\), that is, if and only if \(A\) has eigenvalues of both signs.

The final statements follow from Lemma 3.5: \(\det(A)=\prod_i\lambda_i\) and \(\mathrm{tr}(A)=\sum_i\lambda_i\), with the stated signs read off the eigenvalues. \(\square\)

Why this matters. This theorem turns “is every contrast informative?” into “are all eigenvalues positive?”. A positive definite covariance matrix means every contrast, including the indirect comparison \(\mathbf{a}=(1,-1)^\top\), has strictly positive variance, and a positive definite Gram matrix is exactly what makes the regression of Chapter 6 have a unique, stable solution. The borderline case is the informative one: a zero eigenvalue (positive semidefinite but not definite) is the algebraic fingerprint of a real data problem, namely a covariate direction carrying no independent information, a collinear design matrix, or a linear combination of estimates with no variation at all.

Remark. The same change of variables proves a fact we use repeatedly: a symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive semidefinite if and only if \(A=B^\top B\) for some matrix \(B\), namely \(B=\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top\) when \(A\succeq 0\), while conversely \(\mathbf{x}^\top B^\top B\mathbf{x}=\|B\mathbf{x}\|^2\ge 0\) always. The Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) of any matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) is therefore positive semidefinite, and it is positive definite exactly when \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank, since then \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) forces \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\). This is the algebraic reason the normal equations of Chapter 6 have a unique solution. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The eigenvalue test requires diagonalizing \(A\), which means finding all its eigenvalues. A second, purely arithmetic, test reads positive definiteness off determinants of small submatrices instead. This is worth having because computing eigenvalues is hard (it is, in general, root-finding), whereas computing determinants of small top-left blocks is easy and exact. First we name those submatrices.

Definition 3.8 (Principal minors and leading principal minors) For \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and an index set \(\emptyset\ne S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\), the principal minor indexed by \(S\) is the determinant of the submatrix of \(A\) keeping only the rows and columns in \(S\). The leading principal minor of order \(k\) is the special case \(S=\{1,\dots,k\}\), namely \(\Delta_k=\det(A_k)\) where \(A_k\) is the top-left \(k\times k\) submatrix.

Reader translation. A leading principal minor just chops \(A\) down to its top-left corner of a given size and takes the determinant. “Leading” means the corner always starts at position \((1,1)\); a general “principal” minor may use any subset of the coordinates, not just the first \(k\). The distinction matters below: the positive definite test needs only the leading minors, but the positive semidefinite test needs all principal minors.

We prove the criterion in full. Plan of proof: the forward direction is a one-line restriction argument; for the converse we induct on \(n\), get \(A_{n-1}\succ 0\) from the inductive hypothesis, and rule out a negative eigenvalue by a dimension-count contradiction.

Theorem 3.4 (Sylvester’s criterion (leading principal minors)) For a symmetric \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\), let \(A_k\) denote the top-left \(k\times k\) submatrix and \(\Delta_k=\det(A_k)\) its leading principal minor, \(k=1,\dots,n\). Then \[ A\succ 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Delta_k>0 \text{ for every } k=1,\dots,n . \]

Proof. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose \(A\succ 0\). Fix \(k\) and let \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^k\) be nonzero. Pad it with zeros to \(\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{z}^\top,\mathbf{0}^\top)^\top\in\mathbb{R}^n\); then \(\mathbf{x}\ne \mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}^\top A_k\mathbf{z}>0\). Hence \(A_k\succ 0\), so by Theorem 3.3 all eigenvalues of \(A_k\) are positive and \(\Delta_k=\det(A_k)=\prod(\text{its eigenvalues})>0\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) We induct on \(n\). For \(n=1\), \(\Delta_1=a_{11}>0\) means \(A=(a_{11})\succ 0\). Assume the implication for symmetric matrices of size \(n-1\), and suppose all leading principal minors of the \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) are positive. The leading principal minors of \(A_{n-1}\) are exactly \(\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{n-1}\), all positive, so by the inductive hypothesis \(A_{n-1}\succ 0\). It remains to upgrade this to \(A\succ 0\) using \(\det(A)=\Delta_n>0\).

Suppose, for contradiction, that \(A\) is not positive definite. By Theorem 3.2, \(A\) has an orthonormal eigenbasis \(\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\) with real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\le\cdots\le \lambda_n\). Since \(\det(A)=\prod_i\lambda_i=\Delta_n>0\), no eigenvalue is zero. Since \(A\not\succ 0\), Theorem 3.3 gives some \(\lambda_i\le 0\), hence \(\lambda_1<0\). A product of nonzero reals is positive only with an even number of negative factors, so there are at least two negative eigenvalues; thus \(\lambda_2<0\) as well. Let \[ U=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{q}_1,\mathbf{q}_2\},\qquad V=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n:x_n=0\}. \] The choice of \(V\) is not special to the last coordinate: we only need some \((n-1)\)-dimensional subspace on which \(A\) is positive definite, and the slice \(x_n=0\) is one because restricting \(A\) to it gives \(A_{n-1}\succ 0\). Any other such subspace would serve. On \(U\) the form is negative and on \(V\) it is positive, and a dimension count will force these two subspaces to share a nonzero vector, the contradiction. For nonzero \(\mathbf{x}=c_1\mathbf{q}_1+c_2\mathbf{q}_2\in U\), orthonormality gives \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\lambda_1 c_1^2+\lambda_2 c_2^2<0\). For nonzero \(\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{z}^\top,0)^\top\in V\), we have \(\mathbf{z}\ne\mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}^\top A_{n-1}\mathbf{z}>0\) because \(A_{n-1}\succ 0\). Now \(\dim U=2\) and \(\dim V=n-1\), so by the dimension formula for subspaces (Theorem 1.3), \[ \dim(U\cap V)\ge \dim U+\dim V-n = 2+(n-1)-n = 1 . \] Pick a nonzero \(\mathbf{x}\in U\cap V\). Then \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}<0\) because \(\mathbf{x}\in U\), and \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}>0\) because \(\mathbf{x}\in V\), a contradiction. Therefore \(A\succ 0\), completing the induction. \(\square\)

Why this matters. This is the test you actually run on a small matrix by hand or quickly in code: to check that an estimated covariance matrix, a Hessian, or a Fisher information matrix is positive definite, compute a few determinants of top-left blocks rather than all the eigenvalues. It is a supporting result, not a conceptual one; its value is computational convenience.

Remark. Sylvester’s criterion is for positive definiteness only. The analogous statement for positive semidefiniteness requires all principal minors to be nonnegative, not merely the leading ones; the leading minors alone do not suffice, as the matrix \(\mathrm{diag}(0,-1)\) shows, whose leading minors are \(0\) and \(0\) yet which is not positive semidefinite. We use the eigenvalue characterization (Theorem 3.3) whenever semidefiniteness is at issue. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We can now construct the matrix square root, the function \(f(t)=\sqrt t\) from the remark above, and prove that it is unique among symmetric positive semidefinite roots. Why would a statistician want the square root of a matrix? Because \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) is the tool that whitens correlated data: applying \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\) to a random vector with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) turns its covariance into the identity, removing all correlation and unequal scaling. That same root defines the Mahalanobis distance used by the matching methods of Part V. We construct it here and cash out both uses in Section 3.10.

Proposition 3.2 (Symmetric positive semidefinite square root) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric positive semidefinite. Then there is a unique symmetric positive semidefinite matrix \(A^{1/2}\) with \((A^{1/2})^2=A\), namely \(A^{1/2}=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_n})\,Q^\top\) for any spectral decomposition \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\). If \(A\succ 0\) then \(A^{1/2}\succ 0\) and is invertible, and we write \(A^{-1/2}=(A^{1/2})^{-1}\).

Proof. Existence. By Theorem 3.3, \(A\succeq 0\) gives \(\lambda_i\ge 0\), so \(\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) is real. Put \(S=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_i})\,Q^\top\). It is symmetric, its eigenvalues \(\sqrt{\lambda_i}\ge 0\) make it positive semidefinite, and \[ S^2=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_i})\,Q^\top Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_i})\,Q^\top =Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_i)\,Q^\top=A . \]

Uniqueness. Let \(B\) be symmetric positive semidefinite with \(B^2=A\). By Theorem 3.2 write \(B=\sum_j\mu_j\,\mathbf{p}_j\mathbf{p}_j^\top\) with \(\mu_j\ge 0\) and \(\{\mathbf{p}_j\}\) an orthonormal basis. Orthonormality gives \(B^2=\sum_j\mu_j^2\,\mathbf{p}_j\mathbf{p}_j^\top\), so \(A=B^2\) has each \(\mathbf{p}_j\) as an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\mu_j^2\). The matrix \(S=A^{1/2}\) constructed above acts on each eigenvector of \(A\) by multiplying by the nonnegative square root of the corresponding eigenvalue, since \(S\) restricts to \(\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathrm{Id}\) on each eigenspace \(E_\lambda(A)\). Hence \[ S\mathbf{p}_j=\sqrt{\mu_j^2}\,\mathbf{p}_j=\mu_j\mathbf{p}_j=B\mathbf{p}_j \] for every \(j\). Two matrices that agree on the basis \(\{\mathbf{p}_j\}\) are equal, so \(B=S=A^{1/2}\).

If \(A\succ 0\) then all \(\lambda_i>0\), so all \(\sqrt{\lambda_i}>0\), making \(A^{1/2}\) positive definite and invertible by Theorem 3.3. \(\square\)

Why this matters. A unique symmetric positive semidefinite square root is what makes whitening well defined: there is exactly one natural \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\), so the standardized vector \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\) and the Mahalanobis distance built from it are unambiguous. Standardization, the Mahalanobis distance, and the Gaussian-copula constructions of Part V all rest on this one proposition.

3.7 The Rayleigh quotient and the variational characterization of eigenvalues

The eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are not only roots of a polynomial; they are solutions of optimization problems. The object that does the work is the Rayleigh quotient, which for a covariance matrix answers a concrete question: “how much variance do I see if I look in this particular direction?”. The largest and smallest answers are the extreme eigenvalues, attained along the corresponding eigenvectors. This variational viewpoint is what makes eigenvalues stable under perturbation and is the entry point to principal component analysis and to the conditioning bounds used throughout statistics.

Definition 3.9 (Rayleigh quotient) For a symmetric \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\), the Rayleigh quotient is \[ R_A(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}}. \] It is invariant under nonzero rescaling, \(R_A(c\mathbf{x})=R_A(\mathbf{x})\) for \(c\ne 0\), so it is determined by the direction of \(\mathbf{x}\) and equals \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\) on the unit sphere \(\|\mathbf{x}\|=1\).

Reader translation. Dividing by \(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\) cancels the length of \(\mathbf{x}\), so the Rayleigh quotient depends only on the direction you point in. With \(A= \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) it is the variance per unit length in that direction. Sweeping the direction around, the largest value you can reach is the top eigenvalue and the smallest is the bottom eigenvalue: the most and least uncertain directions of the estimator.

Theorem 3.5 (Variational characterization of the extreme eigenvalues) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric with eigenvalues ordered \(\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\cdots \le\lambda_n\) and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\). Then for every \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\), \[ \lambda_1\le R_A(\mathbf{x})\le\lambda_n, \tag{3.4}\] and \[ \lambda_1=\min_{\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}}R_A(\mathbf{x})=R_A(\mathbf{q}_1),\qquad \lambda_n=\max_{\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}}R_A(\mathbf{x})=R_A(\mathbf{q}_n). \] The minimum is attained exactly on the eigenspace \(E_{\lambda_1}\), and the maximum exactly on \(E_{\lambda_n}\).

Proof. Expand \(\mathbf{x}\) in the orthonormal eigenbasis, \(\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\mathbf{q}_i\) with \(c_i=\mathbf{q}_i^\top\mathbf{x}\). Orthonormality gives \(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}=\sum_i c_i^2\) and, using \(A\mathbf{q}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{q}_i\), \[ \mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i c_i^2 . \] Hence \[ R_A(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i c_i^2}{\sum_i c_i^2}, \] a weighted average of the eigenvalues with weights \(w_i=c_i^2/\sum_j c_j^2\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Any weighted average lies between the smallest and largest values, giving Equation 3.4. Equality on the right, \(R_A(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_n\), forces all weight onto coordinates with \(\lambda_i=\lambda_n\), that is, \(c_i=0\) whenever \(\lambda_i<\lambda_n\), which says exactly \(\mathbf{x}\in E_{\lambda_n}\); taking \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{q}_n\) shows the value \(\lambda_n\) is attained. The minimum statement is the same argument with the inequality reversed. \(\square\)

A one-line numeric check on the running matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues are \(1\) and \(3\): pointing along \(\mathbf{e}_1=(1,0)^\top\) gives \(R_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{e}_1)=\frac{2}{1}=2\), strictly inside \([1,3]\) as Equation 3.4 demands; pointing along the contrast \((1,-1)^\top\) gives \(R_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{2}{2}=1=\lambda_1\), so the indirect-comparison direction is exactly the minimum-variance direction, and pointing along \((1,1)^\top\) gives \(\frac{6}{2}=3=\lambda_2\), the maximum.

Remark. Rayleigh and principal component analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) looks for the direction \(\mathbf{x}\) with \(\|\mathbf{x}\|=1\) that maximizes the variance \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{X})= \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{x}\) of the projected data. By Theorem 3.5 that maximizer is the top eigenvector \(\mathbf{q}_n\) of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), and the maximal variance is the top eigenvalue \(\lambda_n\). The first principal component is therefore an eigenvector, which is the link between this theorem and the one eigenvalue idea most health researchers have already met. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Why this matters. Equation 3.4 says the variance of any contrast lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, so the extreme eigenvalues bracket every possible indirect-comparison variance at once. The top eigenvalue and eigenvector are exactly the first principal component.

The extreme eigenvalues solve an unconstrained problem. The interior eigenvalues solve a min-max problem over subspaces, the Courant-Fischer theorem, which we also prove in full. It needs no calculus, only the spectral theorem and a dimension count. This is a supporting result; a first reader can take its statement on faith and skip the proof.

In words, the first formula below says: among all \(k\)-dimensional subspaces \(S\), look inside each one for the largest Rayleigh value it contains, then pick the \(S\) that makes that largest value as small as possible; the answer is the \(k\)-th eigenvalue \(\lambda_k\), achieved by the span of the bottom \(k\) eigenvectors. The reason to care about these interior eigenvalues is that they control the middle principal components and underlie the eigenvalue interlacing results used in dimension reduction and in the stability analysis of estimated covariance matrices.

Theorem 3.6 (Courant-Fischer min-max theorem) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\le\cdots\le\lambda_n\). For each \(k\in\{1,\dots,n\}\), \[ \lambda_k=\min_{\substack{S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\\ \dim S=k}}\ \max_{\substack{\mathbf{x}\in S\\ \mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}}}R_A(\mathbf{x}) =\max_{\substack{S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\\ \dim S=n-k+1}}\ \min_{\substack{\mathbf{x}\in S\\ \mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}}}R_A(\mathbf{x}), \tag{3.5}\] where \(S\) ranges over subspaces of the stated dimension.

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\) be orthonormal eigenvectors with \(A\mathbf{q}_i=\lambda_i \mathbf{q}_i\). We prove the first equality; the second follows by applying the first to \(-A\), whose eigenvalues are \(-\lambda_n\le\cdots\le-\lambda_1\).

Upper bound. Take the particular subspace \(S_0=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_k\}\), of dimension \(k\). Every \(\mathbf{x}\in S_0\) is \(\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\le k}c_i\mathbf{q}_i\), and as in Theorem 3.5, \[ R_A(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i c_i^2}{\sum_{i\le k}c_i^2}\le\lambda_k, \] because every \(\lambda_i\) with \(i\le k\) satisfies \(\lambda_i\le\lambda_k\). Hence \(\max_{\mathbf{0}\ne\mathbf{x}\in S_0}R_A(\mathbf{x})\le\lambda_k\), so the minimum over all \(k\)-dimensional \(S\) is at most \(\lambda_k\).

Lower bound. Let \(S\) be any \(k\)-dimensional subspace and let \(T=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{q}_k, \mathbf{q}_{k+1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\}\), of dimension \(n-k+1\). By the dimension formula (Theorem 1.3), \[ \dim(S\cap T)\ge\dim S+\dim T-n=k+(n-k+1)-n=1, \] so there is a nonzero \(\mathbf{x}\in S\cap T\). Since \(\mathbf{x}\in T\), \(\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\ge k}c_i \mathbf{q}_i\) and \[ R_A(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\sum_{i\ge k}\lambda_i c_i^2}{\sum_{i\ge k}c_i^2}\ge\lambda_k, \] because every \(\lambda_i\) with \(i\ge k\) satisfies \(\lambda_i\ge\lambda_k\). Therefore \(\max_{\mathbf{0}\ne\mathbf{x}\in S}R_A(\mathbf{x})\ge R_A(\mathbf{x})\ge\lambda_k\) for this particular \(\mathbf{x}\), and since \(S\) was arbitrary, the minimum over all \(k\)-dimensional \(S\) is at least \(\lambda_k\).

The two bounds coincide, giving the first equality in Equation 3.5. \(\square\)

Remark. The Rayleigh principle is the source of two facts used later; both can be deferred on a first reading. The operator \(2\)-norm \(\|A\|_2=\max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}\|A\mathbf{x}\|\) is the largest factor by which \(A\) can stretch any unit vector. First, for symmetric \(A\) this operator \(2\)-norm equals the largest eigenvalue in magnitude, \(\|A\|_2=\max_i|\lambda_i|\), while \(\max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=\lambda_n\); the two agree when \(A\succeq 0\). Second, Courant-Fischer yields Weyl’s monotonicity inequality, that adding a positive semidefinite perturbation can only raise eigenvalues, which underlies the stability of estimated covariance matrices. Both are pursued in the exercises. The bound Equation 3.4 is also a quantitative companion to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Theorem 2.1), specialized from the standard inner product to the quadratic-form geometry of \(A\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

3.8 The singular value decomposition

We now return to the situation that actually occurs in regression and evidence synthesis, where the data matrix is not square and symmetric. A design matrix has one row per patient or per study-arm summary and one column per covariate or model term, so it is rectangular, and the spectral theorem, which applies only to symmetric and hence square matrices, does not reach it. We need a structure theorem for an arbitrary \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\). The singular value decomposition supplies it, and it follows from the spectral theorem applied to the symmetric positive semidefinite matrix \(A^\top A\). Its singular values will tell us, in plain terms, whether the available data actually support the directions in covariate space that we are trying to estimate.

Lemma 3.8 (\(A^\top A\) is symmetric positive semidefinite) For any \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\), the matrix \(A^\top A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative. Moreover \(\mathcal{N}(A^\top A)= \mathcal{N}(A)\), hence \(\mathrm{rank}(A^\top A)=\mathrm{rank}(A)\).

Proof. Symmetry is \((A^\top A)^\top=A^\top A\). For any \(\mathbf{x}\), \(\mathbf{x}^\top(A^\top A)\mathbf{x}= (A\mathbf{x})^\top(A\mathbf{x})=\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2\ge 0\), so \(A^\top A\succeq 0\), and Theorem 3.3 gives nonnegative eigenvalues. If \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) then \(A^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\); conversely if \(A^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) then \(0=\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{x}=\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2\) forces \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\). Thus the null spaces coincide, and by the rank-nullity theorem (Theorem 1.3) the ranks agree. \(\square\)

Theorem 3.7 (Singular value decomposition) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) have rank \(r\). Then there exist orthogonal matrices \(U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\) and \(V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and a matrix \(\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) that is zero off the main diagonal, with diagonal entries \[ \sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r>0=\sigma_{r+1}=\cdots=\sigma_{\min(m,n)}, \] such that \[ A=U\Sigma V^\top . \tag{3.6}\] The numbers \(\sigma_i\), the singular values of \(A\), are uniquely determined by \(A\) as \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)}\) with the eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\) in decreasing order. The columns of \(V\) (the right singular vectors) are orthonormal eigenvectors of \(A^\top A\), and the columns of \(U\) (the left singular vectors) are orthonormal eigenvectors of \(AA^\top\).

Before the proof, here is the story the three matrices tell. Read \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\) right to left as a pipeline acting on an input vector. The rotation \(V^\top\) lines up the special input directions, the right singular vectors \(\mathbf{v}_j\), with the coordinate axes; \(\Sigma\) stretches the \(j\)-th axis by the factor \(\sigma_j\), or annihilates it when \(\sigma_j=0\); and the rotation \(U\) places the stretched axes along the output directions, the left singular vectors \(\mathbf{u}_j\). The net effect is that \(A\) sends each input direction \(\mathbf{v}_j\) to \(\sigma_j\mathbf{u}_j\): input axis in, stretched output axis out. The singular values are the stretch factors, and a small \(\sigma_j\) means \(A\) barely moves that input direction, the signature of a direction the data weakly support. The definition \(\mathbf{u}_j=\sigma_j^{-1}A\mathbf{v}_j\) in the proof is just this sentence written as an equation.

Proof. By Lemma 3.8 the matrix \(A^\top A\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, so by Theorem 3.2 there is an orthogonal \(V=[\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n]\) with \[ A^\top A=V\Lambda V^\top,\qquad \Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n),\quad \lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n\ge 0 . \] By Lemma 3.8, \(\mathrm{rank}(A^\top A)=\mathrm{rank}(A)=r\), and the rank of a diagonalized symmetric matrix is the number of nonzero eigenvalues, so exactly \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_r>0\) and \(\lambda_{r+1}=\cdots=\lambda_n=0\). Define \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\), so \(\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r >0\).

For \(i=1,\dots,r\) define \(\mathbf{u}_i=\sigma_i^{-1}A\mathbf{v}_i\in\mathbb{R}^m\). These are orthonormal: \[ \mathbf{u}_i^\top\mathbf{u}_j=\frac{1}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\mathbf{v}_i^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v}_j =\frac{1}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\mathbf{v}_i^\top(\lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j) =\frac{\lambda_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\,\delta_{ij}=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\,\delta_{ij} =\delta_{ij}, \] using \(A^\top A\mathbf{v}_j=\lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j\) and \(\mathbf{v}_i^\top\mathbf{v}_j=\delta_{ij}\). Extend \(\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r\) to an orthonormal basis \(\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_m\) of \(\mathbb{R}^m\) by Gram-Schmidt (Theorem 2.3), and set \(U=[\mathbf{u}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{u}_m]\), which is orthogonal. Let \(\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) have \(\Sigma_{ii}=\sigma_i\) for \(i\le r\) and all other entries zero.

We verify \(AV=U\Sigma\), which is equivalent to Equation 3.6 after multiplying on the right by \(V^\top\). The \(j\)-th column of \(AV\) is \(A\mathbf{v}_j\). For \(j\le r\), \(A\mathbf{v}_j=\sigma_j\mathbf{u}_j\) by the definition of \(\mathbf{u}_j\). For \(j>r\), \(\lambda_j=0\), so \(\|A\mathbf{v}_j\|^2=\mathbf{v}_j^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v}_j=\lambda_j=0\), giving \(A\mathbf{v}_j=\mathbf{0}\). The \(j\)-th column of \(U\Sigma\) is \(\sigma_j\mathbf{u}_j\) for \(j\le r\) and \(\mathbf{0}\) for \(j>r\). The columns match, so \(AV=U\Sigma\) and \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\).

Uniqueness of the singular values. From Equation 3.6, \(A^\top A=V\Sigma^\top U^\top U\Sigma V^\top= V(\Sigma^\top\Sigma)V^\top\), and \(\Sigma^\top\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_r^2,0,\dots,0)\). This exhibits the \(\sigma_i^2\) as the eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\), which depend only on \(A\); hence the \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)}\) are determined by \(A\). Finally \(AA^\top=U\Sigma V^\top V\Sigma^\top U^\top=U(\Sigma\Sigma^\top)U^\top\) shows the columns of \(U\) are orthonormal eigenvectors of \(AA^\top\), with the same nonzero eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\). \(\square\)

Remark. Geometry and the four subspaces. The decomposition Equation 3.6 says that, in the right orthonormal input basis \(\{\mathbf{v}_j\}\) and output basis \(\{\mathbf{u}_i\}\), \(A\) acts diagonally: it stretches by \(\sigma_i\) along paired axes and annihilates the rest. The image of the unit sphere under \(A\) is an ellipsoid with semi-axes \(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r\) along \(\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r\), so \(\sigma_1=\|A\|_2\) is the operator norm and \(\sigma_r\) measures how close \(A\) is to dropping rank. The singular vectors lay bare the four fundamental subspaces of Theorem 1.5: \(\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r\}\) is an orthonormal basis of the column space \(\mathcal{C}(A)\), \(\{\mathbf{u}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_m\}\) of the left null space \(\mathcal{N}(A^\top)\), \(\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_r\}\) of the row space \(\mathcal{C}(A^\top)\), and \(\{\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\}\) of the null space \(\mathcal{N}(A)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 3.10 (Condition number) For \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) of rank \(r\) with singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r>0\), the condition number (in the \(2\)-norm) is \[ \kappa(A)=\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_r}. \] For a full-rank square \(A\) this equals \(\|A\|_2\,\|A^{-1}\|_2\).

Reader translation. The condition number compares the largest stretch to the smallest. When it is close to \(1\), \(A\) treats all directions evenly and is easy to invert; when it is large, some input direction is barely stretched, so undoing \(A\) blows up tiny errors in that direction.

Why this matters. This is the fact a health researcher fitting a regression most needs. A design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) with two nearly collinear covariates has a tiny smallest singular value, hence a huge \(\kappa(\mathbf{X})\). Because \(\kappa(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})=\kappa(\mathbf{X})^2\), the Gram matrix is then nearly singular, and the variance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), which scales with \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\), is inflated along the ill-supported direction. Large condition number is the linear-algebra name for collinearity and variance inflation.

One more size measure, used in the exercises, fits naturally here. The Frobenius norm of \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) is \(\|A\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2}=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^2}\), the Euclidean length of \(A\) when its entries are read as one long vector; the second equality, which expresses it through the singular values, is proved in Exercise 3.13.

3.9 The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse

When \(A\) is square and invertible, \(A^{-1}\) solves \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) uniquely. When \(A\) is rectangular or rank-deficient, no inverse exists, yet we still want a canonical operator that “best undoes” \(A\). This is the everyday situation in evidence synthesis: the design has too few independent data directions to pin down every coefficient, the covariates are collinear, or several coefficient vectors fit the data equally well. The pseudoinverse is the principled replacement for the missing inverse in all three cases. The singular value decomposition delivers it by inverting only the nonzero stretching factors and leaving the unsupported directions alone. The formal four-equation characterization comes after the definition, but the practical message is exactly this paragraph.

Definition 3.11 (Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) have singular value decomposition \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\) as in Theorem 3.7. The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of \(A\) is \[ A^{+}=V\Sigma^{+}U^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}, \tag{3.7}\] where \(\Sigma^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\) is the matrix with \((\Sigma^{+})_{ii}=\sigma_i^{-1}\) for \(i=1,\dots,r\) and all other entries zero. Equivalently \(A^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i^{-1}\mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{u}_i^\top\).

Reader translation. The pseudoinverse runs the SVD pipeline backwards, but only on the directions that were genuinely stretched. It un-rotates with \(U^\top\), divides by \(\sigma_i\) on the \(r\) supported axes and does nothing on the rest (you cannot divide by the zero stretch of an unsupported direction), then un-rotates with \(V\). It inverts what can be inverted and quietly leaves the null directions alone.

The formula uses a particular singular value decomposition, of which there may be many. The next proposition shows the result does not depend on that choice, by characterizing \(A^{+}\) intrinsically through four equations.

Proposition 3.3 (The Penrose equations characterize \(A^{+}\)) The matrix \(X=A^{+}\) of Definition 3.11 satisfies the four Penrose equations \[ \text{(P1) } AXA=A,\quad \text{(P2) } XAX=X,\quad \text{(P3) } (AX)^\top=AX,\quad \text{(P4) } (XA)^\top=XA, \] and it is the only matrix that does so. In particular \(A^{+}\) is independent of the chosen singular value decomposition. If \(A\) is invertible then \(A^{+}=A^{-1}\).

Proof. Existence. With \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\) and \(X=V\Sigma^{+}U^\top\), orthogonality of \(U,V\) gives \[ AX=U\Sigma\Sigma^{+}U^\top,\qquad XA=V\Sigma^{+}\Sigma V^\top . \] Now \(\Sigma\Sigma^{+}=\mathrm{diag}(I_r,\mathbf{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\) and \(\Sigma^{+}\Sigma= \mathrm{diag}(I_r,\mathbf{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) are diagonal with \(0/1\) entries, hence symmetric, so \(AX\) and \(XA\) are symmetric, giving (P3) and (P4). Because \(\Sigma\Sigma^{+}\Sigma=\Sigma\) and \(\Sigma^{+}\Sigma\Sigma^{+}=\Sigma^{+}\) (a \(0/1\) diagonal scaling fixes the matched diagonal entries), we get \(AXA=U\Sigma\Sigma^{+}\Sigma V^\top=U\Sigma V^\top=A\), which is (P1), and \(XAX=V\Sigma^{+}\Sigma\Sigma^{+}U^\top=V\Sigma^{+}U^\top=X\), which is (P2).

Uniqueness. Suppose \(G\) and \(H\) each satisfy (P1) to (P4) for \(A\). We take the chains one short step at a time, writing the rule used on the right. First we show \(AG=AH\): \[ \begin{aligned} AG &= (AG)^\top && \text{(P3 for }G\text{)}\\ &= G^\top A^\top && \text{(transpose of a product)}\\ &= G^\top (AHA)^\top && \text{(P1 for }H\text{: }A=AHA)\\ &= G^\top A^\top H^\top A^\top && \text{(transpose of a product)}\\ &= (AG)^\top (AH)^\top && \text{(regroup }G^\top A^\top,\ H^\top A^\top)\\ &= (AG)(AH) && \text{(P3 for }G\text{ and }H)\\ &= (AGA)H = AH && \text{(P1 for }G). \end{aligned} \] Symmetrically, using (P4) and (P1), we show \(GA=HA\): \[ \begin{aligned} GA &= (GA)^\top && \text{(P4 for }G\text{)}\\ &= A^\top G^\top && \text{(transpose of a product)}\\ &= (AHA)^\top G^\top && \text{(P1 for }H\text{: }A=AHA)\\ &= A^\top H^\top A^\top G^\top && \text{(transpose of a product)}\\ &= (HA)^\top (GA)^\top && \text{(regroup }A^\top H^\top,\ A^\top G^\top)\\ &= (HA)(GA) && \text{(P4 for }H\text{ and }G)\\ &= H(AGA) = HA && \text{(P1 for }G). \end{aligned} \] Now combine with (P2): \[ \begin{aligned} G &= GAG && \text{(P2 for }G\text{)}\\ &= G(AG) = G(AH) && \text{(}AG=AH\text{ above)}\\ &= (GA)H = (HA)H && \text{(}GA=HA\text{ above)}\\ &= H(AH) = HAH = H && \text{(P2 for }H). \end{aligned} \] Hence the matrix satisfying the four equations is unique; since \(A^{+}\) satisfies them and they do not mention the decomposition, \(A^{+}\) is well defined. If \(A\) is invertible, \(X=A^{-1}\) satisfies all four equations (each reduces to a trivial identity), so by uniqueness \(A^{+}=A^{-1}\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. The four equations pin down \(A^{+}\) with no reference to any particular singular value decomposition, so “the pseudoinverse” is a single well-defined object rather than an artifact of a choice. And because it collapses to \(A^{-1}\) whenever the ordinary inverse exists, it is a genuine extension of the inverse, safe to use as a drop-in replacement throughout regression.

The pseudoinverse is intimately tied to orthogonal projection, which is where Chapter 2 re-enters.

Proposition 3.4 (Pseudoinverse and orthogonal projection) For \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\), the matrix \(AA^{+}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbb{R}^m\) onto the column space \(\mathcal{C}(A)\), and \(A^{+}A\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) onto the row space \(\mathcal{C}(A^\top)\). When \(A\) has full column rank \(r=n\), the Gram matrix \(A^\top A\) is invertible and \[ A^{+}=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top,\qquad AA^{+}=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top . \]

Proof. With \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\), write \(U_r=[\mathbf{u}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{u}_r]\) for the first \(r\) left singular vectors. From the proof of Proposition 3.3, \(AA^{+}=U\,\mathrm{diag}(I_r,\mathbf{0})\,U^\top= U_rU_r^\top\). The columns of \(U_r\) are orthonormal and, by the remark after Theorem 3.7, form a basis of \(\mathcal{C}(A)\). By the projection theorem (Theorem 2.5) and the orthonormal-basis formula for a projection matrix (Definition 2.5), \(U_rU_r^\top\) is exactly the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_r\}=\mathcal{C}(A)\). The same argument applied to \(A^{+}A=V_rV_r^\top\) identifies it with the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_r\}=\mathcal{C}(A^\top)\).

If \(r=n\), then \(A^\top A\succ 0\) by Lemma 3.8 and the remark after Theorem 3.3, hence invertible. The matrix \(M=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) satisfies the four Penrose equations: \(AM=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) is symmetric and \(MA=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top A=I_n\) is symmetric, giving (P3), (P4); \(AMA=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top A=A\) gives (P1); and \(MAM=I_n M=M\) gives (P2). By the uniqueness in Proposition 3.3, \(A^{+}=M=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\), and \(AA^{+}=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. Multiplying by \(A^{+}\) and then by \(A\) is geometry: \(AA^{+}\) projects onto the column space (the fitted values a model can produce), and \(A^{+}A\) projects onto the row space (the part of the coefficients the data can identify). This is the bridge from the abstract pseudoinverse to the familiar machinery of least squares.

The formula \(A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) is precisely the hat matrix of least squares, which Chapter 6 studies in depth; here it appears as a special case of the pseudoinverse. The pseudoinverse also solves the general least-squares problem, even when the solution is not unique, by selecting the solution of minimum norm.

Proposition 3.5 (Minimum-norm least-squares solution) For \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m\), the vector \(\mathbf{x}^\star=A^{+} \mathbf{b}\) minimizes \(\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|\) over all \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n\), and among all minimizers it is the unique one of smallest Euclidean norm.

Proof. Using the orthogonal \(U\), \(\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|=\|U^\top(A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})\|=\|\Sigma V^\top\mathbf{x}-U^\top\mathbf{b}\|\). Put \(\mathbf{z}=V^\top\mathbf{x}\) (a bijective change of variable preserving norm, since \(\|\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{z}\|\)) and \(\mathbf{c}=U^\top\mathbf{b}\). Then \[ \|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2=\sum_{i=1}^r(\sigma_i z_i-c_i)^2+\sum_{i=r+1}^m c_i^2 . \] The second sum is fixed. The first is minimized, to zero, exactly by \(z_i=c_i/\sigma_i\) for \(i\le r\); the coordinates \(z_{r+1},\dots,z_n\) are free and do not affect the residual. Among these minimizers, \(\|\mathbf{x}\|^2=\|\mathbf{z}\|^2=\sum_{i\le r}(c_i/\sigma_i)^2+\sum_{i>r}z_i^2\) is smallest exactly when the free coordinates are zero. That choice gives \(\mathbf{z}=\Sigma^{+}\mathbf{c}\), hence \(\mathbf{x}^\star=V\mathbf{z}=V\Sigma^{+}U^\top\mathbf{b}=A^{+}\mathbf{b}\), and it is unique. \(\square\)

Reader translation. “Minimum norm” is not arbitrary. When several coefficient vectors fit the data equally well, they differ only by directions the data cannot see (the null space of \(A\)). Among all those equally good fits, the pseudoinverse returns the one of smallest Euclidean length, which is the one that adds nothing in the unsupported directions. It is the honest answer: it refuses to invent coefficient values that the data do not constrain.

3.10 Covariance matrices are symmetric and positive semidefinite

The objects of this chapter are not abstract: the covariance matrix of any random vector is symmetric positive semidefinite, which is why the entire spectral machinery transfers directly to statistics.

Remark. Bridge to Chapter 4. Let \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) be a random vector with finite second moments and mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]\). Its covariance matrix \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) (\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\big] \] is symmetric, because \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^\top=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) entrywise, and positive semidefinite, because for any fixed \(\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^n\), \[ \mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{a} =\mathbb{E}\!\big[\big(\mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\big)^2\big] =\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X})\ge 0 . \] This is proved as Theorem 4.9 in Chapter 4. Three consequences of the present chapter then apply verbatim. By Theorem 3.2, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) has an orthonormal eigenbasis; the eigenvectors are the principal axes of variation and the eigenvalues the variances along them, which is principal component analysis. By Proposition 3.2, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) has a symmetric positive semidefinite square root, and when \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\) the whitening transform \(\mathbf{Z}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\) has covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}=I_n\). The induced norm \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}}\) is the Mahalanobis distance that the matching methods of Part V use to compare covariate profiles across studies. In words, it is the standardized distance that accounts for correlation and unequal variances: two covariate profiles that differ by one raw unit count as far apart along directions in which the study has little variation, and close along directions in which it has a lot. Whitening makes this precise, since \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}\|_2\) is ordinary Euclidean distance after the data have been whitened. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

3.11 A fully worked example

We carry one small symmetric matrix through the whole theory, then treat a rank-deficient rectangular matrix to see the singular value decomposition and pseudoinverse in action. Every number below is checkable by hand.

A symmetric \(2\times 2\) matrix. Let \[ A=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}. \] This is exactly the running covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) of Example 3.1 for the two correlated log-odds-ratio estimates \((\hat y_{AB},\hat y_{AC})\); we call it \(A\) here to match the eigen-computation notation and read each result back as a statement about the indirect comparison.

Eigenvalues. The characteristic polynomial is \[ \chi_A(t)=\det\!\begin{pmatrix} t-2 & -1\\ -1 & t-2\end{pmatrix}=(t-2)^2-1=t^2-4t+3=(t-1)(t-3), \] so \(\lambda_1=1\) and \(\lambda_2=3\). As a check, \(\mathrm{tr}(A)=2+2=4=1+3\) and \(\det(A)=4-1=3=1\cdot 3\), matching Lemma 3.5.

Eigenvectors and the spectral decomposition. For \(\lambda=3\), \((A-3I)\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\ 1&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) gives \(\mathbf{v}\propto(1,1)\); for \(\lambda=1\), \((A-I)\mathbf{v}= \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) gives \(\mathbf{v}\propto(1,-1)\). The two are orthogonal, as Lemma 3.7 promises. Normalizing, \[ \mathbf{q}_{3}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{q}_{1}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \] and one verifies \(Q\Lambda Q^\top=A\) directly: \(Q\Lambda=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1 \end{pmatrix}\), and \((Q\Lambda)Q^\top=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&-1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}=A\).

Definiteness. Both eigenvalues are positive, so \(A\succ 0\) by Theorem 3.3. The leading principal minors are \(\Delta_1=2>0\) and \(\Delta_2=\det A=3>0\), confirming positive definiteness through Theorem 3.4 as well.

Rayleigh quotient. By Theorem 3.5, \(R_A\) ranges over \([\lambda_1,\lambda_2]=[1,3]\). The maximum is attained at \(\mathbf{q}_3\): with \(\mathbf{x}=(1,1)\), \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=(1,1)\binom{3}{3}=6\) and \(\|\mathbf{x}\|^2=2\), so \(R_A=3\). The minimum is attained at \(\mathbf{q}_1\): with \(\mathbf{x}= (1,-1)\), \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=(1,-1)\binom{1}{-1}=2\) and \(\|\mathbf{x}\|^2=2\), so \(R_A=1\). A generic direction sits strictly inside: \(\mathbf{x}=(1,0)\) gives \(R_A=2/1=2\in(1,3)\).

Square root. By Proposition 3.2, \(A^{1/2}=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt3,1)\,Q^\top\). Writing \(a=\tfrac{\sqrt3+1}{2}\) and \(b=\tfrac{\sqrt3-1}{2}\), \[ A^{1/2}=\begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ b & a\end{pmatrix}. \] We confirm \((A^{1/2})^2=A\) by hand: \(a^2+b^2=\tfrac{(\sqrt3+1)^2+(\sqrt3-1)^2}{4}=\tfrac{8}{4}=2\) and \(2ab=2\cdot\tfrac{(\sqrt3+1)(\sqrt3-1)}{4}=2\cdot\tfrac{2}{4}=1\), so \((A^{1/2})^2=\begin{pmatrix}a^2+b^2 & 2ab\\ 2ab & a^2+b^2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2 \end{pmatrix}=A\).

Indirect-comparison reading. Now read these numbers as facts about the two correlated estimates \((\hat y_{AB},\hat y_{AC})\) with covariance \(A=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). The two estimates have correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}/\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}}=1/2\). The variance of the Bucher indirect comparison \(\hat d_{BC}=\hat y_{AB}-\hat y_{AC}\) is the quadratic form along the contrast \(\mathbf{a}=(1,-1)^\top\), \[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat d_{BC})=\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a} =\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=2 . \] The two eigenpairs are the principal axes of uncertainty: the high-variance axis \(\mathbf{q}_3=(1,1)/ \sqrt2\) with variance \(\lambda=3\) is the direction in which both estimates move together, and the low-variance axis \(\mathbf{q}_1=(1,-1)/\sqrt2\) with variance \(\lambda=1\) is the contrast direction. The indirect comparison therefore lies on the better-determined axis of the pair. The condition number is \(\kappa(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\sigma_1/\sigma_2=3/1=3\), comfortably small, so this two-estimate problem is well conditioned. For the Mahalanobis distance and whitening, invert and take the square root through the spectrum: \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix} =\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}=Q\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3},1\big)\,Q^\top , \] so the whitened vector \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\hat{\mathbf y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\) has covariance \(I_2\): whitening rescales the variance-\(3\) axis and the variance-\(1\) axis to equal variance \(1\), removing the correlation. This single matrix has now illustrated eigenvalues, the spectral decomposition, definiteness, the Rayleigh bounds, the square root, contrast variance, conditioning, and the Mahalanobis metric, each as a concrete statement about an indirect comparison.

A rank-deficient matrix. Let \[ C=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 4\end{pmatrix}, \] whose rows are proportional, so \(\mathrm{rank}(C)=1\).

Singular values and vectors. Compute \(C^\top C=\begin{pmatrix}5&10\\10&20\end{pmatrix}\), with \(\mathrm{tr}=25\) and \(\det=100-100=0\), hence eigenvalues \(\lambda=25\) and \(\lambda=0\). The single nonzero singular value is \(\sigma_1=\sqrt{25}=5\). The right singular vector solves \((C^\top C-25I)\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}-20&10\\10&-5\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\), giving \(\mathbf{v}_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(1,2)\). The left singular vector is \(\mathbf{u}_1=\sigma_1^{-1}C \mathbf{v}_1=\tfrac{1}{5}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\binom{1+4}{2+8}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\binom{1}{2}\).

Pseudoinverse. By Definition 3.11, \(C^{+}=\sigma_1^{-1}\mathbf{v}_1\mathbf{u}_1^\top= \tfrac{1}{5}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\binom{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(1\ \ 2)=\tfrac{1}{25} \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac{1}{25}C\). We verify the Penrose identity (P1) of Proposition 3.3. Since \(C^2=\begin{pmatrix}5&10\\10&20\end{pmatrix}=5C\), we have \(C^3=5C^2= 25C\), so \[ CC^{+}C=C\cdot\tfrac{1}{25}C\cdot C=\tfrac{1}{25}C^3=\tfrac{1}{25}(25C)=C, \] as required. The projection \(CC^{+}=\tfrac{1}{25}C^2=\tfrac{1}{25}(5C)=\tfrac15 C=\tfrac15 \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric and idempotent, and it is the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathcal{C}(C)=\mathrm{span}\{(1,2)\}\), in agreement with Proposition 3.4.

Conditioning reading. The matrix \(C\) has singular values \(\sigma_1=5\) and \(\sigma_2=0\), so its condition number is infinite: the two rows are exactly proportional, and the data carry no information at all along the second input direction \(\mathbf{v}_2=(2,-1)/\sqrt5\). This is perfect collinearity, the extreme case of Definition 3.10. A real design matrix with two nearly collinear covariates would have a small but nonzero \(\sigma_2\), hence a large finite condition number, and the variance of the corresponding coefficient would be inflated by roughly \(1/\sigma_2^2\). The pseudoinverse keeps the problem well posed by simply declining to invert the zero (or near-zero) direction.

3.12 Notes and references

The material of this chapter is classical linear algebra. The eigenvalue and diagonalization theory, the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices, positive definite matrices and Sylvester’s criterion, the singular value decomposition, and the pseudoinverse are all developed in Strang (2016), which is our standing reference for the field; the determinant facts and the existence and extension of bases that we cite without proof are established there. The fundamental theorem of algebra, used once to guarantee that the characteristic polynomial has a complex root, is the only genuinely analytic prerequisite and is standard.

The inductive proof of the spectral theorem given here, peeling off one real eigenvector and restricting to its \(A\)-invariant orthogonal complement, is the most self-contained route and avoids the machinery of Schur factorization. The variational results, Theorem 3.5 and the Courant-Fischer theorem Theorem 3.6, are proved by elementary weighted-average and dimension-count arguments rather than by Lagrange multipliers, keeping the chapter free of calculus. Sylvester’s criterion is proved by an eigenvalue and dimension-count argument; an alternative proof through the \(LDL^\top\) factorization, in which the pivots equal the ratios of successive leading minors, is outlined in Strang (2016).

These linear-algebra primitives have no separate counterpart in the companion ITC_Coq development, whose formalized theorems live at the causal and statistical layer rather than at the level of matrix factorizations; accordingly, none of the results here carries a machine-checked tag. The downstream uses foreshadowed in Section 3.10, namely the positive semidefiniteness of covariance matrices and the Mahalanobis distance, reappear in the probability chapter and in the distance-matching methods of Part V.

Exercise 3.10 (Computation: the Penrose equations for a rank-one matrix) (Computation.) Let \(C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\), the rank-one matrix of the worked example, whose pseudoinverse is \(C^{+}=\tfrac{1}{25}C\). Verify all four Penrose equations (P1) to (P4) of Proposition 3.3 directly, using \(C^2=5C\). Then confirm that \(CC^{+}\) is symmetric and idempotent, so it is an orthogonal projection, and identify the subspace it projects onto. This is the concrete warm-up for the abstract uniqueness argument and the starred exercises that follow.

Exercise 3.11 (Shared spectrum of \(A^\top A\) and \(AA^\top\) (starred)) \(\star\) (Proof.) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\). Prove that \(A^\top A\) and \(AA^\top\) have exactly the same nonzero eigenvalues, with the same multiplicities, and that \(\mathbf{v}\mapsto A\mathbf{v}\) maps the eigenspace of \(A^\top A\) for a nonzero \(\lambda\) isomorphically onto the eigenspace of \(AA^\top\) for \(\lambda\). Conclude that the nonzero singular values of \(A\) and of \(A^\top\) coincide, and that \(\|A\|_F^2:=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2=\sum_i\sigma_i^2=\mathrm{tr}(A^\top A)\).

Exercise 3.12 (Weyl’s inequality from the Rayleigh principle (starred)) \(\star\) (Proof.) Let \(A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric with eigenvalues written in increasing order, \(\lambda_1(\cdot)\le\cdots\le\lambda_n(\cdot)\). Using the Courant-Fischer theorem (Theorem 3.6), prove Weyl’s inequality \[ \lambda_k(A)+\lambda_1(B)\le\lambda_k(A+B)\le\lambda_k(A)+\lambda_n(B),\qquad k=1,\dots,n. \] Deduce that if \(B\succeq 0\) then \(\lambda_k(A+B)\ge\lambda_k(A)\) for every \(k\) (adding a positive semidefinite matrix cannot decrease any eigenvalue), and that \(|\lambda_k(A+B)-\lambda_k(A)|\le\|B\|_2\) for every \(k\).

Exercise 3.13 (Rank, Frobenius norm, and the best low-rank approximation) Let \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\) have singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r>0\). Prove that \(\mathrm{rank}(A)\) equals the number of nonzero singular values and that \(\|A\|_F^2=\sum_i\sigma_i^2\). Define \(A_k=\sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top\) for \(k<r\). Prove directly that \(\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2\), and explain in one sentence why \(A_k\) is a natural rank-\(k\) approximation of \(A\).

Exercise 3.14 (Covariance and correlation matrices) Let \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) have covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). Prove from the definition that \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, and that \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\) unless some nontrivial linear combination \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is almost surely constant. Show that the correlation matrix \(R\) with \(R_{ij}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}/\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ii} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{jj}}\) is also positive semidefinite, and conclude that every correlation satisfies \(|R_{ij}|\le 1\).
