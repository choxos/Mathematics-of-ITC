7  Generalized Linear Models

7.1 Introduction

The linear model of Chapter 6 assumes a response that is continuous, unbounded, and normally distributed around a mean that moves on the same scale as the data. Almost none of the outcomes that drive an indirect treatment comparison (ITC, Notation) satisfy these assumptions. A clinical trial reports how many of \(n\) randomized patients responded: the outcome is a count of successes out of a fixed total, bounded between \(0\) and \(n\), with a variance that shrinks to zero as the success probability approaches either endpoint. A safety analysis reports the number of adverse events in a fixed person-time of follow-up: a nonnegative integer whose variance grows with its mean. A survival analysis reports event times and censoring. For each of these the normal linear model is not merely inefficient; it is structurally wrong. Fitting a straight line to a probability produces fitted values outside \([0,1]\), and assuming constant variance contradicts the arithmetic of a binomial or a Poisson count.

Generalized linear models (GLMs) repair this in a single, unified stroke. They keep the central idea of the linear model, a linear predictor \(\eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) that carries all the covariate information, but they separate it cleanly from two other choices: the probability law of the response, and the scale on which the mean is linear. The probability law is taken from a single rich family, the exponential dispersion family, which contains the normal, Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, and gamma distributions as special cases and ties each one’s variance to its mean through a fixed variance function. The scale is fixed by a link function \(g\) that maps the mean \(\mu_i\) to the linear predictor, \(g(\mu_i) = \eta_i\). Choosing the normal law and the identity link \(g(\mu)=\mu\) returns exactly the linear model of Chapter 6. Choosing the Bernoulli law and the logit link returns logistic regression and the odds ratio. Choosing the Poisson law and the log link returns Poisson regression and the rate ratio.

This chapter matters for indirect comparison for three reasons, each of which is developed in a later part of the book and seeded here. First, network meta-analysis (Chapters 13 to 15) and multilevel network meta-regression (Chapters 20 to 23) write the linear predictor of every trial arm as a GLM and combine evidence on the link scale, almost always the log-odds or log-rate scale; the algebra of this chapter is the algebra those methods run. Second, the maximum likelihood machinery developed here, the score equations, Fisher scoring, and iteratively reweighted least squares, is the numerical engine underneath every fitted outcome model in the book, and its large-sample distribution theory (which we import from Chapter 5) is what turns a point estimate into a confidence interval. Third, and most consequential for what follows, the nonlinearity of the link creates a phenomenon with no analogue in the linear model: the non-collapsibility of the odds ratio and the hazard ratio. A treatment effect that is identical in every stratum of a covariate can differ from the effect computed on the marginal, population-averaged risks, even with no confounding whatsoever. This single fact forces the distinction between conditional and marginal estimands that organizes Chapters 11, 17, and 19, and we prove it here with an explicit two-stratum example whose every number the reader can check by hand.

The development is self-contained and proves everything. It rests on the probability of Chapter 4 (the exponential family members, the moment and cumulant generating functions of Definition 4.7 and Theorem 4.10, Jensen’s inequality Theorem 4.6), the inference of Chapter 5 (the score Definition 5.5, the Fisher information Definition 5.7, the information equality Theorem 5.4, and above all the asymptotic normality of the maximum likelihood estimator, Theorem 5.6), and the least-squares linear algebra of Chapter 6 (the normal equations Theorem 6.1 and the projection geometry of Definition 6.3). The two primary references are McCullagh and Nelder (1989), the canonical monograph on the subject, and Dias et al. (2018), which casts the entire ITC enterprise as a collection of linked GLMs.

Remark (A note on reused symbols). This chapter uses four symbols whose meaning the reader should hold distinct from a neighboring use elsewhere in the book. The natural parameter of the exponential family is written \(\theta\); it is a specific instance of the generic statistical parameter \(\boldsymbol\theta\) of Chapter 5, and it is not the subscripted outcome-scale mean \(\theta_{ijk}=g^{-1}(\eta_{ijk})\) that the network-model chapters use. The dispersion parameter is written \(\phi\); when the standard normal density is needed (only in the discussion of the probit link) it is written as the derivative \(\Phi'\) of the standard normal distribution function \(\Phi\), so the symbol \(\phi\) is never overloaded inside this chapter. The GLM mean is always written \(\mu_i=\mathbb{E}[Y_i]\). Finally the letter \(\nu\) does double duty: it is the reference measure in the definition of the exponential dispersion family (a single piece of bookkeeping that lets one formula cover both continuous and discrete responses; explained in plain terms in Section 7.3), and, separately, the shape parameter of the gamma distribution in Example 7.3. The two never appear in the same display, so no formula mixes them. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.2 GLM objects and scales: a first trial example

The single hardest thing about a first encounter with GLMs is not any one proof; it is keeping track of how many different scales a single number can live on. A trial reports a count, the model predicts a mean, the mathematics works internally with a natural parameter, the regression part lives on a linear predictor, and the effect a clinician reads off is a ratio. These are five different scales for what is, morally, one fitted quantity. Before any formal definitions we therefore introduce one concrete trial, label every object on it, and collect the symbols in a table. Every later section is an instruction for moving between the columns of that table, and we return to this example at each transition.

Why a single family. One could, in principle, build the binary-outcome model, the count model, and the survival model separately, each with its own likelihood, its own estimating equation, and its own software. The exponential dispersion family (EDF), defined in Section 7.3, is the device that makes this unnecessary. It is a template: a single algebraic form, Equation 7.1, into which the normal, Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, and gamma distributions all fit. The payoff is leverage. Because they share the template, one mean-variance theorem (Theorem 7.1), one score equation (Theorem 7.2), and one fitting algorithm (Theorem 7.3) serve all of them at once. Proving a fact about the template proves it simultaneously for logistic regression, Poisson regression, and ordinary linear regression. That is why the abstraction is worth the price of admission.

Example 7.1 (A running trial: one binary arm and one count arm) Consider a single arm of a clinical trial in which \(n=60\) patients are randomized and \(R=18\) of them respond. We will reuse these numbers throughout the chapter.

The binary (response) outcome. We record the proportion who responded, \(y=R/n=18/60=0.30\). The model’s job is to estimate the underlying response probability \(\mu=p\). The proportion does not live on the same scale as the probability’s regression: a probability is trapped in the interval \((0,1)\), whereas a regression \(\eta=\mathbf x^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) ranges over all of \(\mathbb R\). The link \(g(\mu)=\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\) (the logit) is the strictly increasing change of scale that carries the probability onto the whole real line, so that \(\eta=g(\mu)\) can be modeled linearly. If the arm receives treatment, coded by an indicator \(x=1\), its coefficient \(\beta_1\) is a log odds ratio and \(e^{\beta_1}\) is the odds ratio a meta-analysis would combine. The natural variability of the proportion is \(\mathrm{Var}(y)=p(1-p)/n\): largest near \(p=\tfrac12\), vanishing as \(p\) approaches \(0\) or \(1\).

The count (adverse-event) outcome. Suppose the same arm accrues \(7\) adverse events over \(t=50\) person-years. Here the response is the count \(y=7\), the model estimates the expected count \(\mu=\lambda\), and the natural variability is \(\mathrm{Var}(y)=\lambda\) (a Poisson count’s variance equals its mean). The link is the log, \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\), which keeps the fitted mean positive; the known exposure enters as an offset \(\log t\), and a treatment coefficient \(\beta_1\) is then a log rate ratio, with \(e^{\beta_1}\) the incidence rate ratio. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The objects just named, and the scale each one lives on, are collected below. This local map is the chapter in miniature; the formal definitions that follow simply make each row precise.

Symbol Name Role (what it does) In the binary trial arm
\(Y_i,\;y_i\) response the observed data for unit or group \(i\) proportion \(y=R/n=0.30\)
\(\mu_i=\mathbb E[Y_i]\) mean what the model predicts, on the data’s own scale response probability \(p\)
\(\theta\) natural parameter the EDF’s internal coordinate; tunes the distribution \(\log\frac{p}{1-p}\)
\(b(\theta)\) cumulant function the EDF normalizer; its derivatives give mean and variance \(\log(1+e^\theta)\)

Reading down the table is the same as touring the five scales: the data scale (\(y\)), the mean scale (\(\mu\), a probability or a rate), the natural-parameter scale (\(\theta\), the EDF’s internal bookkeeping), the linear-predictor or link scale (\(\eta=g(\mu)\), where the regression lives), and the ratio scale reached by exponentiating a coefficient (\(e^{\beta_1}\), an odds or rate ratio).

Remark (Why trial data arrive as a proportion with a weight). A health researcher usually pictures a trial arm as a two-by-two count: \(R\) responders and \(n-R\) nonresponders. A GLM consumes the same information in the slightly different shape “response \(y=R/n\) with prior weight \(w=n\).” The reason is the arithmetic of Example 7.1: each of the \(n\) patients is an independent \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) trial, so their average \(y=R/n\) has mean \(p\) and variance \(p(1-p)/n\). The denominator \(n\) is not data to be modeled; it is a known multiplier that says how much each proportion should count, and it is exactly the prior weight \(w_i\). Concretely, \(R=18\) of \(n=60\) gives \(y=0.30\), \(w=60\), mean \(\mu=p\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(y)=p(1-p)/60\). Trial-level ITC data almost always take this grouped form, one proportion-and-weight pair per arm, which is why we carry the weight \(w\) through every formula. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.3 The exponential dispersion family

The random component of a GLM is a member of the following family. The definition is stated once for both continuous and discrete responses, and the only price of that economy is a single symbol, the reference measure \(\nu\). Operationally \(\nu\) is nothing more exotic than a reminder of how to add up: for a continuous response (the normal, the gamma) “integrate against \(\nu\)” means ordinary integration \(\int f(y)\,dy\), and for a discrete response (the Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson) it means summation \(\sum_y f(y)\) over the possible values. A reader who has never met measure theory can mentally replace every \(\int(\cdot)\,\nu(dy)\) by “integrate if continuous, sum if discrete” and lose nothing; the unified notation exists only so that the mean-variance theorem and the score equation below need to be proved once rather than twice. With that understanding we fix \(\nu\) to be Lebesgue measure (ordinary \(dy\)) for continuous responses and counting measure on the integers (a sum) for discrete ones.

Definition 7.1 (Exponential dispersion family) A random variable \(Y\) taking values in a set \(\mathcal Y\subseteq\mathbb R\) belongs to the exponential dispersion family (EDF) with natural parameter \(\theta\), dispersion parameter \(\phi>0\), and known prior weight \(w>0\) if its density (or probability mass function) with respect to \(\nu\) has the form \[ f(y;\theta,\phi) \;=\; \exp\!\left\{\frac{y\,\theta - b(\theta)}{a(\phi)} \;+\; c(y,\phi)\right\}, \qquad a(\phi)=\frac{\phi}{w}, \tag{7.1}\] where:

  1. the cumulant function \(b:\Theta\to\mathbb R\) is twice continuously differentiable with \(b''(\theta)>0\) for every \(\theta\) in the interior of \(\Theta\), and does not depend on \(\phi\);

  2. the natural parameter space \(\Theta=\bigl\{\theta\in\mathbb R:\int_{\mathcal Y}\exp(y\theta/a(\phi)+c(y,\phi))\,\nu(dy)<\infty\bigr\}\) is a nonempty open interval;

  3. \(c(\cdot,\cdot)\) is a function not involving \(\theta\), chosen so that Equation 7.1 integrates to one.

We write \(Y\sim\mathrm{EDF}(\theta,\phi;w)\). When \(w=1\) we write \(\mathrm{EDF}(\theta,\phi)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. The formula Equation 7.1 looks forbidding, but it has only three moving parts and each plays a single role. The term \(y\theta\) couples the data to the parameter: it is the one place where the observation \(y\) multiplies the unknown \(\theta\), and it is what makes \(\theta\) learnable from \(y\). The term \(-b(\theta)\) normalizes: it is whatever is needed to make the total probability equal one, and the next theorem shows it secretly stores the mean and variance. The term \(c(y,\phi)\) collects the leftovers that depend on the data and the dispersion but not on \(\theta\); because it is free of \(\theta\) it will drop out of every score equation and never touch \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\). The dispersion \(\phi\) and the weight \(w\) enter only through \(a(\phi)=\phi/w\), an overall divisor of the exponent. Despite its name, the prior weight \(w\) is not a Bayesian prior: it is a known multiplicity, typically the binomial denominator \(n_i\) of a trial arm as in Example 7.1.

Condition (iii) can be made explicit. The density Equation 7.1 must integrate to \(1\) over \(\mathcal Y\); since the factor \(\exp\{-b(\theta)/a(\phi)\}\) is constant in \(y\) it pulls outside the integral, and clearing it to the other side shows that for every \(\theta\in\Theta\), \[ \int_{\mathcal Y}\exp\!\left\{\frac{y\,\theta}{a(\phi)}+c(y,\phi)\right\}\nu(dy) \;=\;\exp\!\left\{\frac{b(\theta)}{a(\phi)}\right\}. \tag{7.2}\] Equivalently, \(b(\theta)=a(\phi)\log\int_{\mathcal Y}\exp(y\theta/a(\phi)+c(y,\phi))\,\nu(dy)\); the defining requirement that a single cumulant function \(b\) serve all dispersions \(\phi\) is what the word “dispersion” in the name records, and it holds for every example we use. Identity Equation 7.2 is the engine of the next theorem.

The exponential dispersion family is genuinely a family of distributions, one for each pair \((\theta,\phi)\); varying \(\theta\) moves the mean, and varying \(\phi\) scales the variance without changing the mean, as we now prove.

Before the general theorem, it helps to watch the template Equation 7.1 absorb one distribution slowly, with every term named.

Example 7.2 (The EDF density for one Bernoulli response, term by term) Let \(Y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) (Definition 4.8), so \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) with \(P(Y=1)=p\). Its mass function \(p^y(1-p)^{1-y}\), rewritten through its logarithm, is \[ p^y(1-p)^{1-y}=\exp\Bigl\{\,y\underbrace{\log\tfrac{p}{1-p}}_{=\,\theta}\;+\;\underbrace{\log(1-p)}_{=\,-b(\theta)}\,\Bigr\}. \] Matching this to Equation 7.1 term by term gives the whole decomposition. The natural parameter is \(\theta=\log\frac{p}{1-p}\), the log odds, so \(p=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)\). The cumulant function is \(b(\theta)=-\log(1-p)=\log(1+e^\theta)\), using \(1-p=1/(1+e^\theta)\). The dispersion is \(\phi=1\) and the weight is \(w=1\), so \(a(\phi)=1\). The leftover term is \(c(y,\phi)=0\): nothing is left over, because a single Bernoulli mass function has no data-only factor to absorb. Differentiating \(b\) already previews the theorem below: \(b'(\theta)=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)=p=\mu\) returns the mean, and \(b''(\theta)=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)^2=p(1-p)\) returns the variance function \(V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)\). The single binary patient of Example 7.1 is exactly this object, with \(p\) the response probability. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.3.1 Mean and variance from the cumulant function

The single most useful fact about the EDF is that its mean and variance are read off from the derivatives of \(b\). Two objects with nearly identical names appear here and must be kept apart: the cumulant function \(b(\theta)\) is the fixed piece of the density Equation 7.1, a property of the distribution, whereas the cumulant generating function \(K_Y(t)=\log\mathbb E[e^{tY}]\) is a tool we build from \(Y\) to extract its moments. They share the word “cumulant” because, as the proof shows, the first controls the second.

Strategy of the proof. The mean and variance are the first two cumulants of \(Y\), and cumulants are by definition the derivatives of \(K_Y\) at \(t=0\), so \(\mathbb E[Y]=K_Y'(0)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=K_Y''(0)\). The proof is therefore a two-step plan: first compute \(K_Y(t)\) in closed form, where it turns out to equal \([b(\theta+t\,a(\phi))-b(\theta)]/a(\phi)\), then differentiate it twice at \(0\). A reader who grasps this plan can skip the algebra and still trust the conclusion.

Theorem 7.1 (Mean and variance of an EDF response) Let \(Y\sim\mathrm{EDF}(\theta,\phi;w)\) with \(\theta\) in the interior of \(\Theta\), and write \(a(\phi)=\phi/w\). Then the moment generating function of \(Y\) is finite in a neighborhood of the origin and \[ \mathbb E[Y]=b'(\theta)=:\mu,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y)=a(\phi)\,b''(\theta)=\frac{\phi}{w}\,b''(\theta). \tag{7.3}\] Moreover the map \(\theta\mapsto\mu=b'(\theta)\) is a strictly increasing bijection from the interior of \(\Theta\) onto an open interval \(\mathcal M\subseteq\mathbb R\), the mean space; its inverse is written \(\theta(\mu)\), and the variance function \[ V(\mu):=b''\bigl(\theta(\mu)\bigr)>0 \tag{7.4}\] expresses the variance as \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=a(\phi)\,V(\mu)\), a known function of the mean times the dispersion.

Why this matters. Equation Equation 7.3 is the lever the rest of the chapter pulls. Once the cumulant function \(b\) of a distribution is known, its mean and its whole variance structure follow by differentiation alone, with no further integration. The same cumulant-function trick reappears in the score equation (Theorem 7.2), the Fisher information (Proposition 7.2), and the large-sample theory (Corollary 7.2), so the work done here is repaid three more times. For an indirect comparison this is what puts every trial outcome, binary, count, or continuous, on a common footing with a known mean and variance, the precondition for combining them in a network meta-analysis.

Proof. Fix \(\theta\) interior to \(\Theta\). Because \(\Theta\) is open, there is \(\epsilon>0\) with \([\theta-\epsilon a(\phi),\,\theta+\epsilon a(\phi)]\subseteq\Theta\). The substitution that drives the computation is \(\theta'=\theta+t\,a(\phi)\): it is chosen precisely so that the extra factor \(e^{ty}\) from the moment generating function merges into the natural-parameter term of the density, because in the exponent \[ ty+\frac{y\theta}{a(\phi)}=\frac{y\bigl(\theta+t\,a(\phi)\bigr)}{a(\phi)}=\frac{y\theta'}{a(\phi)}. \] For \(|t|<\epsilon\) put \(\theta'=\theta+t\,a(\phi)\in\Theta\) and compute the moment generating function \(M_Y(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tY}]\) (Definition 4.7) directly from Equation 7.1: \[ M_Y(t)=\int_{\mathcal Y}e^{ty}\exp\!\left\{\frac{y\theta-b(\theta)}{a(\phi)}+c(y,\phi)\right\}\nu(dy) =e^{-b(\theta)/a(\phi)}\int_{\mathcal Y}\exp\!\left\{\frac{y\theta'}{a(\phi)}+c(y,\phi)\right\}\nu(dy). \] The integral on the right is finite because \(\theta'\in\Theta\), and by the normalization identity Equation 7.2 applied at \(\theta'\) it equals \(\exp\{b(\theta')/a(\phi)\}\). Hence, for \(|t|<\epsilon\), \[ M_Y(t)=\exp\!\left\{\frac{b(\theta+t\,a(\phi))-b(\theta)}{a(\phi)}\right\}<\infty. \] The moment generating function is therefore finite on a neighborhood of \(0\), and the cumulant generating function \(K_Y(t)=\log M_Y(t)\) is \[ K_Y(t)=\frac{b(\theta+t\,a(\phi))-b(\theta)}{a(\phi)}. \tag{7.5}\] Since \(b\) is twice continuously differentiable on the open set \(\Theta\), so is \(K_Y\) near \(0\), with \[ K_Y'(t)=b'(\theta+t\,a(\phi)),\qquad K_Y''(t)=a(\phi)\,b''(\theta+t\,a(\phi)). \] Now relate the derivatives of \(K_Y\) at \(0\) to the mean and variance. Because \(M_Y\) is finite near \(0\) it is infinitely differentiable there with \(M_Y(0)=1\), \(M_Y'(0)=\mathbb E[Y]\), and \(M_Y''(0)=\mathbb E[Y^2]\) (Theorem 4.10). From \(K_Y=\log M_Y\), \[ K_Y'=\frac{M_Y'}{M_Y},\qquad K_Y''=\frac{M_Y''M_Y-(M_Y')^2}{M_Y^2}, \] so at \(t=0\), using \(M_Y(0)=1\), \[ K_Y'(0)=M_Y'(0)=\mathbb E[Y],\qquad K_Y''(0)=M_Y''(0)-\bigl(M_Y'(0)\bigr)^2=\mathbb E[Y^2]-\bigl(\mathbb E[Y]\bigr)^2=\mathrm{Var}(Y). \] Evaluating the two displayed derivatives of Equation 7.5 at \(t=0\) gives \(\mathbb E[Y]=b'(\theta)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=a(\phi)b''(\theta)\), which is Equation 7.3.

For the second claim, \(b''>0\) on the interior of \(\Theta\) makes \(b'\) strictly increasing there, hence injective, and continuous. Its image \(\mathcal M\) is an interval, because a continuous function carries an interval to an interval (the intermediate value theorem: every value lying between two attained values is itself attained). That interval is moreover open: a strictly increasing function on an open interval attains neither a largest nor a smallest value, since any candidate extreme point has a still-smaller or still-larger neighbor inside the domain, so \(\mathcal M\) contains none of its endpoints. Thus \(b':\mathrm{int}\,\Theta\to\mathcal M\) is a continuous strictly increasing bijection with continuous inverse \(\theta(\cdot)\). Define \(V(\mu)=b''(\theta(\mu))\); it is positive because \(b''>0\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=a(\phi)b''(\theta)=a(\phi)V(\mu)\) by substituting \(\theta=\theta(\mu)\). \(\square\)

Remark (Why the variance is tied to the mean). Equation Equation 7.3 is the structural heart of the GLM. In the normal model the variance \(\phi\) is free of the mean, but in every other member of the family the variance is forced to be \(a(\phi)V(\mu)\) with \(V\) fixed by the distribution. For the Bernoulli law \(V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)\), so the variance must vanish at the endpoints; for the Poisson law \(V(\mu)=\mu\), so the variance must equal the mean. For a trial this is not bookkeeping: the uncertainty in a response probability near \(\tfrac12\) genuinely exceeds that near \(0\) or \(1\), and the scatter of an event count genuinely grows with its expected size, so tying the variance to the mean is what lets a GLM report honest standard errors. A GLM does not get to choose the mean-variance relationship independently of the response distribution, and this is exactly the discipline that a normal linear model on a count or a proportion lacks. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.3.2 The standard members

We verify that the distributions named in the introduction are EDF members and record their cumulant function, dispersion, and variance function. Each verification is a short algebraic rearrangement of the density into the canonical form Equation 7.1.

Example 7.3 (Five exponential dispersion families) Normal. For \(Y\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) (Notation), expand the density: \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-(y-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} =\exp\!\left\{\frac{y\mu-\mu^2/2}{\sigma^2}-\frac{y^2}{2\sigma^2}-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\right\}. \] This is Equation 7.1 with \(\theta=\mu\), \(b(\theta)=\theta^2/2\), \(\phi=\sigma^2\), \(w=1\), and \(c(y,\phi)=-y^2/(2\phi)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\phi)\). Then \(b'(\theta)=\theta=\mu\) and \(b''(\theta)=1\), so \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\phi=\sigma^2\) and \(V(\mu)=1\): the variance is constant, as it must be.

Bernoulli. For \(Y\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) (Definition 4.8), \(p^y(1-p)^{1-y}=\exp\{y\log\frac{p}{1-p}+\log(1-p)\}\). Set \(\theta=\log\frac{p}{1-p}\), so \(p=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)\) and \(\log(1-p)=-\log(1+e^\theta)\). Thus \(b(\theta)=\log(1+e^\theta)\), \(\phi=1\), \(w=1\), \(c\equiv0\). Here \(b'(\theta)=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)=p=\mu\) and \(b''(\theta)=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)^2=p(1-p)\), so \(V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mu(1-\mu)\).

Binomial proportion. For a grouped binomial response \(Y=R/n\) with \(R\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\) (Definition 4.9), the proportion has the same \(\theta=\log\frac{p}{1-p}\) and \(b(\theta)=\log(1+e^\theta)\) as the Bernoulli, but with prior weight \(w=n\) and \(\phi=1\), so \(a(\phi)=1/n\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mu(1-\mu)/n\). The grouped form is what trial-level ITC data take.

Poisson. For \(Y\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\) (Definition 4.10), \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^y/y!=\exp\{y\log\lambda-\lambda-\log y!\}\). Set \(\theta=\log\lambda\), so \(b(\theta)=e^\theta=\lambda\), \(\phi=1\), \(w=1\), \(c(y,\phi)=-\log y!\). Then \(b'(\theta)=e^\theta=\lambda=\mu\) and \(b''(\theta)=e^\theta=\lambda\), so \(V(\mu)=\mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mu\): variance equals mean.

Gamma. For \(Y\) gamma with mean \(\mu\) and shape \(\nu\), density \(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}(\nu/\mu)^\nu y^{\nu-1}e^{-\nu y/\mu}\), write the exponent as \(\frac{y(-1/\mu)+\log\mu}{1/\nu}+(\nu-1)\log y+\nu\log\nu-\log\Gamma(\nu)\). Set \(\theta=-1/\mu<0\), so \(b(\theta)=-\log(-\theta)=\log\mu\), \(\phi=1/\nu\), \(w=1\). Then \(b'(\theta)=-1/\theta=\mu\) and \(b''(\theta)=1/\theta^2=\mu^2\), giving \(V(\mu)=\mu^2\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mu^2/\nu\): constant coefficient of variation. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The five members are summarized below; the canonical link in the last column is defined in Section 7.4.

Bernoulli/Binomial \(\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\) \(\log(1+e^\theta)\) \(1\) \(\mu(1-\mu)\) logit \(\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\)

The theorem introduced the mean space \(\mathcal M\), which deserves a plain reading: it is simply the set of values the mean is allowed to take for the distribution at hand. For the Bernoulli and binomial it is the open interval \((0,1)\), the range of a probability; for the Poisson and gamma it is \((0,\infty)\), the range of a positive rate or mean; for the normal it is all of \(\mathbb R\). The link function, defined next, is a change of scale that acts exactly on \(\mathcal M\).

7.4 Link functions and the generalized linear model

The EDF supplies the random component. The systematic component is the familiar linear predictor \(\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\). The link function is the bridge between them.

Definition 7.2 (Link function) A link function is a function \(g:\mathcal M\to\mathbb R\) defined on the mean space \(\mathcal M\) of an EDF that is twice continuously differentiable and strictly monotone, hence a bijection from \(\mathcal M\) onto an open interval \(g(\mathcal M)\subseteq\mathbb R\). Its inverse \(g^{-1}\), called the mean function or response function, recovers the mean from the linear predictor. A generalized linear model relates the mean of the \(i\)-th response to its linear predictor by \[ g(\mu_i)=\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mu_i=g^{-1}(\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta). \tag{7.6}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. A link is just a relabeling of the mean onto the regression scale. It takes a mean \(\mu\), which may be confined to a probability in \((0,1)\) or a positive rate in \((0,\infty)\), and reports a number \(\eta=g(\mu)\) that is free to be any real, so the linear predictor \(\mathbf x^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) can equal it without running into a boundary. The logit turns a probability into a log odds; the log turns a rate into a log rate. Inverting the link, \(\mu=g^{-1}(\eta)\), turns a fitted linear predictor back into a legal mean.

Strict monotonicity is exactly what guarantees that \(g\) can be inverted, so that every value of the linear predictor names exactly one mean; and because \(g\) is strictly monotone and continuous on the open interval \(\mathcal M\), its image \(g(\mathcal M)\) is again an open interval (the same argument that made \(\mathcal M\) open above), which is what lets the unrestricted linear predictor range over it freely. The twice-differentiability requirement is the one clause a beginner does not need yet: it is invisible until the estimation theory of Section 7.5, where the fitting algorithm differentiates the link twice; until then it can be read as “smooth enough.” Among all links for a given EDF, one is distinguished.

Definition 7.3 (Canonical link) The canonical link (or natural link) of an EDF is \(g_{\mathrm{c}}=(b')^{-1}=\theta(\cdot)\), the inverse of the mean map of Theorem 7.1. It is a valid link function: \(b'\) is a strictly increasing \(C^1\) bijection from \(\mathrm{int}\,\Theta\) onto \(\mathcal M\) by Theorem 7.1, so \(\theta(\cdot)=g_{\mathrm c}\) is a strictly increasing \(C^1\) bijection from \(\mathcal M\) onto \(\mathrm{int}\,\Theta\), and it is \(C^2\) because \(b'\) is \(C^2\) with nonvanishing derivative \(b''>0\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. Of the many links one could attach to a distribution, the canonical link is the one that coincides with the distribution’s own natural parameter \(\theta\). Choosing it is a convenience worth understanding now for one reason and trusting for another: it makes the estimation algorithm simplest (proved in Section 7.5), and for the families health researchers use it also yields the interpretable effect measures, the logit giving the odds ratio and the log giving the rate ratio. It is a sensible default, not an obligation.

Proposition 7.1 (The canonical link identifies the linear predictor with the natural parameter) A GLM uses its canonical link if and only if the natural parameter equals the linear predictor for every observation: \(\theta_i=\eta_i\).

Proof. With the canonical link, \(\eta_i=g_{\mathrm c}(\mu_i)=\theta(\mu_i)=\theta(b'(\theta_i))=\theta_i\), the last step because \(\theta(\cdot)\) inverts \(b'\). Conversely, if \(\theta_i=\eta_i=g(\mu_i)\) for the link in use, then \(g(\mu_i)=\theta_i=\theta(\mu_i)\) for all attainable \(\mu_i\), so \(g=\theta(\cdot)=g_{\mathrm c}\) on \(\mathcal M\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. The identity \(\theta_i=\eta_i\) is precisely why the canonical link makes the mathematics clean: the natural parameter, the EDF’s internal coordinate, and the linear predictor, the regression, become one and the same object, collapsing the chain of scales in Section 7.2 by a link. This single coincidence produces the simple score equation of Corollary 7.1 and the global concavity of Lemma 7.1, and it is why logistic and Poisson regression, both canonical, are the workhorses on which network meta-analysis is built.

Reading the canonical-link column of the table in Section 7.3 confirms the familiar choices: the identity link for the normal, the logit link \(g(\mu)=\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\) for the Bernoulli and binomial, the log link \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\) for the Poisson, and the reciprocal link \(-1/\mu\) for the gamma. The canonical link is not mandatory, and Section 7.4 below introduces the probit and complementary log-log links for binary data, but it confers the analytic and computational simplifications proved in Section 7.5.

We can now state the object of study precisely.

Definition 7.4 (Generalized linear model) A generalized linear model for independent responses \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) with known covariate vectors \(\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^p\) (the rows of the design matrix \(\mathbf X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\)) and known prior weights \(w_1,\dots,w_n>0\) consists of three components:

  1. Random component. Each \(Y_i\sim\mathrm{EDF}(\theta_i,\phi;w_i)\) independently, with the same cumulant function \(b\) and dispersion \(\phi\) across \(i\), so that by Theorem 7.1 \(\mu_i=\mathbb E[Y_i]=b'(\theta_i)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y_i)=a_i(\phi)V(\mu_i)\) with \(a_i(\phi)=\phi/w_i\).

  2. Systematic component. A linear predictor \(\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) with unknown coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol\beta\in\mathbb R^p\).

  3. Link. A link function \(g\) (Definition 7.2) with \(g(\mu_i)=\eta_i\).

The normal linear model of Chapter 6 (Definition 6.1) is the special case in which the random component is normal and the link is the identity. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. A GLM is assembled from three independent choices, and naming all three specifies the model completely: pick the response distribution (which fixes the mean-variance link through \(V\)), pick the covariates that enter the linear predictor, and pick the link scale. Logistic regression is the choice (Bernoulli, covariates, logit); Poisson regression is (Poisson, covariates, log); the linear model of Chapter 6 is (normal, covariates, identity). Everything downstream, the estimate, its standard error, the model tests, is then computed mechanically. Notice one small notational shift now that several observations are in play: the single dispersion divisor \(a(\phi)\) of Definition 7.1 becomes the per-observation \(a_i(\phi)=\phi/w_i\), differing only through the known weight \(w_i\).

7.4.1 Logistic regression

Take the Bernoulli or binomial random component and the canonical logit link. Writing \(p_i=\mu_i\) for the success probability, the model is \[ \mathrm{logit}(p_i)=\log\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta, \qquad p_i=\frac{e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}}{1+e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}}. \tag{7.7}\] The inverse link \(g^{-1}(\eta)=e^\eta/(1+e^\eta)\) is the standard logistic distribution function; it maps the whole real line into \((0,1)\), so fitted probabilities are automatically valid. The interpretation of the coefficients is the reason this model dominates the analysis of binary trial outcomes. The quantity \(p_i/(1-p_i)\) is the odds of success, and \(\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) is the log-odds. If two observations differ by one unit in covariate \(x_j\) and agree on every other covariate, their log-odds differ by exactly \(\beta_j\), so their odds differ by the multiplicative factor \(e^{\beta_j}\). That factor is the odds ratio (OR) associated with a unit change in \(x_j\): \[ \frac{\mathrm{odds}(x_j+1)}{\mathrm{odds}(x_j)} =\frac{p(x_j+1)/(1-p(x_j+1))}{p(x_j)/(1-p(x_j))}=e^{\beta_j}. \tag{7.8}\] A treatment indicator’s coefficient is therefore a log odds ratio, and \(e^\beta\) is the odds ratio that network meta-analysis combines across trials (Dias et al. (2018), Chapter 4). The conditioning on “every other covariate held fixed” in this interpretation is precisely the conditional-effect language whose divergence from the marginal effect we examine in Section 7.7. Hold onto the word conditional: this coefficient answers the question “what happens within a fixed covariate profile?”, whereas an indirect comparison often needs a marginal answer averaged over a whole trial population, and Section 7.7 shows that for the odds ratio these two answers can differ even with no confounding at all.

7.4.2 Poisson regression

Take the Poisson random component and the canonical log link. With \(\lambda_i=\mu_i\) the mean count, \[ \log\lambda_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta, \qquad \lambda_i=e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}. \tag{7.9}\] The mean is forced positive, as a count’s mean must be. A unit change in \(x_j\) multiplies the expected count by \(e^{\beta_j}\), the rate ratio. When the observations accrue over different exposures (a person-time \(t_i\) for a rate, or a denominator population for an incidence), one writes \(\lambda_i=t_i\,e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}\), equivalently \(\log\lambda_i=\log t_i+\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\). The term \(\log t_i\) is a known regressor with coefficient fixed at \(1\), called an offset; it is not estimated. With an offset the linear predictor models the log-rate \(\log(\lambda_i/t_i)\), and \(e^{\beta_j}\) is an incidence rate ratio, the count analogue of the odds ratio.

7.4.3 The probit and complementary log-log links

For binary data the logit link is canonical but not the only sensible choice. Two non-canonical links recur in the ITC literature.

The probit link is \(g(\mu)=\Phi^{-1}(\mu)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function; its inverse link is \(g^{-1}(\eta)=\Phi(\eta)\). It arises from a latent-variable model: if an unobserved \(Y_i^\ast=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta+\varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) crosses zero, \(Y_i=\mathbf 1\{Y_i^\ast>0\}\), then \(P(Y_i=1)=P(\varepsilon_i>-\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta)=\Phi(\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta)\). The inverse-link derivative is \(\frac{d\mu}{d\eta}=\Phi'(\eta)\), the standard normal density. The probit and logit fits are numerically very close after rescaling the coefficients, because the logistic and normal distribution functions are similar in shape; the logit is usually preferred only because its coefficients carry the exact log odds ratio interpretation of Equation 7.8.

The complementary log-log (cloglog) link is \(g(\mu)=\log(-\log(1-\mu))\), with inverse link \(g^{-1}(\eta)=1-\exp(-e^\eta)\). Unlike the logit and probit it is asymmetric in \(\mu\) and \(1-\mu\). It arises from grouped survival data under a proportional hazards mechanism: if a continuous event time has hazard multiplied by \(e^{\mathbf x^\top\boldsymbol\beta}\), the probability of an event within a fixed interval satisfies \(\log(-\log(1-\mu))=\log(-\log S_0)+\mathbf x^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) for a baseline survival \(S_0\), exactly a cloglog GLM with offset \(\log(-\log S_0)\). This connection to the hazard ratio is the reason the cloglog link reappears when Section 7.7 discusses the non-collapsibility of the hazard ratio.

Remark (Why the link scale matters for indirect comparison). The choice of link is not a modeling nicety; it fixes the scale on which an indirect comparison does its arithmetic. Network meta-analysis and its regression extensions write the linear predictor of every trial arm as a study baseline plus relative treatment effects, and they add and subtract those effects on the link scale: the log-odds scale for binary outcomes, the log-rate scale for counts. A Bucher indirect comparison of treatments \(B\) and \(C\) through a common comparator \(A\) (developed in Chapter 14), for instance, forms \(d_{BC}=d_{AC}-d_{AB}\), subtracting two log odds ratios; the operation is coherent only because each \(d\) lives on the same additive logit scale. The link is thus the common currency of the whole enterprise, and the conditional, link-scale reading of a coefficient from Section 7.4.1 is the reading those methods rely on. The price of that convenience, charged in Section 7.7, is that a link-scale effect need not agree with the effect computed on population-averaged risks. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.5 Maximum likelihood estimation

The coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is estimated by maximum likelihood (Definition 5.8). Because the responses are independent EDF members, the log-likelihood is a sum of EDF log-densities, and its maximization has a remarkable structure: the stationarity condition is a weighted version of the normal equations of Chapter 6, and the Newton-type algorithm that solves it is a sequence of weighted linear regressions. We derive all of this from Equation 7.1.

What the software is doing. When a health researcher fits a logistic regression, the program is solving a single optimization problem: find the coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol\beta\) that makes the observed responses most probable under the model. Unlike linear regression, this has no closed-form answer for the logistic or Poisson likelihood, so the program proceeds in steps. It writes down the condition that the gradient of the log-likelihood vanishes (the score equations), measures the curvature of the log-likelihood (the Fisher information), and walks toward the maximum by a Newton-type update (Fisher scoring) that turns out to be an ordinary weighted least-squares fit (IRLS). The four named results of this section are exactly those four ingredients, in that order; the worked example of Section 7.8 then runs them on real numbers.

Throughout, the log-likelihood of the GLM as a function of \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is, dropping the additive constant \(\sum_i c(y_i,\phi)\) that does not involve \(\boldsymbol\beta\), \[ \ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)}{a_i(\phi)}, \qquad a_i(\phi)=\frac{\phi}{w_i}, \tag{7.10}\] in which each \(\theta_i=\theta(\mu_i)\) and \(\mu_i=g^{-1}(\eta_i)\) and \(\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) are functions of \(\boldsymbol\beta\) through the chain \(\boldsymbol\beta\mapsto\eta_i\mapsto\mu_i\mapsto\theta_i\).

7.5.1 The score equations

Theorem 7.2 (GLM score equations) For the GLM of Definition 7.4 the score function (Definition 5.5) \(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ell(\boldsymbol\beta)\) has components \[ U_j(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{w_i\,(y_i-\mu_i)}{\phi\,V(\mu_i)}\,\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}\,x_{ij}, \qquad j=1,\dots,p, \tag{7.11}\] equivalently, in matrix form with \(\mathbf D=\mathrm{diag}(d\mu_i/d\eta_i)\) and the response covariance \(\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf Y)=\mathrm{diag}(a_i(\phi)V(\mu_i))\), \[ \mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu). \tag{7.12}\] The maximum likelihood estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) solves \(\mathbf U(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})=\mathbf 0\). The dispersion \(\phi\) enters Equation 7.11 only as the overall factor \(1/\phi\), so the solution \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) does not depend on \(\phi\).

Proof. Differentiate Equation 7.10 by the chain rule along \(\boldsymbol\beta\mapsto\eta_i\mapsto\mu_i\mapsto\theta_i\). The four links of the chain are: \[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta_i}=\frac{y_i-b'(\theta_i)}{a_i(\phi)}=\frac{y_i-\mu_i}{a_i(\phi)}, \qquad \frac{d\theta_i}{d\mu_i}=\frac{1}{b''(\theta_i)}=\frac{1}{V(\mu_i)}, \] the first because \(\partial_{\theta_i}(y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i))=y_i-b'(\theta_i)\) and \(b'(\theta_i)=\mu_i\) by Theorem 7.1. The second factor unpacks in three short steps: (1) since \(\mu_i=b'(\theta_i)\), differentiating gives \(d\mu_i/d\theta_i=b''(\theta_i)\); (2) by the definition Equation 7.4 of the variance function, \(b''(\theta_i)=V(\mu_i)\); (3) the derivative of an inverse function is the reciprocal of the derivative, so \(d\theta_i/d\mu_i=1/(d\mu_i/d\theta_i)=1/V(\mu_i)\). Continuing the chain, \[ \frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}=\bigl(g'(\mu_i)\bigr)^{-1}, \qquad \frac{\partial\eta_i}{\partial\beta_j}=x_{ij}, \] the first because \(\eta_i=g(\mu_i)\) gives \(d\eta_i/d\mu_i=g'(\mu_i)\). Multiplying the four factors and summing over \(i\), \[ U_j(\boldsymbol\beta)=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_j} =\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{y_i-\mu_i}{a_i(\phi)}\cdot\frac{1}{V(\mu_i)}\cdot\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}\cdot x_{ij} =\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{w_i(y_i-\mu_i)}{\phi\,V(\mu_i)}\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}x_{ij}, \] using \(a_i(\phi)=\phi/w_i\). This is Equation 7.11. Collecting the summands into matrices with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(a_i(\phi)V(\mu_i))\) gives the \(j\)-th component of \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu)\), which is Equation 7.12. The maximum likelihood estimator is an interior stationary point of the differentiable concave-or-not log-likelihood, so it satisfies the first-order condition \(\mathbf U(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})=\mathbf 0\). Finally, every summand of Equation 7.11 carries the same factor \(1/\phi\), so \(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\phi^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf U}(\boldsymbol\beta)\) for a \(\phi\)-free vector \(\tilde{\mathbf U}\); the equation \(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf 0\) is equivalent to \(\tilde{\mathbf U}(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf 0\), and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is unchanged by \(\phi\). \(\square\)

The score equation has a transparent reading: \(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf 0\) requires the covariate-weighted residuals \(y_i-\mu_i\) to vanish, with each residual weighted by the local sensitivity of the mean to the linear predictor and inversely by its variance. Why this matters: this is the GLM’s version of the orthogonality between residuals and the design columns that defined ordinary least squares in Chapter 6, and it is the exact condition every fitting routine drives to zero. For the canonical link a dramatic simplification occurs.

Corollary 7.1 (Score equations under the canonical link) Under the canonical link, \(d\mu_i/d\eta_i=V(\mu_i)\), and the score equations reduce to \[ \mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\frac{1}{\phi}\,\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W_0(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu) \;\;\text{with}\;\;\mathbf W_0=\mathrm{diag}(w_i), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n w_i(y_i-\mu_i)\,\mathbf x_i=\mathbf 0. \tag{7.13}\] In particular, if the model contains an intercept (a column of \(\mathbf X\) equal to \(\mathbf 1_n\)), then \(\sum_i w_i\hat\mu_i=\sum_i w_iy_i\): the fitted values reproduce the response total exactly.

Proof. With the canonical link \(\eta_i=\theta_i\) by Proposition 7.1, so \(d\mu_i/d\eta_i=d\mu_i/d\theta_i=b''(\theta_i)=V(\mu_i)\). Substituting into Equation 7.11, the factor \(V(\mu_i)\) cancels: \[ U_j(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_i\frac{w_i(y_i-\mu_i)}{\phi V(\mu_i)}V(\mu_i)x_{ij} =\frac1\phi\sum_i w_i(y_i-\mu_i)x_{ij}. \] This is Equation 7.13. If a column of \(\mathbf X\) is \(\mathbf 1_n\), the corresponding component of \(\mathbf U(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})=\mathbf 0\) reads \(\sum_i w_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)=0\). \(\square\)

Remark (The logistic score in its familiar form). It is worth seeing Corollary 7.1 land on the textbook logistic score before meeting the general machinery. For logistic regression the EDF member is the binomial proportion with weight \(w_i=n_i\) and mean \(\mu_i=p_i\), so the canonical (logit) score equations read \[ \sum_{i=1}^n n_i\,(y_i-p_i)\,\mathbf x_i=\mathbf 0: \] the covariate-weighted gap between observed proportions \(y_i\) and fitted probabilities \(p_i\) must vanish, exactly the form quoted in introductory texts. With an intercept column this forces \(\sum_i n_i p_i=\sum_i n_i y_i=\sum_i R_i\), so the fitted model reproduces the total number of responders. For the single arm of Example 7.1, an intercept-only fit solves \(60\,(0.30-\hat p)=0\), returning \(\hat p=0.30\), the observed proportion. The general formula Equation 7.11 is just this familiar object dressed for an arbitrary link and distribution. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.5.2 Fisher information and Fisher scoring

To solve \(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf 0\) numerically we need the curvature of the log-likelihood. We use the expected information rather than the observed Hessian, an algorithm due to Fisher, because its form is especially clean.

Proposition 7.2 (Fisher information of a GLM) The Fisher information matrix (Definition 5.7) of the GLM of Definition 7.4 is \[ \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X, \qquad \mathbf W=\mathrm{diag}(W_i), \quad W_i=\frac{w_i}{\phi\,V(\mu_i)}\left(\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}\right)^2. \tag{7.14}\] The weights \(W_i\) are positive, and \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta)\succeq 0\), with \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta)\succ 0\) whenever \(\mathbf X\) has full column rank \(p\).

Proof. By the information equality (Theorem 5.4), \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta))\). From Equation 7.12, \(\mathbf U=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu)\), where only \(\mathbf y\) is random with \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf y)=\boldsymbol\Sigma\). Hence \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf U) =\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf D\,\mathbf X =\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf D\,\mathbf X, \] using that \(\mathbf D\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) are diagonal hence symmetric. The diagonal matrix \(\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf D\) has \(i\)-th entry \[ \frac{(d\mu_i/d\eta_i)^2}{a_i(\phi)V(\mu_i)}=\frac{w_i}{\phi V(\mu_i)}\left(\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}\right)^2=W_i, \] so \(\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf D=\mathbf W\) and \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\). Each \(W_i>0\) because \(w_i,\phi,V(\mu_i)>0\) and \(d\mu_i/d\eta_i\neq0\) (the link is strictly monotone). For any \(\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^p\), \(\mathbf v^\top\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\mathbf v=\|\mathbf W^{1/2}\mathbf X\mathbf v\|_2^2\ge0\), so \(\mathcal I\succeq0\); it equals zero only if \(\mathbf W^{1/2}\mathbf X\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\), i.e. \(\mathbf X\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) (as \(\mathbf W^{1/2}\) is invertible), which forces \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) when \(\mathbf X\) has full column rank. The characterization of positive definiteness used here is Theorem 3.3. \(\square\)

Why this matters. The information matrix \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\) is the GLM’s analogue of the \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) that governed precision in Chapter 6, now reweighted by the working weights \(\mathbf W\). Its inverse is the large-sample covariance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) (Corollary 7.2), so the diagonal entries of \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\) are the squared standard errors that a fitted model prints beside each coefficient. Curvature of the log-likelihood becomes precision of the estimate; this is the bridge from the abstract Fisher information to the standard error on a report.

The Fisher scoring algorithm is the Newton-type iteration that replaces the observed Hessian by its expectation \(\mathcal I\): \[ \boldsymbol\beta^{(m+1)}=\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}+\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)})^{-1}\,\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}). \tag{7.15}\] We now show that this iteration is exactly a weighted least squares fit, which is the content of the acronym IRLS and the reason GLMs are computationally no harder than linear regression.

7.5.3 Iteratively reweighted least squares

Theorem 7.3 (Iteratively reweighted least squares) The Fisher scoring update Equation 7.15 for the GLM of Definition 7.4 is identical to the weighted least squares estimator \[ \boldsymbol\beta^{(m+1)}=\bigl(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf X\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf z^{(m)}, \tag{7.16}\] in which the working weights \(\mathbf W^{(m)}=\mathrm{diag}(W_i^{(m)})\) are the information weights of Equation 7.14 and the working response has components \[ z_i^{(m)}=\eta_i^{(m)}+\bigl(y_i-\mu_i^{(m)}\bigr)\,\frac{d\eta_i}{d\mu_i}\bigg|^{(m)} =\eta_i^{(m)}+\bigl(y_i-\mu_i^{(m)}\bigr)\,g'(\mu_i^{(m)}), \tag{7.17}\] all quantities evaluated at \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}\). Each iteration is therefore the solution of the weighted normal equations \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf X\,\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf z^{(m)}\) of Chapter 6 applied to the data \((\mathbf z^{(m)},\mathbf X)\) with weights \(\mathbf W^{(m)}\). The common factor \(1/\phi\) in \(\mathbf W^{(m)}\) cancels between \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf X)^{-1}\) and \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\), so neither the iterates nor their limit \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) depend on \(\phi\).

Proof. Write \(\mathbf D=\mathrm{diag}(d\mu_i/d\eta_i)\), evaluated at \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}\), and recall from the proof of Proposition 7.2 that \(\mathbf W=\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf D\), hence \(\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}=\mathbf W\mathbf D^{-1}\) (all factors diagonal and invertible). By Equation 7.12 the score is \[ \mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)})=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu^{(m)}) =\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\,\mathbf D^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu^{(m)}). \] The diagonal matrix \(\mathbf D^{-1}\) has \(i\)-th entry \(d\eta_i/d\mu_i=g'(\mu_i)\), so \(\mathbf D^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu^{(m)})\) has \(i\)-th entry \((y_i-\mu_i^{(m)})g'(\mu_i^{(m)})=z_i^{(m)}-\eta_i^{(m)}\) by Equation 7.17. Therefore \[ \mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)})=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\,(\mathbf z^{(m)}-\boldsymbol\eta^{(m)}), \qquad\boldsymbol\eta^{(m)}=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}. \] Insert this and \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)})=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\) (Equation 7.14) into the Fisher scoring update Equation 7.15: \[ \boldsymbol\beta^{(m+1)} =\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}+(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W(\mathbf z^{(m)}-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}). \] Now \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}=\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}\), so the two \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}\) terms cancel: \[ \boldsymbol\beta^{(m+1)}=\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)}+(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf z^{(m)}-\boldsymbol\beta^{(m)} =(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf z^{(m)}, \] which is Equation 7.16. This is the weighted least squares solution because it solves the weighted normal equations \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf z^{(m)}\), the weighted analogue of Theorem 6.1. Writing \(\mathbf W^{(m)}=\phi^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf W}^{(m)}\) with \(\tilde{\mathbf W}^{(m)}\) free of \(\phi\), the scalar \(\phi^{-1}\) multiplies both \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf X\) and \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W^{(m)}\mathbf z^{(m)}\) and cancels in Equation 7.16. \(\square\)

Remark (IRLS in words, and why it is a sequence of linear regressions). The algorithm Equation 7.16 says: given a current coefficient guess, form a synthetic response \(\mathbf z\) by taking the current linear predictor and adding the raw residual rescaled to the linear-predictor scale, attach the weight \(W_i\) to observation \(i\), run an ordinary weighted linear regression of \(\mathbf z\) on \(\mathbf X\), and repeat until the coefficients stop changing. Because each step is a weighted projection of \(\mathbf z\) onto \(\mathcal C(\mathbf X)\) in the \(\mathbf W\)-inner product (Definition 6.3), every tool of Chapter 6, the hat matrix, leverages, and the standard-error formula, transfers verbatim to the final iteration. This is why fitting a logistic or Poisson regression costs no more than a handful of linear regressions, and why GLM software is built on a linear-algebra core. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (One IRLS step, with numbers). To watch the machinery move before meeting more theory, take the two-group logistic data of Section 7.8 (\(R_1=2\) of \(n_1=10\) at \(x=0\); \(R_2=7\) of \(n_2=10\) at \(x=1\)) and start from \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(0)}=(0,0)^\top\), so every fitted probability is \(p_i^{(0)}=\tfrac12\). The recipe of Equation 7.17 and Equation 7.14 gives, for group \(1\), a working response \(z_1=0+(0.2-0.5)/(0.5\cdot0.5)=-1.2\) and a working weight \(W_1=n_1p(1-p)=10(0.25)=2.5\), and likewise \(z_2=0.8\), \(W_2=2.5\). A single weighted least squares fit of the synthetic response \(\mathbf z=(-1.2,\,0.8)^\top\) on \(\mathbf X\) with these weights returns \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(1)}=(-1.2,\,2.0)^\top\), already most of the way to the maximum likelihood value \((-1.386,\,2.234)\). That is the whole algorithm in one line: build \(\mathbf z\), attach the weights, run one linear regression, repeat. The full iteration and its convergence are carried out in Section 7.8. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

For the canonical link the algorithm has a further property: Fisher scoring coincides with the exact Newton-Raphson method, because the observed and expected information agree.

Lemma 7.1 (Canonical link: observed equals expected information, and concavity) Under the canonical link the Hessian of the log-likelihood is the nonrandom matrix \[ \nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=-\,\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X=-\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta), \] so the observed information equals the expected information and Fisher scoring Equation 7.15 is Newton-Raphson. Consequently \(\ell\) is concave in \(\boldsymbol\beta\), and strictly concave when \(\mathbf X\) has full column rank, so a stationary point is the unique global maximizer.

Proof. Under the canonical link, \(\eta_i=\theta_i\) (Proposition 7.1), so Equation 7.10 becomes \(\ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_i(y_i\eta_i-b(\eta_i))/a_i(\phi)\) with \(\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\). Differentiating twice, \[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_j}=\sum_i\frac{(y_i-b'(\eta_i))x_{ij}}{a_i(\phi)}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\beta_j\partial\beta_k}=-\sum_i\frac{b''(\eta_i)x_{ij}x_{ik}}{a_i(\phi)}. \] Because \(b''(\eta_i)=V(\mu_i)\) and \(a_i(\phi)=\phi/w_i\), the \(i\)-th coefficient is \(w_iV(\mu_i)/\phi=W_i\) (the canonical-link weight, since \(d\mu_i/d\eta_i=V(\mu_i)\) makes \(W_i=w_iV(\mu_i)^2/(\phi V(\mu_i))=w_iV(\mu_i)/\phi\)). Hence \(\nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=-\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X=-\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta)\). This matrix does not involve \(\mathbf y\), so the observed information \(-\nabla^2\ell\) equals the expected information \(\mathcal I\), and the Newton step \(-(\nabla^2\ell)^{-1}\mathbf U=\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf U\) is the Fisher scoring step. Since \(\mathcal I=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\succeq0\) by Proposition 7.2, \(\nabla^2\ell\preceq0\) and \(\ell\) is concave; full column rank gives \(\mathcal I\succ0\), strict concavity, and uniqueness of the maximizer. \(\square\)

Remark (When the maximizer fails to exist: separation). Strict concavity guarantees the maximizer is unique if it exists, but for logistic regression it may fail to exist. If a hyperplane \(\mathbf x^\top\boldsymbol\beta=0\) perfectly separates the successes from the failures, the likelihood increases without bound as \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\to\infty\) along the separating direction, and the IRLS weights \(W_i=n_ip_i(1-p_i)\to0\). For a concrete health-data instance, take a subgroup table in which all \(8\) treated patients respond and none of the \(6\) controls do: the fitted treated odds are \(8/0\), the estimated log odds ratio marches off to \(+\infty\), and no finite \(\hat\beta_1\) solves the score equation. This is complete separation; the finite-sample maximum likelihood estimator does not exist, even though the population parameter is perfectly well defined. The phenomenon is benign in large randomized trials but common in sparse subgroup tables, and it motivates the penalized and Bayesian fits of Chapter 8. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (Overdispersion: where the dispersion re-enters). Theorem 7.2 showed that \(\phi\) cancels from the estimating equation, so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is the same whether the binomial or Poisson dispersion takes its nominal value \(\phi=1\) or not. That does not make \(\phi\) irrelevant. Real trial and count data are often overdispersed: the observed spread of the proportions or counts exceeds the \(V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)\) or \(V(\mu)=\mu\) that the family mandates, usually because of unmodeled heterogeneity between patients or clusters (extra-binomial or extra-Poisson variation). The point estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) remain valid, but the model-based standard errors from \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\), computed as if \(\phi=1\), are then too small. The standard remedies estimate a free dispersion \(\hat\phi>1\) and inflate every standard error by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\) (quasi-likelihood), or move to a genuinely overdispersed model such as the negative binomial or a random-effects GLM, the latter being the subject of Chapter 8. The one thing overdispersion never does is change which \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) solves the score equation. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.5.4 Large-sample distribution of the estimator

The estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is a maximum likelihood estimator of a regular parametric model, so its large-sample behavior is governed by the general theorem of Chapter 5. We state the specialization and verify its hypotheses.

Corollary 7.2 (Asymptotic normality of the GLM estimator) Consider the GLM of Definition 7.4 with true coefficient \(\boldsymbol\beta_0\), fixed dimension \(p\), and design sequence such that \(\frac1n\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W(\boldsymbol\beta_0)\mathbf X\to\mathbf A\) for a positive definite limit \(\mathbf A\), with the regularity conditions (A1) to (A3) of Theorem 5.6 holding (they do for the canonical link with full column rank by Lemma 7.1). Then the maximum likelihood estimator satisfies \[ \sqrt n\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta_0)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\mathcal N_p\!\bigl(\mathbf 0,\;\mathbf A^{-1}\bigr), \tag{7.18}\] and in the working finite-sample form \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\;\dot\sim\;\mathcal N_p\!\bigl(\boldsymbol\beta_0,\,(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\bigr)\). A standard error for \(\hat\beta_j\) is the square root of the \((j,j)\) entry of \((\mathbf X^\top\hat{\mathbf W}\mathbf X)^{-1}\), with \(\hat{\mathbf W}=\mathbf W(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})\).

Why this matters. This corollary is the reason a fitted GLM reports more than a point estimate. The limiting covariance \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\) is exactly what statistical software returns as the squared standard errors next to each coefficient, and exponentiating a Wald interval for \(\hat\beta_j\) (the next remark) is how the same software produces the confidence interval for an odds ratio or rate ratio that a meta-analysis then carries forward. Without this theorem an estimate would be a bare number with no measure of its uncertainty, and no indirect comparison could be built on it.

Proof. The GLM is a parametric model with log-likelihood Equation 7.10, score \(\mathbf U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf D\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu)\) (Theorem 7.2), and Fisher information \(\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\) (Proposition 7.2), where the subscript \(n\) records that this is the information in all \(n\) observations. The per-observation scores are independent with mean zero (by Theorem 7.2 each summand of Equation 7.11 has expectation \(\propto\mathbb E[y_i-\mu_i]=0\)) and finite covariance, so the hypotheses of Theorem 5.6 are exactly the stated regularity conditions. For the canonical link, Lemma 7.1 shows the Hessian is the constant matrix \(-\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\), so condition (A3) (a local uniform law for the Hessian) holds trivially and the model is regular on a neighborhood of \(\boldsymbol\beta_0\); condition (A2) is \(\mathbf A\succ0\). Applying Theorem 5.6 with total information \(\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0)\) and the scaling \(\frac1n\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0)=\frac1n\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\to\mathbf A\) yields Equation 7.18. The finite-sample statement substitutes \(\mathcal I_n^{-1}=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\) for \(\frac1n\mathbf A^{-1}\), and consistency of \(\hat{\mathbf W}\) for \(\mathbf W(\boldsymbol\beta_0)\) (continuity of \(\mathbf W(\cdot)\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\xrightarrow{p}\boldsymbol\beta_0\)) with the continuous mapping theorem justifies the plug-in standard error. \(\square\)

Remark (Standard errors for odds ratios and rate ratios). The reported effect is usually not \(\hat\beta_j\) but the odds ratio \(e^{\hat\beta_j}\) or rate ratio \(e^{\hat\beta_j}\). By the delta method (Theorem 5.3) with \(h(\beta)=e^\beta\) and \(h'(\beta)=e^\beta\), \[ \mathrm{SE}\bigl(e^{\hat\beta_j}\bigr)\approx e^{\hat\beta_j}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j), \] and a confidence interval for the odds ratio is obtained by exponentiating the endpoints of a Wald interval for \(\beta_j\), which respects the positivity of the ratio. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (Why this matters for indirect comparison). Everything in this section runs once per trial arm when an indirect comparison is assembled. The score equation locates each arm’s fitted log-odds or log-rate; iteratively reweighted least squares computes it at the cost of a few linear regressions; the asymptotic theory attaches a standard error to it. A network meta-analysis then treats those per-arm link-scale effects, with their standard errors, as the data it combines (Chapters 13 to 15). The estimation machinery here is therefore not a detour: it is the exact procedure that turns a stack of trial two-by-two tables into the log odds ratios and variances the later chapters pool. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.6 Deviance

The residual sum of squares of Chapter 6 measured the misfit of a linear model and drove its \(F\)-tests. Its GLM generalization is the deviance, and the idea behind it is a direct translation of that story. In a linear model, misfit is the squared gap between the data and the fit; in a GLM, where the response need not be normal, the natural currency of fit is not squared error but log-likelihood, so we measure misfit by how far the fitted model’s log-likelihood falls short of the best any model in the family could achieve on this data. That best is the saturated model, which fits every observation exactly. Twice the log-likelihood gap between the saturated fit and our fit is the deviance: it is nonnegative, it is zero exactly when the fit is perfect, and (as Proposition 7.3 will show) for the normal linear model it equals the residual sum of squares outright, so the deviance genuinely extends Chapter 6 rather than replacing it. Differences of deviances between nested models then test whether added covariates improve fit, the GLM version of the extra-sum-of-squares \(F\)-test. We build these objects in order: the saturated model, the deviance itself, its nonnegativity, and finally the model-comparison test.

Definition 7.5 (Saturated model) The saturated model for responses \(y_1,\dots,y_n\) is the GLM with one free mean parameter per observation, fitted at \(\tilde\mu_i=y_i\) for every \(i\), with natural parameter \(\tilde\theta_i=\theta(y_i)\). It attains the largest log-likelihood achievable by any model in the family for the given data, because each term \(y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)\) in Equation 7.10 is maximized over \(\theta_i\) where its derivative vanishes, \(\frac{d}{d\theta_i}\bigl[y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)\bigr]=y_i-b'(\theta_i)=0\), that is at \(b'(\theta_i)=y_i\), i.e. at \(\theta_i=\tilde\theta_i\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 7.6 (Deviance and residual deviance) Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) be the fitted coefficients of a GLM, with fitted means \(\hat\mu_i\) and natural parameters \(\hat\theta_i=\theta(\hat\mu_i)\). The scaled deviance is twice the log-likelihood gap between the saturated model and the fitted model, \[ D^\ast(\mathbf y;\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})=2\bigl[\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}(\mathbf y)-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})\bigr] =\frac{2}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\Bigl[y_i(\tilde\theta_i-\hat\theta_i)-b(\tilde\theta_i)+b(\hat\theta_i)\Bigr], \tag{7.19}\] and the deviance is its dispersion-free numerator \[ D(\mathbf y;\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})=\phi\,D^\ast(\mathbf y;\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}) =2\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\Bigl[y_i(\tilde\theta_i-\hat\theta_i)-b(\tilde\theta_i)+b(\hat\theta_i)\Bigr] =\sum_{i=1}^n d_i. \tag{7.20}\] The value of \(D\) at the fitted model is the residual deviance; the deviance residual of observation \(i\) is \(r_i^D=\mathrm{sign}(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\sqrt{d_i}\), so that \(\sum_i(r_i^D)^2=D\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. The deviance is one number saying how far the fitted model sits from a flawless fit: large deviance, poor fit; zero deviance, perfect fit. The scaled deviance simply divides by the dispersion \(\phi\) (for the binomial and Poisson \(\phi=1\), so the two coincide). The deviance residuals \(r_i^D\) are the GLM’s stand-ins for the ordinary residuals \(y_i-\hat\mu_i\) of Chapter 6: they carry the sign of the raw residual, and their squares sum to the total deviance, exactly as squared residuals summed to the residual sum of squares.

Remark (Bregman divergence (a named building block)). The proof of the next proposition uses one recurring construction, so we name it once here rather than mid-argument. For a smooth strictly convex function \(b\), the Bregman divergence from \(v\) to \(u\) is \[ D_b(u,v)=b(u)-b(v)-b'(v)(u-v), \] the gap at the point \(u\) between \(b\) itself and its tangent line drawn at \(v\). Because a convex function lies on or above each of its tangents, \(D_b(u,v)\ge0\), with equality only when \(u=v\); this single inequality is what forces the deviance to be nonnegative. No prior acquaintance with the term is assumed: it is just a name for “function minus its own tangent line.” \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proposition 7.3 (The deviance is a sum of nonnegative Bregman divergences) Each deviance contribution satisfies \(d_i\ge0\), with \(d_i=0\) if and only if \(\hat\mu_i=y_i\). Hence \(D\ge0\), and \(D=0\) if and only if the fit is saturated. For the normal linear model with identity link, \(D=\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2\) is the residual sum of squares of Chapter 6.

Proof. Fix \(i\) and write \(u=\hat\theta_i\), \(v=\tilde\theta_i\). Since \(\tilde\mu_i=y_i=b'(\tilde\theta_i)=b'(v)\), \[ \tfrac12\,d_i/w_i=y_i(v-u)-b(v)+b(u)=b'(v)(v-u)-b(v)+b(u)=b(u)-b(v)-b'(v)(u-v)=:D_b(u,v). \] The quantity \(D_b(u,v)\) is exactly the Bregman divergence of the convex function \(b\) just defined: by Taylor’s theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder there is \(\xi\) between \(u\) and \(v\) with \(D_b(u,v)=\tfrac12 b''(\xi)(u-v)^2\ge0\), since \(b''>0\). It is zero exactly when \(u=v\), i.e. \(\hat\theta_i=\tilde\theta_i\), equivalently \(\hat\mu_i=b'(\hat\theta_i)=b'(\tilde\theta_i)=y_i\). Thus \(d_i=2w_iD_b(u,v)\ge0\) with equality iff \(\hat\mu_i=y_i\), and summing gives \(D\ge0\) with equality iff the fit is saturated. For the normal model \(b(\theta)=\theta^2/2\), \(\theta=\mu\), \(w_i=1\), so \(d_i=2[y_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)-\tfrac12y_i^2+\tfrac12\hat\mu_i^2]=y_i^2-2y_i\hat\mu_i+\hat\mu_i^2=(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2\), and \(D=\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2=\mathrm{RSS}\). \(\square\)

The deviance specializes to clean closed forms for the discrete families, which are the ones used in practice.

Example 7.4 (Poisson and binomial deviance) Poisson. With \(b(\theta)=e^\theta\), \(\theta=\log\mu\), \(w_i=1\), the \(i\)-th contribution is \(d_i=2[y_i\log y_i-y_i-y_i\log\hat\mu_i+\hat\mu_i]=2[y_i\log(y_i/\hat\mu_i)-(y_i-\hat\mu_i)]\), so \[ D_{\mathrm{Poisson}}=2\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i}-(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\Bigr], \] with the convention \(0\log0=0\). If the model has an intercept then \(\sum_i\hat\mu_i=\sum_iy_i\) by Corollary 7.1, and the second term vanishes, leaving \(D=2\sum_iy_i\log(y_i/\hat\mu_i)\).

Binomial proportion. With \(y_i=R_i/n_i\), \(\hat\mu_i=\hat p_i\), \(w_i=n_i\), and writing the fitted counts \(\hat R_i=n_i\hat p_i\), the contribution is \(d_i=2n_i[y_i\log(y_i/\hat p_i)+(1-y_i)\log((1-y_i)/(1-\hat p_i))]\), so in count form \[ D_{\mathrm{binom}}=2\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl[R_i\log\frac{R_i}{\hat R_i}+(n_i-R_i)\log\frac{n_i-R_i}{n_i-\hat R_i}\Bigr]. \] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.6.1 Deviance for model comparison

The deviance is to GLMs what the residual sum of squares is to linear models: the basis of the goodness-of-fit and nested-model tests. The key fact is that a difference of scaled deviances is exactly a likelihood ratio statistic.

Theorem 7.4 (Analysis of deviance) Let \(M_0\subset M_1\) be nested GLMs with the same EDF, the same link, and the same known dispersion \(\phi\), with \(p_0<p_1\) parameters, fitted by maximum likelihood with residual deviances \(D_0\ge D_1\). Then the scaled deviance difference equals the likelihood ratio statistic for \(M_0\) within \(M_1\), \[ \frac{D_0-D_1}{\phi}=2\bigl[\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1)-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)\bigr], \tag{7.21}\] and under \(M_0\), as \(n\to\infty\), \[ \frac{D_0-D_1}{\phi}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\chi^2_{\,p_1-p_0}. \tag{7.22}\] When \(\phi\) is unknown (as for the normal or gamma families) it is replaced by a consistent estimator \(\hat\phi\) and \((D_0-D_1)/\{\hat\phi(p_1-p_0)\}\) is referred to an \(F_{p_1-p_0,\,n-p_1}\) distribution.

Proof. By Equation 7.19 the scaled deviance of a model is \(D^\ast=2[\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})]\), and the saturated log-likelihood \(\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}(\mathbf y)\) does not depend on which fitted model is used. Subtracting, \[ \frac{D_0-D_1}{\phi}=D_0^\ast-D_1^\ast =2[\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)]-2[\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1)] =2[\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1)-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)], \] which is Equation 7.21: the saturated term cancels and what remains is twice the maximized log-likelihood gap between the two models, i.e. the log likelihood ratio statistic for testing \(M_0\) inside \(M_1\). Under \(M_0\) this statistic converges in distribution to \(\chi^2_{p_1-p_0}\) by Wilks’s theorem (Theorem 5.9), the number of degrees of freedom being the difference \(p_1-p_0\) in the number of free parameters. The \(F\) reference distribution for unknown \(\phi\) follows from writing the statistic as a ratio of the deviance difference to an independent estimator of \(\phi\) scaled by its degrees of freedom, exactly as the linear-model \(F\)-test of Chapter 6 arises from the ratio of two sums of squares. \(\square\)

Remark (Reading an analysis-of-deviance table). Theorem Theorem 7.4 licenses the standard workflow: fit a sequence of nested models, tabulate their residual deviances, and test each added term by referring the drop in deviance to a chi-square (or \(F\)) distribution on the added degrees of freedom. This is the GLM counterpart of the analysis-of-variance \(F\)-test used for the linear model of Chapter 6: the drop in deviance plays the role of the extra regression sum of squares, and for the normal family with the identity link the deviance test reduces to exactly that \(F\)-test. The residual deviance of a single model, compared to its degrees of freedom \(n-p\), is itself a rough absolute goodness-of-fit check, because under a correctly specified model with \(\phi\) known the residual deviance is approximately \(\chi^2_{n-p}\) in favorable (large per-group count) regimes. The worked example of Section 7.8 carries this out by hand. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.7 Non-collapsibility of the odds ratio and the hazard ratio

We now reach the phenomenon that distinguishes nonlinear-link GLMs from the linear model and that reverberates through the rest of the book. In a linear model the coefficient of a treatment indicator is both the effect within every covariate stratum and the effect on the population average, because expectation is linear. For the odds ratio and the hazard ratio this is false: an effect that is constant across strata need not equal the effect computed from the population-averaged risks. The marginal and the conditional effect are different numbers, and the difference is not bias from confounding but an arithmetic consequence of the nonlinearity of the link.

To see why the linear model is the special case, write the marginal treatment effect on the identity scale as the average of the stratum-specific effects. With no confounding, \[ \mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)]-\mathbb E_X[\mu_0(X)]=\mathbb E_X\bigl[\mu_1(X)-\mu_0(X)\bigr]=\delta, \] so if the within-stratum difference is a constant \(\delta\), the population-average difference is that same \(\delta\). Linearity of the expectation is doing all the work; the odds ratio and hazard ratio break precisely because their scales are nonlinear.

For a health researcher the phenomenon is concrete before it is formal. Suppose a trial reports a conditional odds ratio of \(3.5\) for an active drug versus control, and that this same \(3.5\) holds in both younger and older patients, so there is no effect modification by age. Yet when the two age groups are pooled and a single odds ratio is computed from the combined response rates, the answer is about \(3.14\), not \(3.5\). Nothing was confounded and no subgroup interaction was hidden; the pooled number simply differs from the common stratum number by arithmetic. The whole of this section formalizes that observation, and its consequence for a systematic review is immediate: an odds ratio reported by a trial is a conditional effect tied to the covariates in that trial’s model, whereas an indirect comparison may need a marginal effect for a target population, and the two are not interchangeable even under flawless randomization. The numbers \(3.5\) and \(3.14\) reappear, computed exactly, in Example 7.5.

7.7.1 A precise definition

We work with two treatments, indexed by the potential outcomes \(Y(0),Y(1)\) of Notation, and a baseline covariate \(X\) that stratifies the population. Write \(\mu_t(x)=\mathbb E[Y(t)\mid X=x]\) for the stratum-specific mean outcome under treatment \(t\), and assume throughout this section the no-confounding identity \(\mathbb E[Y(t)]=\mathbb E_X[\mu_t(X)]\), so that the marginal mean is the average of the conditional means. We compare effects measured on the scale of a strictly increasing transform \(g\), the effect measure’s link (logit for the odds ratio).

Definition 7.7 (Collapsibility and non-collapsibility of an effect measure) For a strictly increasing transform \(g\) define the conditional effect in stratum \(x\) and the marginal effect by \[ \psi_g(x)=g(\mu_1(x))-g(\mu_0(x)), \qquad \psi_g^{\mathrm M}=g\bigl(\mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)]\bigr)-g\bigl(\mathbb E_X[\mu_0(X)]\bigr). \tag{7.23}\] The effect measure based on \(g\) is collapsible over \(X\) if, whenever the conditional effect is constant in \(x\) (no effect modification on the \(g\)-scale), \(\psi_g(x)\equiv\psi_g\), the marginal effect equals that common value, \(\psi_g^{\mathrm M}=\psi_g\), for every distribution of \(X\) and every pair of stratum means \(\mu_0(\cdot),\mu_1(\cdot)\) consistent with it. The measure is non-collapsible if it is not collapsible, that is, if there exist stratum means with constant conditional effect for which \(\psi_g^{\mathrm M}\neq\psi_g\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

In plain terms, a measure is collapsible when a conditional effect that is identical in every stratum is inherited unchanged by the pooled population, no matter how large or small the strata are; it is non-collapsible when pooling can move that common number even though nothing modifies the effect from one stratum to the next.

The definition isolates the question cleanly: collapsibility is the statement that the link commutes with averaging across strata when the conditional effect is constant. For some scales it does.

Proposition 7.4 (The risk difference and the risk ratio are collapsible) The identity-scale effect measure (the risk difference, \(g(\mu)=\mu\)) and the log-scale effect measure (the risk ratio, \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\)) are collapsible over \(X\).

[machine-checked: theories/STC.v, Lemma STC_collapse_linear]

Proof. Risk difference. Suppose \(\psi_{\mathrm{id}}(x)=\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)\equiv\delta\). By linearity of expectation, \[ \psi_{\mathrm{id}}^{\mathrm M}=\mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)]-\mathbb E_X[\mu_0(X)]=\mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)-\mu_0(X)]=\mathbb E_X[\delta]=\delta. \] Risk ratio. Suppose \(\psi_{\log}(x)=\log\mu_1(x)-\log\mu_0(x)\equiv\rho\), equivalently \(\mu_1(x)=e^\rho\mu_0(x)\) for all \(x\). Taking expectations, \(\mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)]=e^\rho\mathbb E_X[\mu_0(X)]\), so \[ \psi_{\log}^{\mathrm M}=\log\frac{\mathbb E_X[\mu_1(X)]}{\mathbb E_X[\mu_0(X)]}=\log e^\rho=\rho. \] In both cases the marginal effect equals the common conditional effect for every distribution of \(X\), so the measure is collapsible. The mean-difference (identity-link) case is the one verified in the Coq development, where it is proved from linearity of expectation alone, exactly the property that the nonlinear logit lacks. \(\square\)

7.7.2 The odds ratio is non-collapsible

The contrast with the logit scale is sharp, and a single explicit example proves non-collapsibility.

Example 7.5 (A two-stratum non-collapsible odds ratio) Read \(X\) as an age group (\(0\) for younger, \(1\) for older patients), split evenly across the trial, and read each pair of numbers below as the probability of response under control and under an active drug in that age group. Let \(X\in\{0,1\}\) be this binary prognostic covariate with \(P(X=0)=P(X=1)=\tfrac12\), and let the stratum-specific outcome probabilities under control and treatment be \[ \mu_0(0)=0.3,\quad \mu_1(0)=0.6,\qquad \mu_0(1)=0.6,\quad \mu_1(1)=0.84. \] The conditional log odds ratio is the same in both strata. In stratum \(X=0\), \[ \psi_{\mathrm{logit}}(0)=\mathrm{logit}(0.6)-\mathrm{logit}(0.3) =\log\frac{0.6/0.4}{0.3/0.7}=\log\frac{1.5}{0.428571\ldots}=\log 3.5=1.25276\ldots, \] and in stratum \(X=1\), \[ \psi_{\mathrm{logit}}(1)=\mathrm{logit}(0.84)-\mathrm{logit}(0.6) =\log\frac{0.84/0.16}{0.6/0.4}=\log\frac{5.25}{1.5}=\log 3.5=1.25276\ldots . \] So the conditional effect is constant, \(\psi_{\mathrm{logit}}(x)\equiv\log 3.5\), with conditional odds ratio \(3.5\) and no effect modification on the logit scale.

The marginal risks are the stratum averages, \[ \mathbb E[Y(0)]=\tfrac12(0.3+0.6)=0.45,\qquad \mathbb E[Y(1)]=\tfrac12(0.6+0.84)=0.72, \] and the marginal log odds ratio is \[ \psi_{\mathrm{logit}}^{\mathrm M}=\mathrm{logit}(0.72)-\mathrm{logit}(0.45) =\log\frac{0.72/0.28}{0.45/0.55}=\log\frac{2.571429\ldots}{0.818182\ldots}=\log 3.142857\ldots=1.14513\ldots . \] The marginal odds ratio is \(\tfrac{22}{7}=3.142857\ldots\), strictly less than the conditional odds ratio \(3.5\). Therefore \[ \psi_{\mathrm{logit}}^{\mathrm M}=1.14513\ldots\;\neq\;1.25276\ldots=\psi_{\mathrm{logit}}, \] so the odds ratio is non-collapsible, proving the negative half of Definition 7.7. The marginal odds ratio is attenuated toward the null value \(1\) relative to the conditional odds ratio \(3.5\). This direction is not an accident of the chosen numbers: whenever the conditional odds ratio exceeds \(1\) and the baseline risks genuinely differ across strata, the marginal odds ratio falls strictly between \(1\) and the conditional value, so it is always pulled toward the null. The general statement, and its proof from the convexity of the logit (Jensen’s inequality, Theorem 4.6), are the content of the starred Exercise 7.12; the mechanism is unpacked in the remark just below. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (Non-collapsibility is not confounding). The example is engineered so that there is no confounding: we specified the potential-outcome stratum means \(\mu_t(x)\) directly and used \(\mathbb E[Y(t)]=\mathbb E_X[\mu_t(X)]\) to form the marginal risks, as would hold in a randomized trial where \(X\) is a pure prognostic factor balanced across arms. No backdoor path, no omitted common cause, and yet the marginal and conditional odds ratios differ. The mechanism is Jensen’s inequality (Theorem 4.6): the marginal effect applies the nonlinear logit to the average risk, \(g(\mathbb E_X[\mu_t(X)])\), whereas the constant conditional effect lives on the average of the logits; for a nonaffine \(g\) these disagree, \[ g\bigl(\mathbb E_X[\mu_t(X)]\bigr)\neq\mathbb E_X\bigl[g(\mu_t(X))\bigr], \] and the two halves of the contrast fail to cancel. The risk difference and risk ratio escape this fate because, as Proposition 7.4 shows, their links interact linearly with the averaging. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.7.3 The hazard ratio, and why this seeds later chapters

The same mechanism afflicts the hazard ratio of survival analysis. A precise treatment requires the survival function and the proportional-hazards model, which we develop later, but the statement and its cause can be recorded now. If the cause-specific hazard under treatment \(t\) in stratum \(x\) is \(h_t(x)\) with a constant conditional hazard ratio \(h_1(x)/h_0(x)\equiv r\), the marginal hazard ratio, formed from the population-averaged survival functions \(\bar S_t(u)=\mathbb E_X[\exp(-\int_0^u h_t(x)\,ds)]\), is in general not equal to \(r\) and moreover drifts with time \(u\). The reason is again a nonlinear averaging: survival is an exponential functional of the integrated hazard, and the population average of an exponential is not the exponential of the average. Concretely, writing the integrated hazard \(H_t(x,u)=\int_0^u h_t(x)\,ds\), Jensen’s inequality (Theorem 4.6) applied to the strictly convex map \(v\mapsto e^{-v}\) gives \[ \bar S_t(u)=\mathbb E_X\!\bigl[e^{-H_t(X,u)}\bigr]\;>\;e^{-\mathbb E_X[H_t(X,u)]}, \] so the population survival curve is not the survival curve of the average patient, and the marginal hazard \(-\tfrac{d}{du}\log\bar S_t(u)\) it induces is not \(r\) times any baseline hazard. This subsection is only a conceptual preview: the survival function, the integrated hazard, and the proportional-hazards model are developed in later chapters, and the full non-collapsibility proof for the hazard ratio is deferred to that machinery (flagged again in Section 7.9). Thus the hazard ratio, like the odds ratio, is non-collapsible; this is exactly why the cloglog GLM of Section 7.4.3, which encodes a conditional proportional-hazards model, cannot be read marginally without care.

Remark (Consequences threaded through the book). This single section seeds three later developments. In Chapter 11 collapsibility and effect modification are separated as the two distinct reasons a stratified and a pooled analysis can disagree, with the odds ratio’s non-collapsibility as the running example. In Chapter 17 the choice between a conditional and a marginal estimand becomes the central design decision of a population-adjusted indirect comparison, precisely because on the non-collapsible logit and log-hazard scales the two estimands differ even under perfect adjustment. In Chapter 19 the simulated treatment comparison is shown to have two flavors, a conditional and a marginal version, that disagree by exactly the gap exhibited in Example 7.5, which is why a naive plug-in of covariate means into a fitted logistic model targets the wrong estimand. The network meta-analysis of Dias et al. (2018) sidesteps the issue by combining evidence on the conditional log-odds scale and never marginalizing, and multilevel network meta-regression resolves it by integrating the inverse link over the covariate distribution rather than evaluating it at the mean. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

7.8 A fully worked example: logistic regression by hand

We carry out a complete logistic regression on a small grouped data set, computing the maximum likelihood estimate in closed form, executing one iteration of IRLS by hand to see the machinery move, and testing the slope with an analysis of deviance. Every number can be reproduced with a calculator.

The data. Two groups defined by a binary covariate \(x\in\{0,1\}\), each of \(n=10\) trials, with observed successes \[ x_1=0:\;\; R_1=2 \text{ of } n_1=10,\qquad x_2=1:\;\; R_2=7 \text{ of } n_2=10, \] so the observed proportions are \(y_1=0.2\) and \(y_2=0.7\). The model is logistic with linear predictor \(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), design matrix and prior weights \[ \mathbf X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad w_1=w_2=10,\qquad\phi=1. \]

Closed-form maximum likelihood estimate. The model has two parameters and two groups, so it is saturated: the score equations Equation 7.13 force \(\hat p_i=y_i\), the observed proportions. Hence \[ \hat\beta_0=\mathrm{logit}(0.2)=\log\frac{0.2}{0.8}=\log 0.25=-1.38629, \] \[ \hat\beta_1=\mathrm{logit}(0.7)-\mathrm{logit}(0.2)=\log\frac{0.7/0.3}{0.2/0.8}=\log\frac{2.33333}{0.25}=\log 9.33333=2.23359. \] The fitted odds ratio for the covariate is \(e^{\hat\beta_1}=9.3333=\tfrac{(7/3)}{(2/8)}\), the cross-product ratio of the two-by-two table, as it must be for a saturated logistic model.

One iteration of IRLS from \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(0)}=(0,0)^\top\). At the start the linear predictors are \(\eta_i^{(0)}=0\), so \(p_i^{(0)}=\tfrac12\) for both groups, and the logit-link quantities are \(d\mu/d\eta=p(1-p)=0.25\) and \(g'(\mu)=1/(p(1-p))=4\). The working response Equation 7.17 and the working weights Equation 7.14 are \[ z_i^{(0)}=\eta_i^{(0)}+\frac{y_i-p_i^{(0)}}{p_i^{(0)}(1-p_i^{(0)})}, \qquad W_i^{(0)}=n_i\,p_i^{(0)}(1-p_i^{(0)}). \] Group 1: \(z_1=0+\dfrac{0.2-0.5}{0.25}=-1.2\), \(\;W_1=10(0.25)=2.5\). Group 2: \(z_2=0+\dfrac{0.7-0.5}{0.25}=0.8\), \(\;W_2=10(0.25)=2.5\). The weighted least squares step Equation 7.16 solves \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X\,\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf z\) with \[ \mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X=\begin{pmatrix}W_1+W_2&W_2\\W_2&W_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5&2.5\\2.5&2.5\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf z=\begin{pmatrix}W_1z_1+W_2z_2\\W_2z_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1.0\\2.0\end{pmatrix}. \] Subtracting the second equation from the first eliminates \(\beta_1\): \(2.5\,\beta_0=-1.0-2.0=-3.0\), so \(\beta_0^{(1)}=-1.2\); back-substituting, \(2.5(-1.2)+2.5\,\beta_1=2.0\) gives \(\beta_1^{(1)}=2.0\). Thus \[ \boldsymbol\beta^{(1)}=(-1.2,\;2.0)^\top. \] One step has moved the estimate from \((0,0)\) a large fraction of the way to the maximum likelihood value \((-1.386,\,2.234)\). A second IRLS iteration, evaluated at \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(1)}\) where \(p_1^{(1)}=1/(1+e^{1.2})=0.2315\) and \(p_2^{(1)}=1/(1+e^{-0.8})=0.6900\), yields \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(2)}=(-1.377,\,2.224)^\top\), and the iterates converge to the closed-form estimate above. This illustrates Theorem 7.3 concretely: each step is an ordinary weighted least squares fit of the synthetic response \(\mathbf z\) on \(\mathbf X\).

Testing the slope by analysis of deviance. The fitted (saturated) two-parameter model has residual deviance \(D_1=0\) by Proposition 7.3, since \(\hat p_i=y_i\). Compare it to the null, intercept-only model \(\mathrm{logit}(p)=\beta_0\), whose maximum likelihood estimate is the pooled proportion \(\hat p=(R_1+R_2)/(n_1+n_2)=9/20=0.45\), giving fitted counts \(\hat R_i=n_i\hat p=4.5\) in each group. Its residual deviance from the binomial formula of Example 7.4 is \[ D_0=2\sum_{i=1}^2\Bigl[R_i\log\frac{R_i}{\hat R_i}+(n_i-R_i)\log\frac{n_i-R_i}{n_i-\hat R_i}\Bigr]. \] Group 1 (\(R_1=2\)): \(2\log\frac{2}{4.5}+8\log\frac{8}{5.5}=2(-0.81093)+8(0.37469)=-1.62186+2.99753=1.37567\). Group 2 (\(R_2=7\)): \(7\log\frac{7}{4.5}+3\log\frac{3}{5.5}=7(0.44183)+3(-0.60614)=3.09282-1.81841=1.27441\). Summing and doubling, \(D_0=2(1.37567+1.27441)=2(2.65008)=5.30016\).

The analysis-of-deviance statistic for testing \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) is, by Theorem 7.4 with \(\phi=1\), \[ D_0-D_1=5.30016-0=5.300\;\;\text{on}\;\;p_1-p_0=1\;\text{degree of freedom}. \] Referred to the \(\chi^2_1\) distribution, whose \(95\%\) quantile is \(3.841\), the observed value \(5.300\) exceeds the critical value, so \(H_0\) is rejected at the \(5\%\) level; the upper-tail probability is \(P(\chi^2_1>5.300)=0.0213\). The covariate is associated with the outcome, with estimated odds ratio \(e^{2.234}=9.33\). This is the complete logistic-regression workflow, estimate, algorithm, and test, carried out by hand.

7.9 Notes and references

The exponential dispersion family and the unifying GLM framework are due to McCullagh and Nelder (1989), whose Chapters 2 and 4 contain the mean-variance identity Theorem 7.1, the score equations Theorem 7.2, the iteratively reweighted least squares algorithm Theorem 7.3, and the deviance Definition 7.6; our development gives full proofs of statements that the monograph often presents as calculations. The cumulant-generating-function proof of Theorem 7.1 follows the exponential family tradition and uses the moment results of Chapter 4 (Definition 4.7, Theorem 4.10). The asymptotic normality of the estimator, Corollary 7.2, is the specialization to the GLM of the general maximum likelihood theory of Chapter 5 (Theorem 5.6, Definition 5.7, Theorem 5.4); the analysis-of-deviance test Theorem 7.4 rests on Wilks’s theorem (Theorem 5.9). The IRLS connection to weighted least squares ties the chapter back to the normal equations of Chapter 6 (Theorem 6.1) and the projection geometry of Definition 6.3.

The treatment of GLMs as the outcome models of indirect comparison, with the linear predictor written as a study baseline plus relative treatment effects on the link scale, is the framework of Dias et al. (2018), whose Chapter 4 develops logistic, Poisson, and other GLM likelihoods for trial data and combines them on the conditional log-odds and log-rate scales. The non-collapsibility of the odds ratio (Example 7.5) is a classical fact of categorical data analysis; the numerical example here is the one used in the companion proof catalogue (catalogue result B.13). The collapsibility of the mean-difference (identity-link) effect, Proposition 7.4, has a machine-checked counterpart in the Coq development at theories/STC.v, Lemma STC_collapse_linear, which proves marginal equals conditional for linear models from linearity of expectation alone; the proof there isolates exactly the property that the nonlinear logit lacks. The non-collapsibility of the hazard ratio is stated here and proved in full once the survival machinery is available; this is the one result of the chapter left as a forward reference rather than proved in place.
