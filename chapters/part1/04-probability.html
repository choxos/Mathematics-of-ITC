4  Probability and Distributions

4.1 Why probability comes first

Every claim made in an indirect treatment comparison is, at bottom, a claim about a probability distribution. When we say that the effect of a drug measured in one trial population also describes what would happen in a different population, we are asserting a relationship between two joint distributions of covariates and outcomes. When we say that a weighted average of patient-level predictions reproduces a population mean, we are computing an expectation. When we warn that a marginal odds ratio from one population does not transport to another, we are invoking a property of nonlinear transformations of expectations. None of these statements can be made precise, let alone proved, without the language of measure-theoretic probability. This chapter builds that language from the ground up.

Three tools recur so often in later chapters that they deserve to be flagged at the outset. The first is the expectation operator together with its conditional version and the tower property; almost every identification and adjustment result in the book is, in its skeleton, an application of the identity \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]]\). The second is Jensen’s inequality, which is the reason a nonlinear function of an average differs from the average of the nonlinear function; this single fact is the source of aggregation bias in meta-regression, of the non-collapsibility of the odds ratio and the hazard ratio, and of the scale mismatches that make marginal effects hard to transport. The third is the multivariate normal distribution, which is both the exact sampling model behind the normal-theory likelihoods used throughout network meta-analysis and the approximate model that the delta method and the central limit theorem deliver for almost any estimator. We develop all three here, with full proofs.

We follow the measure-theoretic framework of Kolmogorov. Readers with graduate probability may skim to the boxed statements, but should read Theorem 4.6, Theorem 4.7, and the multivariate normal results Theorem 4.14 and Theorem 4.15 in full, since later chapters cite specific steps of those proofs. The causal motivation throughout is drawn from Hernán and Robins (2020); the measure theory is standard and we cite Billingsley (1995) and Williams (1991) for the results we use without proof. The material of this chapter corresponds to results A.1 through A.10 of the proof catalogue.

A reader new to measure theory should not try to absorb every proof on a first pass. Read the motivating prose, the definitions, the reader translation that follows each definition, and the why this matters note that follows each major theorem; treat the heaviest proofs, the graphoid axioms Theorem 4.5 and the differentiation of the moment generating function Theorem 4.10, as optional on a first reading and return to them once the running example below feels familiar. The symbols used here are collected in the Notation chapter.

4.2 A running clinical-trial example

Every abstract object in this chapter is a precise way of talking about a concrete quantity in a trial. To keep that concreteness in view, we fix one small example now and return to it at each transition.

Example 4.1 (A toy two-arm trial) A randomized trial compares an active drug (\(T=1\)) against control (\(T=0\)). Each enrolled patient has a baseline severity \(X\in\{0,1\}\), with \(0\) meaning low risk and \(1\) meaning high risk, and a binary response \(Y\in\{0,1\}\), with \(1\) meaning the patient responded. A second trial of the same drug, run in a sicker population, is labeled by a study indicator \(S\in\{1,2\}\). Suppose the response risks within each severity stratum are those in the table.

severity \(X\) share of patients risk under control \(P(Y=1\mid X,T=0)\) risk under active \(P(Y=1\mid X,T=1)\)
low (\(X=0\)) \(0.6\) \(0.20\) \(0.50\)
high (\(X=1\)) \(0.4\) \(0.50\) \(0.90\)

These numbers are all we need. We will use them to read off a probability space (Section 4.3), random variables and their laws (Section 4.4), an expectation (Section 4.5), a conditional expectation and the tower property (Section 4.6), independence and conditional independence (Section 4.7), and the Jensen gap behind non-collapsibility (Section 4.8.1). The potential-outcome symbols \(Y(0),Y(1)\) and the formal treatment-effect machinery are built in Chapter 9; here they serve only as motivation.

Two probability spaces, not one. It is worth deciding at the outset what “an outcome” is, because the indirect-comparison literature quietly uses two different choices. In a patient-level probability space, one outcome \(\omega\) is one patient’s full record \((X,T,Y)\), and an event is a set of patients, for example “high-risk patients who responded.” In a study-level probability space, one outcome is an entire trial dataset or its aggregate summary, and an event is a set of possible datasets, for example “trials in which the observed response rate exceeds \(0.5\).” Both are legitimate; the table above lives in the patient-level space, while a sampling statement about an estimated response rate lives in the study-level space. Whenever the distinction matters we name the space explicitly.

4.3 Probability spaces and \(\sigma\)-algebras

The starting point is a triple that encodes a source of randomness, the collection of questions we are allowed to ask of it, and the answer to each question expressed as a probability.

Definition 4.1 (Probability space) A probability space is a triple \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) in which:

  1. \(\Omega\) is a nonempty set, the sample space, whose elements \(\omega\) are outcomes;
  2. \(\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{\Omega}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega\), meaning that
    1. \(\Omega\in\mathcal{F}\); (ii) \(A\in\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow A^{c}:=\Omega\setminus A\in\mathcal{F}\); and (iii) for every sequence \(\{A_n\}_{n\ge 1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\), \(\bigcup_{n\ge 1}A_n\in\mathcal{F}\). The elements of \(\mathcal{F}\) are called events;
  3. \(P:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]\) is a probability measure: \(P(\Omega)=1\), and for every sequence of pairwise disjoint events \(\{A_n\}_{n\ge 1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\), \[ P\!\left(\bigsqcup_{n\ge 1}A_n\right)=\sum_{n\ge 1}P(A_n). \tag{4.1}\] The property Equation 4.1 is called countable additivity.

Remark. Reader translation. A probability space is the trial bookkeeping made exact: \(\Omega\) is the list of things that can happen (one entry per patient, in the patient-level reading of Example 4.1), \(\mathcal{F}\) is the list of questions we are allowed to ask, and \(P\) assigns each question a number between \(0\) and \(1\). The disjoint-union symbol \(\bigsqcup\) in Equation 4.1 is an ordinary union with a reminder attached: the events do not overlap, so their probabilities simply add.

In a clinical-trial reading of the definition, an outcome \(\omega\) is the entire record of one patient: baseline covariates, assigned treatment, and follow-up response. The event “the patient survives beyond five years” is a subset of \(\Omega\); if it lies in \(\mathcal{F}\) we may speak of its probability. The requirement that \(\mathcal{F}\) be closed under countable unions, not merely finite ones, is exactly what separates a \(\sigma\)-algebra from a mere algebra of sets, and it is what makes limiting operations, on which all of analysis depends, available inside the theory. A concrete instance: the event “the patient relapses at some point during follow-up” is the union over all times \(n=1,2,\dots\) of the events “relapses by time \(n\),” a genuinely countable union; without closure under such unions we could not even assign it a probability.

The defining axioms list only a few closure properties. The remaining ones we need follow from them.

Lemma 4.1 (Closure properties of a \(\sigma\)-algebra) Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega\) and \(\{A_n\}_{n\ge 1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\). Then \(\varnothing\in\mathcal{F}\), and \(\mathcal{F}\) is closed under countable intersections \(\bigcap_n A_n\), under finite differences \(A\setminus B\), and under the set limits \(\limsup_n A_n:=\bigcap_{m\ge 1}\bigcup_{n\ge m}A_n\) and \(\liminf_n A_n:=\bigcup_{m\ge 1}\bigcap_{n\ge m}A_n\).

Remark. Reader translation. The two set limits read as tail events. The expression \(\bigcap_{m\ge 1}\bigcup_{n\ge m}A_n\) unpacks as “for every starting time \(m\), at least one \(A_n\) with \(n\ge m\) occurs,” which is exactly the event that \(A_n\) occurs for infinitely many \(n\); this is \(\limsup_n A_n\). Dually, \(\liminf_n A_n\) is the event that \(A_n\) occurs for all but finitely many \(n\). The lemma simply says a \(\sigma\)-algebra is closed under all the everyday set operations, so every such event has a probability; Exercise 4.5 puts this to work.

Proof. Since \(\Omega\in\mathcal{F}\), axiom (ii) gives \(\varnothing=\Omega^{c}\in\mathcal{F}\). For countable intersections, axiom (ii) gives \(A_n^{c}\in\mathcal{F}\), axiom (iii) gives \(\bigcup_n A_n^{c}\in\mathcal{F}\), and axiom (ii) once more gives \(\bigl(\bigcup_n A_n^{c}\bigr)^{c}=\bigcap_n A_n\in\mathcal{F}\) by De Morgan’s law (the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements). For finite differences, \(A\setminus B=A\cap B^{c}\) is the intersection of two events. For the set limits, \(\bigcup_{n\ge m}A_n\in\mathcal{F}\) for each \(m\) by axiom (iii), and the countable intersection over \(m\) lies in \(\mathcal{F}\) by what we have just shown; the argument for \(\liminf\) is the mirror image. \(\square\)

This is result A.1 of the proof catalogue. We will not construct probability measures by hand. The existence of measures with prescribed values on a generating class, in particular Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) and the measures induced by distribution functions, is guaranteed by the Carathéodory extension theorem; see Billingsley (1995), §3. We take that machinery as given.

4.4 Random variables and their distributions

A probability space is rarely interesting on its own. What we observe are numerical summaries of an outcome: a covariate, a treatment indicator, a survival time. These are functions on \(\Omega\), and to assign probabilities to statements about their values we require that the preimage of every reasonable set of values be an event.

Throughout, \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\) denotes the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\), the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing all open sets. Its existence follows from the fact that an arbitrary intersection of \(\sigma\)-algebras is again a \(\sigma\)-algebra, so one may intersect all \(\sigma\)-algebras containing the open sets.

Definition 4.2 (Random variable and its law) Let \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) be a probability space and \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}')\) a measurable space (a set \(\mathcal{S}\) paired with a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{S}'\) of its subsets; the prime is merely a label distinguishing the \(\sigma\)-algebra from the set, not a derivative). A function \(X:\Omega\to\mathcal{S}\) is measurable, and is called a random variable when \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}')=(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\) and a random vector when \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}')=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\), if \[ X^{-1}(B):=\{\omega\in\Omega: X(\omega)\in B\}\in\mathcal{F}\qquad\text{for every }B\in\mathcal{S}'. \] The law (or distribution) of \(X\) is the set function \(P_X\) on \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}')\) defined by the pushforward \[ P_X(B):=P\bigl(X^{-1}(B)\bigr),\qquad B\in\mathcal{S}'. \tag{4.2}\]

Remark. Reader translation. A random variable is a measurement: it reads a number off each outcome. Measurability is the modest demand that every question about that number (“is severity high?”, “is the response time under five years?”) be a legitimate event, so it has a probability. The law \(P_X\) is what remains after forgetting the patient and keeping only how often \(X\) lands in each value-set; the pushforward Equation 4.2 is the recipe for it, pushing each value-set \(B\) back through \(X\) to a patient event \(X^{-1}(B)\) and taking its probability. From the change-of-variables identity Equation 4.3 onward we work entirely with the law and never mention \(\Omega\) again.

Proposition 4.1 (The law is a probability measure) For a random variable \(X\), the pushforward \(P_X\) of Equation 4.2 is a probability measure on \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}')\).

Proof. Measurability of \(X\) guarantees \(X^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{F}\), so \(P_X(B)\) is defined. First, \(P_X(\mathcal{S})=P(X^{-1}(\mathcal{S}))=P(\Omega)=1\), because every \(\omega\) is mapped somewhere. Nonnegativity is inherited from \(P\). For countable additivity, let \(\{B_n\}\) be pairwise disjoint in \(\mathcal{S}'\). Preimages preserve disjointness and distribute over unions, \(X^{-1}\bigl(\bigsqcup_n B_n\bigr)=\bigsqcup_n X^{-1}(B_n)\), so by countable additivity of \(P\), \[ P_X\!\left(\bigsqcup_n B_n\right) =P\!\left(\bigsqcup_n X^{-1}(B_n)\right) =\sum_n P\bigl(X^{-1}(B_n)\bigr) =\sum_n P_X(B_n).\qquad\square \]

This is result A.2. The law \(P_X\) is the object we actually reason about; the underlying \(\Omega\) recedes into the background. A random variable \(X\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) is summarized by its cumulative distribution function (CDF) \[ F_X(x):=P_X\bigl((-\infty,x]\bigr)=P(X\le x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}. \] Every CDF is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and satisfies \(F_X(-\infty)=0\), \(F_X(+\infty)=1\), and conversely every function with these properties is the CDF of a unique law on \((\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\); see Billingsley (1995), §14. We write \(F_X^{-1}\) for the generalized inverse (quantile function) \(F_X^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F_X(x)\ge u\}\), the value below which a fraction \(u\) of the distribution lies, the population version of a sample quantile; it returns in Part V to generate correlated covariates by the inverse-CDF method. The standard normal CDF and density are written \(\Phi\) and \(\phi\) throughout, following the notation chapter.

The practical dividend of the pushforward is the change-of-variables identity for expectations, which lets us integrate over the values of \(X\) rather than over \(\Omega\): \[ \mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\int_{\Omega}h(X(\omega))\,dP(\omega)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}h(s)\,dP_X(s), \tag{4.3}\] valid for every measurable \(h\) for which either side is defined. In words, to average any function of \(X\) we integrate over the values of \(X\) weighted by its law \(P_X\); the abstract sample space has done its job and disappears, which is why every later computation is phrased in terms of laws and densities rather than \(\Omega\).

The words introduced so far are easy to conflate. They line up as follows.

object what it is type trial reading
event a set of outcomes subset of \(\Omega\) “high risk and responded”
random variable \(X\) a measurement on outcomes function \(\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\) severity, treatment, or response
law / distribution \(P_X\) how often \(X\) hits each value-set measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) \(P(X=1)=0.4\)
CDF \(F_X\) the law accumulated up to \(x\) function \(\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\) \(F_X(0)=P(X\le 0)=0.6\)
density / mass \(f_X\) the law per unit value function on \(\mathbb{R}\) \(f_X(1)=0.4,\ f_X(0)=0.6\)
expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) the law’s center of mass a single number mean severity \(\mathbb{E}[X]=0.4\)

The first three describe where the randomness lives; the last three are summaries of the law. A density exists only when the law is smooth enough (the continuous case) or sits on isolated points (the discrete case, where it is a mass function), but the CDF and the expectation always exist. We prove the meaning of these integrals next.

4.5 Expectation as an integral

The expectation of a random variable \(X\) is its Lebesgue integral against \(P\), \(\mathbb{E}[X]:=\int_{\Omega}X\,dP\), defined in the usual three stages. For a nonnegative simple random variable \(X=\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_k\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\) with \(c_k\ge 0\) and \(A_k\in\mathcal{F}\) (here the indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{A}\) equals \(1\) on the event \(A\) and \(0\) off it; we also write \(\mathbf{1}\{X=x\}\) for the indicator of the event \(\{X=x\}\)), one sets \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_k P(A_k)\). For a general nonnegative measurable \(X\), one sets \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\sup\{\mathbb{E}[S]: 0\le S\le X,\ S\text{ simple}\}\in[0,\infty]\). For a general \(X\), one writes \(X=X^{+}-X^{-}\) with \(X^{\pm}=\max(\pm X,0)\) and sets \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[X^{-}]\) whenever at least one term is finite; \(X\) is integrable, written \(X\in L^1\) (the space of integrable random variables), when \(\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty\). The construction and the elementary properties (monotonicity, and additivity on simple functions) are standard; see Billingsley (1995), §15.

Remark. Reader translation. Expectation is a weighted average, and the three-stage construction only makes that idea survive when the averaging has infinitely many pieces. For a discrete measurement it is the familiar sum \(\sum_x x\,P(X=x)\): in Example 4.1 the severity \(X\) is \(\mathrm{Ber}(0.4)\), so \(\mathbb{E}[X]=0\cdot 0.6+1\cdot 0.4=0.4\). For a continuous measurement the sum becomes the integral \(\int x\,f_X(x)\,dx\). The simple-then-nonnegative-then-general staircase is precisely what guarantees these are one notion, so the single symbol \(\mathbb{E}\) covers a finite trial average, a discrete mean, and a continuous integral without contradiction, and continues to work for unbounded outcomes.

Two limit theorems govern the interchange of limits with integration and are cited, not reproved, here; they are the analytic backbone of every later argument that passes a limit inside an expectation. They constitute result A.3.

Theorem 4.1 (Monotone convergence (cited)) If \(0\le X_n\uparrow X\) almost surely (the arrow \(\uparrow\) denotes that \(X_n\) increases to its limit, and almost surely, abbreviated a.s., means with probability one, that is, outside a set of outcomes of probability zero), that is, \(X_n(\omega)\) is nondecreasing in \(n\) with limit \(X(\omega)\) for \(P\)-almost every \(\omega\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X_n]\uparrow\mathbb{E}[X]\).

Theorem 4.2 (Dominated convergence (cited)) If \(X_n\to X\) almost surely and there is an integrable \(Y\) with \(|X_n|\le Y\) almost surely for all \(n\), then \(X\) is integrable and \(\mathbb{E}[X_n]\to\mathbb{E}[X]\); moreover \(\mathbb{E}|X_n-X|\to 0\).

Proofs are in Billingsley (1995), §16, the second deduced from Fatou’s lemma. We will reference these as Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2. The first result we prove from scratch is the linearity of expectation, the most heavily used identity in the book.

Theorem 4.3 (Linearity of expectation) Let \(X,Y\in L^1\) and \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\). Then \(aX+bY\in L^1\) and \[ \mathbb{E}[aX+bY]=a\,\mathbb{E}[X]+b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]. \]

Proof. Integrability of \(aX+bY\) follows from the triangle inequality \(|aX+bY|\le|a|\,|X|+|b|\,|Y|\) and monotonicity of the integral. For the identity we proceed through the construction. For nonnegative simple functions \(S=\sum_k c_k\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\) and \(T=\sum_\ell d_\ell\mathbf{1}_{B_\ell}\), refining to the common partition generated by \(\{A_k\}\) and \(\{B_\ell\}\) shows \(\mathbb{E}[\alpha S+\beta T]=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[S]+\beta\,\mathbb{E}[T]\) for \(\alpha,\beta\ge 0\), since on each atom of the refinement the integrand is a constant and \(P\) is additive over the atoms. For nonnegative measurable \(X,Y\), take simple \(0\le S_n\uparrow X\) and \(0\le T_n\uparrow Y\) (such sequences exist by the standard dyadic approximation: round \(X\) down to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-n}\) and cap at height \(n\), giving simple functions that increase pointwise to \(X\)). Then \(\alpha S_n+\beta T_n\uparrow \alpha X+\beta Y\), and Theorem 4.1 gives \[ \mathbb{E}[\alpha X+\beta Y]=\lim_n\mathbb{E}[\alpha S_n+\beta T_n] =\alpha\lim_n\mathbb{E}[S_n]+\beta\lim_n\mathbb{E}[T_n]=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[X]+\beta\,\mathbb{E}[Y] \] for \(\alpha,\beta\ge 0\). Finally, for general integrable \(X,Y\) and real \(a,b\), decompose each variable into positive and negative parts and each coefficient into sign and magnitude, and combine the nonnegative case with the additive splitting \(\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}[Z^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[Z^{-}]\); every term is finite by integrability, so the rearrangement is legitimate. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. Linearity is the workhorse behind every weighted estimator in the book. An inverse probability weighted mean, a matching-adjusted average, and a network meta-analysis contrast are all linear combinations of responses, and linearity is what lets us pass expectations through them. We prove it from the construction, rather than assume it, because the unbiasedness arguments of Part IV and the limit theorems we later cite all rest on this identity holding exactly, not approximately.

Linearity is half of result A.4; the tower property is the other half and is proved in Section 4.6 after we have conditional expectation in hand. We also record the second-moment summaries that organize all variance calculations to come.

Definition 4.3 (Variance, covariance, and the covariance matrix) For \(X,Y\in L^2\), that is, \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]<\infty\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Y^2]<\infty\), define \[ \mathrm{Var}(X):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2\bigr],\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X-\mathbb{E}X)(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)\bigr]. \] For a random vector \(\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_p)^{\top}\) with each \(X_i\in L^2\) and mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]\) (taken componentwise), the covariance matrix is the \(p\times p\) matrix \[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}:=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}) :=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\bigr], \qquad \Sigma_{ij}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j). \]

Remark. Reader translation. Variance is the average squared distance of a measurement from its mean, a single number for spread; covariance is the signed version for a pair, positive when the two move together. The covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) packages all pairwise covariances of a random vector, and the identity Equation 4.4 below says the spread of any linear summary \(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is read off \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) as a quadratic form. This is the hinge between probability and the matrix algebra of Chapter 3.

By linearity, \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}\) is symmetric and bilinear. A computation we will reuse: for a fixed vector \(\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^p\), \[ \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) =\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu}))^2\bigr] =\mathbf{a}^{\top}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\bigr]\,\mathbf{a} =\mathbf{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{a}, \tag{4.4}\] which expresses the variance of a linear combination as a quadratic form in the covariance matrix. This identity is the bridge to Section 4.9.

4.6 Conditional expectation and the tower property

For a discrete random variable \(X\) with \(P(X=x)>0\) one defines the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]\) as the ratio \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}\{X=x\}]/P(X=x)\), the average response among the patients with \(X=x\). This is the everyday notion of a subgroup mean. The difficulty is that for a continuous \(X\) every single value has probability zero, \(P(X=x)=0\), so the ratio becomes the meaningless \(0/0\) and the elementary definition collapses. The measure-theoretic definition repairs this not by rescuing the ratio but by characterizing the whole function \(x\mapsto\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]\) at once, through an averaging identity that never evaluates a probability-zero event. It reduces to the familiar subgroup mean whenever the subgroups have positive probability, as we verify in Example 4.2 below.

Definition 4.4 (Conditional expectation) Let \(Y\in L^1\) on \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) and let \(\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\) be a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra. A conditional expectation of \(Y\) given \(\mathcal{G}\) is any \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable \(Z\) with \(Z\in L^1\) satisfying the defining property \[ \mathbb{E}[Z\,\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\mathbf{1}_A]\qquad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{G}. \tag{4.5}\] Any two such \(Z\) agree almost surely; we fix one version and write \(Z=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\). When \(\mathcal{G}=\sigma(X)\) is the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by a random variable or vector \(X\), we write \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\), and there is a measurable function \(m\) with \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=m(X)\) almost surely; the value \(m(x)\) is what we mean by \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]\).

Remark. Reader translation. Think of \(\mathcal{G}\) as the information you are allowed to use, and \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\) as your best guess of \(Y\) given only that information. The defining property Equation 4.5 says the guess must get the totals right: on every event \(A\) you are allowed to see, the guess and the truth average to the same thing. Two guesses that always agree on those events agree almost surely, which is why we speak of a version and then fix one. When the information is “the value of \(X\),” written \(\mathcal{G}=\sigma(X)\), the guess is a function of \(X\) and its value at \(x\) is the subgroup mean \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]\). Two sanity checks: \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]=Y\) when \(Y\) is itself \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable (you already know \(Y\)), and \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is the constant \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) when \(\mathcal{G}\) carries no information about \(Y\).

The existence of a random variable satisfying Equation 4.5 is guaranteed by a deep theorem of measure theory, the Radon-Nikodym theorem. We record the construction for completeness and then never use its internals again; a reader meeting these words for the first time may take existence on faith and lose nothing that follows. Concretely, the set function \(\nu(A):=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\mathbf{1}_A]\) for \(A\in\mathcal{G}\) is a finite signed measure on \((\Omega,\mathcal{G})\), absolutely continuous with respect to the restriction \(P|_{\mathcal{G}}\), so it has a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable density \(Z=d\nu/dP|_{\mathcal{G}}\), which is Equation 4.5; see Williams (1991), §9. Uniqueness up to null sets is immediate: if \(Z,Z'\) both satisfy Equation 4.5, then \(\mathbb{E}[(Z-Z')\mathbf{1}_A]=0\) for all \(A\in\mathcal{G}\); taking \(A=\{Z-Z'>0\}\in\mathcal{G}\) forces \(P(Z-Z'>0)=0\), and likewise for the reverse inequality. This is result A.5. We record the operational properties; the tower property is the one we own and prove in full.

The definition earns its keep by reducing to the elementary subgroup mean. We check this on the running trial, and read off the tower property it will satisfy.

Example 4.2 (Subgroup means in the toy trial) Work on the control arm of Example 4.1, where severity \(X\) has \(P(X=0)=0.6\) and \(P(X=1)=0.4\) and the response risks are \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=0]=0.20\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=1]=0.50\). Take \(\mathcal{G}=\sigma(X)\).

The elementary ratio. Since \(X\) is discrete with positive cell probabilities, \[ \mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=1]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y\,\mathbf{1}\{X=1\}]}{P(X=1)}=\frac{P(Y=1,\,X=1)}{P(X=1)} =\frac{0.4\cdot 0.50}{0.4}=0.50, \] and likewise \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=0]=0.20\). So \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=m(X)\) with \(m(0)=0.20\) and \(m(1)=0.50\).

The defining property. This same \(m(X)\) satisfies Equation 4.5. The nontrivial events of \(\sigma(X)\) are \(\{X=0\}\), \(\{X=1\}\), and their union \(\Omega\); on \(A=\{X=1\}\), \[ \mathbb{E}[m(X)\,\mathbf{1}_A]=0.50\cdot P(X=1)=0.50\cdot 0.4=0.20=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\mathbf{1}_A], \] and similarly on \(\{X=0\}\), so the measure-theoretic guess and the elementary subgroup mean coincide.

The tower property in advance. Averaging the subgroup means against the subgroup sizes recovers the overall mean, \[ \mathbb{E}[Y]=0.6\cdot 0.20+0.4\cdot 0.50=0.12+0.20=0.32, \] which is the law of total expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\bigr]\). This “average the subgroup means and recover the whole” is the identity Theorem 4.4 now proves in general.

Theorem 4.4 (Tower property) Let \(Y\in L^1\) and let \(\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\) be nested sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras. Then \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\in L^1\) and \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\,\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}\,\bigr]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{H}] \quad\text{a.s.}; \tag{4.6}\] in particular, taking \(\mathcal{H}=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}\) the trivial \(\sigma\)-algebra, \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\,\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\,\bigr]=\mathbb{E}[Y]. \tag{4.7}\]

Proof. Write \(Z=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\). Integrability of \(Z\): applying Equation 4.5 with the events \(A^{+}=\{Z>0\}\) and \(A^{-}=\{Z\le 0\}\), both in \(\mathcal{G}\), gives \(\mathbb{E}|Z|=\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{A^{+}}]-\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{A^{-}}] =\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_{A^{+}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_{A^{-}}]\le\mathbb{E}|Y|<\infty\), so \(Z\in L^1\).

Let \(W=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{H}]\), which is \(\mathcal{H}\)-measurable by construction. To show \(W=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{H}]\) it suffices, by uniqueness in Definition 4.4, to verify the defining property \(\mathbb{E}[W\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\) for every \(A\in\mathcal{H}\). Fix such an \(A\). Since \(W=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{H}]\) and \(A\in\mathcal{H}\), the defining property of \(W\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[W\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_A]\). Since \(A\in\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}\), the defining property of \(Z=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\). Chaining the two equalities yields \(\mathbb{E}[W\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\), which is Equation 4.6. Specializing to the trivial \(\mathcal{H}\), whose only events are \(\varnothing\) and \(\Omega\), the random variable \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{H}]\) is the constant \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\), and Equation 4.7 follows by taking expectations on both sides of Equation 4.6, that is, by setting \(A=\Omega\) in \(\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. The tower property is the engine of identification. In a trial we can estimate a conditional mean, the response within each covariate stratum; what a decision maker wants is a marginal mean in some target population. The tower property \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]]\) is precisely the bridge: average the stratum-specific means against the target’s covariate distribution and you obtain the target’s marginal mean, exactly as in Example 4.2. Every standardization, transportability, and population-adjustment formula later in the book, including the core ML-NMR estimand of Part V, is this identity with a particular choice of distribution to average over.

The tower property completes result A.4. We will use the remaining standard properties of conditional expectation, proved in Williams (1991), §9: linearity; taking out what is known (if a factor is already determined by the conditioning information, it slides outside the conditional expectation as a constant), namely \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)Y\mid X]=g(X)\,\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\) for bounded measurable \(g\) (for an indicator \(g=\mathbf{1}_B\) with \(B\in\sigma(X)\) this is immediate from Equation 4.5, and the general case follows by linearity and Theorem 4.1); and the fact that \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]=\mathbb{E}[Y]\) when \(Y\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\). Two immediate corollaries are recorded next.

Corollary 4.1 (Law of total expectation and total probability) For \(Y\in L^1\) and any sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]]\). For an event \(B\) and a countable partition \(\{A_n\}\) of \(\Omega\) into events with \(P(A_n)>0\), \[ P(B)=\sum_n P(B\mid A_n)\,P(A_n),\qquad P(B\mid A_n):=\frac{P(B\cap A_n)}{P(A_n)}. \]

Proof. The first claim is Equation 4.7. For the second, apply linearity and countable additivity to the indicator \(\mathbf{1}_B=\sum_n\mathbf{1}_{B\cap A_n}\): taking expectations, \(P(B)=\sum_n P(B\cap A_n)=\sum_n P(B\mid A_n)P(A_n)\), the last step by the definition of conditional probability. Interchanging expectation and the infinite sum of nonnegative terms is licensed by Theorem 4.1 applied to the partial sums. \(\square\)

This is result A.9. The variance counterpart of the tower property is the decomposition that separates within-group from between-group variation; we will invoke it for mixed-count likelihoods later.

Corollary 4.2 (Law of total variance) For \(Y\in L^2\) and a random vector \(X\), \[ \mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)\bigr]+\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\bigr), \tag{4.8}\] where \(\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X):=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X])^2\mid X]\).

Proof. Write \(m(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\) and decompose \(Y-\mathbb{E}Y=(Y-m(X))+(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y)\). Squaring and taking expectations, \[ \mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-m(X))^2]+2\,\mathbb{E}[(Y-m(X))(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y)]+\mathbb{E}[(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y)^2]. \] The cross term vanishes: conditioning on \(X\) and taking out the \(X\)-measurable factor \(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y\), \[ \mathbb{E}[(Y-m(X))(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y)\mid X]=(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y)\,\mathbb{E}[Y-m(X)\mid X] =(m(X)-\mathbb{E}Y)\cdot 0=0, \] since \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=m(X)\); by Equation 4.7 the unconditional cross term is then \(0\). The first term equals \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[(Y-m(X))^2\mid X]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)]\) by the tower property. The third term is \(\mathrm{Var}(m(X))=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X])\) because \(\mathbb{E}[m(X)]=\mathbb{E}Y\). Summing gives Equation 4.8. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. The law of total variance splits the variability of an outcome into a part within covariate strata and a part between them. For a hospital’s monthly count of deaths it separates within-ward chance fluctuation from genuine between-ward differences in patient risk; the mixed-count likelihoods of Part V use exactly this split to attach the right variance to an aggregate event count, rather than the too-small variance a single binomial would suggest.

4.7 Independence and conditional independence

Independence is the formal statement that knowledge of one variable conveys nothing about another.

Definition 4.5 (Independence) Events \(A,B\in\mathcal{F}\) are independent if \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). Random vectors \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, written \(X\perp Y\), if their joint law is the product of their marginals, equivalently if \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\,\mathbb{E}[g(Y)] \] for all bounded measurable \(f,g\). A family \(\{X_i\}_{i\in I}\) is mutually independent if for every finite subset the joint law factors into the product of marginals.

Remark. Reader translation. Independence means learning one variable tells you nothing about the other, so their probabilities multiply. In Example 4.1, if a fair coin assigned treatment regardless of severity, then \(T\perp X\) and \(P(T=1,\,X=1)=P(T=1)\,P(X=1)=0.5\cdot 0.4=0.20\). The expectation form \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\,\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]\) is the same statement read through test functions: no function of one variable correlates with any function of the other.

Independence implies uncorrelatedness: taking \(f,g\) the identity on bounded variables, or passing to the limit under domination for \(L^2\) variables, gives \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0\). The converse fails in general; Exercise 4.8 supplies a standard counterexample. The conditional version of independence is the relation on which the entire causal-inference apparatus of Part II rests.

Definition 4.6 (Conditional independence) Random vectors \(X\) and \(Y\) are conditionally independent given \(Z\), written \(X\perp Y\mid Z\), if for all bounded measurable \(f,g\), \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)\mid Z]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\,\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\mid Z]\quad\text{a.s.} \tag{4.9}\] Equivalently, the conditional joint law of \((X,Y)\) given \(Z\) factors as the product of the conditional marginal laws.

Remark. Reader translation. Conditional independence says that once you know \(Z\), learning \(Y\) tells you nothing more about \(X\). This is the language of adjustment. The defining assumption of a covariate-adjusted trial is that treatment is assigned without regard to the patient’s potential outcomes once the covariates used in assignment are fixed, written \(T\perp (Y(0),Y(1))\mid X\) and called conditional ignorability in Chapter 9. Reading \(X\) for \(Z\), \(T\) for one variable, and a potential outcome for the other, every adjustment method in Part II is an effort to make a statement of exactly this shape hold.

The relation \(\cdot\perp\cdot\mid\cdot\) obeys a small algebra of inference rules, the graphoid axioms, which is what allows high-dimensional conditional-independence statements to be manipulated symbolically; this manipulation is exactly how the propensity-score reduction of Chapter 10 is justified, starting from the conditional ignorability of Chapter 9.

The five rules below are the algebra of this relation. A first-time reader should take in the plain-language column of the roadmap, read the causal illustration in Example 4.3, and treat the proof itself as optional; later chapters invoke the rules, not the proof technique.

axiom symbolic form plain meaning where it is used
(G1) symmetry \(X\perp Y\mid Z\iff Y\perp X\mid Z\) irrelevance is mutual every manipulation
(G2) decomposition \(X\perp(Y,W)\mid Z\Rightarrow X\perp Y\mid Z\) irrelevant jointly, so irrelevant singly dropping a nuisance variable
(G3) weak union \(X\perp(Y,W)\mid Z\Rightarrow X\perp Y\mid(Z,W)\) revealing more keeps irrelevance conditioning on extra covariates
(G4) contraction \(X\perp Y\mid Z\) and \(X\perp W\mid(Z,Y)\Rightarrow X\perp(Y,W)\mid Z\) combine two irrelevancies into one building up an adjustment set

Example 4.3 (Conditional independence on three binary variables) Let \(X\) be a covariate, \(T\) a treatment, and \(Y\) an outcome, all binary, and suppose treatment is assigned by a coin whose bias depends only on \(X\), so \(P(T=1\mid X,Y)=e(X)\) with \(e(0)=0.3\) and \(e(1)=0.7\). Then \(T\perp Y\mid X\), because given \(X\) the assignment probability does not look at \(Y\): \(P(T=1\mid X,Y)=e(X)=P(T=1\mid X)\). Decomposition (G2) is the statement that if assignment ignores the pair \((Y,W)\) given \(X\), where \(W\) is, say, a downstream marker, then it ignores \(Y\) alone given \(X\): summing the joint factorization over the values of \(W\) leaves the same product form. Contraction (G4) runs the other way: if assignment ignores \(Y\) given \(X\), and also ignores \(W\) given \((X,Y)\), then it ignores the pair \((Y,W)\) given \(X\). These are the moves that let us add or drop variables from an adjustment set without re-deriving everything, and they are exactly what the propensity-score argument of Chapter 10 uses.

Theorem 4.5 (Graphoid axioms) The conditional-independence relation of Definition 4.6 satisfies, for all random vectors \(X,Y,Z,W\):

  • (G1) Symmetry: \(X\perp Y\mid Z\iff Y\perp X\mid Z\).
  • (G2) Decomposition: \(X\perp (Y,W)\mid Z\Rightarrow X\perp Y\mid Z\).
  • (G3) Weak union: \(X\perp (Y,W)\mid Z\Rightarrow X\perp Y\mid (Z,W)\).
  • (G4) Contraction: \(\bigl(X\perp Y\mid Z\bigr)\wedge\bigl(X\perp W\mid (Z,Y)\bigr)\Rightarrow X\perp (Y,W)\mid Z\).
  • (G5) Intersection: if the joint density of \((X,Y,Z,W)\) is strictly positive on its support, then \(\bigl(X\perp Y\mid (Z,W)\bigr)\wedge\bigl(X\perp W\mid (Z,Y)\bigr)\Rightarrow X\perp (Y,W)\mid Z\).

[machine-checked: theories/ConditionalIndep.v, Lemmas cond_indep_symmetry, cond_indep_decomposition; Theorems cond_indep_weak_union, cond_indep_contraction]

Proof. (G1) is immediate, since Equation 4.9 is symmetric under exchanging the pair \((f,X)\) with \((g,Y)\).

(G2) Apply Equation 4.9 with \(g\) depending on the \(Y\)-coordinate alone, that is, take the test function on \((Y,W)\) to be \(g(Y)\cdot 1\). The hypothesis \(X\perp (Y,W)\mid Z\) then reads \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)\mid Z]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\,\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\mid Z]\) for all bounded measurable \(f,g\), which is precisely \(X\perp Y\mid Z\).

(G3) We must show \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)\mid Z,W]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,W]\,\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\mid Z,W]\). The hypothesis \(X\perp(Y,W)\mid Z\) gives, for every bounded measurable \(f\) and every bounded measurable \(h\) of \((Y,W)\), \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)h(Y,W)\mid Z]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\,\mathbb{E}[h(Y,W)\mid Z]. \] Taking \(h(Y,W)=k(W)\) a function of \(W\) alone shows \(X\perp W\mid Z\), whence \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,W]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\) almost surely: indeed, the right side is \((Z,W)\)-measurable, and for any bounded \(k\), \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\,k(W)\mid Z]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\,\mathbb{E}[k(W)\mid Z] =\mathbb{E}[f(X)k(W)\mid Z]\), which by the defining property identifies \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\) as a version of \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,W]\). Now taking \(h(Y,W)=g(Y)k(W)\) in the displayed identity and using taking-out-what-is-known on the \(W\)-measurable factors, \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[k(W)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)\mid Z,W]\,\middle|\,Z\right] =\mathbb{E}\!\left[k(W)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,W]\,\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\mid Z,W]\,\middle|\,Z\right] \] for every bounded \(k\), where on the right we substituted \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,W]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\) and reassembled. Since this holds for all bounded measurable \(k\) of \(W\), the two \((Z,W)\)-measurable integrands agree almost surely, which is the claim.

(G4) Fix bounded measurable \(f,g,k\) acting on \(X,Y,W\) respectively. By taking out what is known and the hypothesis \(X\perp W\mid (Z,Y)\), \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)k(W)\mid Z] =\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(Y)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)k(W)\mid Z,Y]\,\big|\,Z\bigr] =\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(Y)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,Y]\,\mathbb{E}[k(W)\mid Z,Y]\,\big|\,Z\bigr]. \] The hypothesis \(X\perp Y\mid Z\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z,Y]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\), by the same argument as in (G3) with the roles of \(W\) and \(Y\) exchanged. Pulling the now \(Z\)-measurable factor \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\) outside, \[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)k(W)\mid Z] =\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Z]\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(Y)k(W)\,\big|\,Z\bigr], \] where we recognized \(\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\,\mathbb{E}[k(W)\mid Z,Y]\mid Z]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)k(W)\mid Z]\) by the tower property. Since \(f,g,k\) were arbitrary bounded measurable functions, \(X\perp(Y,W)\mid Z\).

(G5) Under strict positivity of the joint density the proof factors the density logarithmically and is given in Hernán and Robins (2020) (technical points) and, in full, in Pearl’s development of graphical models; we cite it rather than reproduce the density bookkeeping. \(\square\)

Remark. Reading the weak-union step (G3). In words, (G3) says that if \(X\) is irrelevant to the pair \((Y,W)\) once \(Z\) is known, then revealing \(W\) as well cannot make \(X\) relevant to \(Y\). The proof first extracts \(X\perp W\mid Z\) (so conditioning on \(W\) does not change \(X\)’s conditional law), substitutes that fact inside the conditional expectation, and matches both sides against every test function of \(W\). Each “by the defining property” step is just the totals-match rule of Definition 4.4 applied to a new event, and “taking out what is known” pulls out the factors already determined by the conditioning variables.

This is result A.6. The first four axioms hold with no further assumption; only intersection requires positivity, which in the indirect-comparison setting is exactly the overlap condition that the target covariate density be positive wherever the source density is.

Remark. Why this matters. These rules are the symbolic engine behind covariate adjustment. The propensity-score reduction of Chapter 10, that adjusting for the scalar propensity score suffices whenever adjusting for the full covariate vector suffices, is a chain of weak union and contraction applied to \(T\perp (Y(0),Y(1))\mid X\). The positivity hypothesis on (G5) is the same overlap condition that population-adjusted indirect comparison requires: the target covariate density must be positive wherever the source density is, or the adjustment is not identified.

4.8 Two fundamental inequalities

We now prove the two inequalities that drive the qualitative phenomena of the rest of the book.

4.8.1 Jensen’s inequality

Recall that \(\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is convex if \(\varphi(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\le\lambda\varphi(x)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(y)\) for all \(x,y\) and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), and strictly convex if the inequality is strict whenever \(x\ne y\) and \(\lambda\in(0,1)\). The geometric content of convexity is that the graph lies above each of its tangent or supporting lines; the following lemma makes that precise and is the engine of the proof.

Lemma 4.2 (Supporting line for a convex function) Let \(\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) be convex and \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). The one-sided derivatives \(\varphi'_{-}(t)\) and \(\varphi'_{+}(t)\) exist with \(\varphi'_{-}(t)\le\varphi'_{+}(t)\), and for every slope \(a\in[\varphi'_{-}(t),\varphi'_{+}(t)]\), \[ \varphi(x)\ge\varphi(t)+a\,(x-t)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}, \tag{4.10}\] with strict inequality for every \(x\ne t\) when \(\varphi\) is strictly convex.

Proof. The slope inequality for convex \(\varphi\) states that for \(s<t<u\), \[ \frac{\varphi(t)-\varphi(s)}{t-s}\le\frac{\varphi(u)-\varphi(s)}{u-s}\le\frac{\varphi(u)-\varphi(t)}{u-t}. \] To see it, write \(t=\lambda s+(1-\lambda)u\) with \(\lambda=\frac{u-t}{u-s}\in(0,1)\), so that \(1-\lambda=\frac{t-s}{u-s}\); convexity gives \(\varphi(t)\le\lambda\varphi(s)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(u)\). Subtracting \(\varphi(s)\) from both sides, \[ \varphi(t)-\varphi(s)\le(1-\lambda)\bigl(\varphi(u)-\varphi(s)\bigr)=\frac{t-s}{u-s}\bigl(\varphi(u)-\varphi(s)\bigr), \] and dividing by the positive length \(t-s\) gives the left inequality. Subtracting \(\varphi(u)\) from both sides instead, \[ \varphi(t)-\varphi(u)\le\lambda\bigl(\varphi(s)-\varphi(u)\bigr)=\frac{u-t}{u-s}\bigl(\varphi(s)-\varphi(u)\bigr), \] and dividing by the positive length \(u-t\), then multiplying through by \(-1\) to flip the sign, gives the right inequality. The inequality shows that the difference quotient \(x\mapsto\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(t)}{x-t}\) is nondecreasing on each side of \(t\) and that every quotient with \(x<t\) is at most every quotient with \(x>t\). Hence the right derivative \(\varphi'_{+}(t)=\inf_{x>t}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(t)}{x-t}\) and the left derivative \(\varphi'_{-}(t)=\sup_{x<t}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(t)}{x-t}\) exist and satisfy \(\varphi'_{-}(t)\le\varphi'_{+}(t)\). For \(a\) in this interval and \(x>t\) we have \(\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(t)}{x-t}\ge\varphi'_{+}(t)\ge a\), which rearranges to Equation 4.10; for \(x<t\) we have \(\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(t)}{x-t}\le\varphi'_{-}(t)\le a\), and multiplying by the negative quantity \(x-t\) reverses the inequality to give Equation 4.10 again. Strict convexity makes every difference quotient strictly monotone, so the inequalities are strict for \(x\ne t\). \(\square\)

Theorem 4.6 (Jensen’s inequality) Let \(\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) be convex and let \(X\in L^1\) with \(\varphi(X)\in L^1\). Then \[ \varphi\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr)\le\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X)\bigr]. \tag{4.11}\] If \(\varphi\) is strictly convex and \(X\) is nondegenerate, that is, not almost surely constant, the inequality is strict.

Proof. Set \(t=\mathbb{E}[X]\) and choose any slope \(a\in[\varphi'_{-}(t),\varphi'_{+}(t)]\). By Lemma 4.2, the supporting-line inequality holds pointwise: \(\varphi(X)\ge\varphi(t)+a\,(X-t)\) almost surely. Both sides are integrable, \(\varphi(X)\) by hypothesis and the right side because \(X\in L^1\), so taking expectations and using linearity, \[ \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]\ge\varphi(t)+a\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]-t\bigr)=\varphi(t)=\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X]), \] because \(\mathbb{E}[X]-t=0\). This is Equation 4.11. If \(\varphi\) is strictly convex and \(X\) is nondegenerate, then Lemma 4.2 gives \(\varphi(X)>\varphi(t)+a(X-t)\) on the event \(\{X\ne t\}\), which has positive probability; the expectation of a random variable that is everywhere at least a given quantity and strictly greater on a set of positive probability is strictly greater, so the inequality in Equation 4.11 is strict. \(\square\)

This is result A.7. The Jensen gap \(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X])\ge 0\) measures the failure of \(\varphi\) to commute with the expectation. The smallest instructive instance is \(\varphi(x)=x^2\), for which the gap is exactly the variance: \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2=\mathrm{Var}(X)\). In later chapters \(\varphi\) will be the inverse link \(g^{-1}\) of a generalized linear model, and the gap will be aggregation bias and non-collapsibility, made concrete in the next example.

Example 4.4 (Aggregation bias on the logit scale) This is the instance of Jensen that drives non-collapsibility. Consider a population in two equally sized severity strata whose response risks are the active-arm risks of Example 4.1, \(p_{\text{low}}=0.5\) and \(p_{\text{high}}=0.9\). On the log-odds (logit) scale the two linear predictors are \[ \eta_{\text{low}}=\mathrm{logit}(0.5)=0,\qquad \eta_{\text{high}}=\mathrm{logit}(0.9)=\ln 9=2\ln 3. \] The true population-average risk is the average of the two risks, \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\eta)\bigr]=\tfrac12(0.5)+\tfrac12(0.9)=0.70. \] The risk you would predict by first averaging on the linear-predictor scale and then transforming back is \[ \mathrm{logit}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\eta]\bigr) =\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\Bigl(\tfrac12\cdot 0+\tfrac12\cdot 2\ln 3\Bigr) =\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\ln 3)=\frac{3}{1+3}=0.75. \] The two disagree, \(0.70\ne 0.75\): the inverse link of the average linear predictor overstates the population risk by five percentage points. Since \(\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\) is concave for \(\eta\ge 0\), Theorem 4.6 predicts the direction, \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\eta)]\le\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[\eta])\). A model fit on the logit scale therefore cannot be collapsed to a population by averaging linear predictors and back-transforming; this gap is the source of the non-collapsibility of the odds ratio studied in Chapter 11.

Remark. Why this matters. Jensen’s inequality is why “the effect in the average patient” and “the average effect over patients” are different numbers whenever the link is nonlinear. Aggregation bias in meta-regression, the non-collapsibility of odds and hazard ratios, and the failure of marginal effects to transport across populations are all the Jensen gap of Example 4.4 wearing different clothes. The entire purpose of population adjustment, MAIC, STC, and ML-NMR, is to compute the average on the correct side of this inequality.

4.8.2 The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for expectations

Theorem 4.7 (Cauchy-Schwarz for expectations) For random variables \(X,Y\in L^2\), \[ \bigl(\mathbb{E}[XY]\bigr)^2\le\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2], \tag{4.12}\] with equality if and only if \(X=0\) almost surely, or \(Y=cX\) almost surely for some constant \(c\).

Proof. First note \(XY\in L^1\), since \(|XY|\le\tfrac12(X^2+Y^2)\) by the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, so the left side is defined. If \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0\) then \(X=0\) almost surely, both sides are \(0\), and the equality case holds. Assume \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]>0\). For every \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\), nonnegativity of a square and linearity give \[ 0\le\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y-\lambda X)^2\bigr] =\mathbb{E}[Y^2]-2\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[XY]+\lambda^2\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]. \] The right side is a quadratic in \(\lambda\) with positive leading coefficient that is nonnegative for all real \(\lambda\); hence its discriminant is nonpositive, \[ 4\bigl(\mathbb{E}[XY]\bigr)^2-4\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]\le 0, \] which is Equation 4.12. Equality holds if and only if the discriminant is zero, that is, if and only if the quadratic has a real double root \(\lambda^{*}\), at which \(\mathbb{E}[(Y-\lambda^{*}X)^2]=0\), equivalently \(Y=\lambda^{*}X\) almost surely. \(\square\)

This is result A.8. Applied to a centered pair \((X-\mathbb{E}X,\,Y-\mathbb{E}Y)\) it gives the covariance bound \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)^2\le\mathrm{Var}(X)\,\mathrm{Var}(Y)\), so the correlation coefficient lies in \([-1,1]\). A second instance is the bound behind effective sample size. Take the probability space to be the index set \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) equipped with the empirical measure \(P(\{i\})=1/n\), and on it the two random variables \(X(i)=w_i\) (the matching weight of patient \(i\)) and \(Y(i)=1\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[XY]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i w_i\), \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i w_i^2\), and \(\mathbb{E}[Y^2]=1\), so Equation 4.12 reads \[ \Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i w_i\Bigr)^2\le\frac{1}{n}\sum_i w_i^2, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \Bigl(\sum_i w_i\Bigr)^2\le n\sum_i w_i^2. \] Rearranged, this is the effective sample size bound \(\mathrm{ESS}=\bigl(\sum_i w_i\bigr)^2/\sum_i w_i^2\le n\), with equality if and only if all weights are equal.

Remark. Why this matters. The ESS bound is the formal reason matching-adjusted indirect comparison loses precision under poor covariate overlap. When the source and target populations differ sharply the weights \(w_i\) become very unequal, the inequality is far from tight, and the effective sample size collapses well below \(n\). Cauchy-Schwarz here is not an abstract nicety; it quantifies the precision cost of reweighting, which Chapter 18 develops in full.

We also record the multidimensional integration theorem, result A.10, which licenses every interchange of the order of integration in the aggregate likelihoods of Part V.

Theorem 4.8 (Fubini-Tonelli (cited)) Let \((\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},\mu)\) and \((\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{B},\nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces (\(\sigma\)-finite means each space can be covered by countably many pieces of finite measure; Lebesgue measure and every probability measure qualify). If \(f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty]\) is jointly measurable, then the two iterated integrals and the double integral against the product measure \(\mu\otimes\nu\) all agree (Tonelli). If instead \(f\) is real-valued with \(\int|f|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty\), the iterated integrals are well-defined almost everywhere and equal the double integral (Fubini).

A proof is in Billingsley (1995), §18.

Remark. Why this matters. Fubini-Tonelli is the license to average in stages. In Part V an aggregate likelihood is built by first averaging an individual outcome over a covariate distribution within a study, then combining across studies or arms; the theorem guarantees that the order of these nested averages does not change the answer, so the integral defining the aggregate model is well posed.

We turn to the structural fact that links the covariance matrix to the positive-definite theory of Section 4.9.

4.9 Covariance matrices are positive semidefinite

The covariance matrix is not an arbitrary symmetric matrix; the quadratic-form identity Equation 4.4 forces it into the positive semidefinite cone, and conversely every member of that cone arises as a covariance. This is the precise sense in which the spectral theory of Chapter 3 is the right language for second moments. Recall from Chapter 3 the characterization Theorem 3.3, that a symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive semidefinite (\(A\succeq 0\)) if and only if \(\mathbf{a}^{\top}A\,\mathbf{a}\ge 0\) for all \(\mathbf{a}\), equivalently if and only if all its eigenvalues are nonnegative, and the spectral theorem Theorem 3.2, that a symmetric \(A\) factors as \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda\) diagonal.

Theorem 4.9 (Covariance matrices and the PSD cone) Let \(\mathbf{X}\) be a random vector in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) with all components in \(L^2\) and covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X})\). Then \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Conversely, every symmetric positive semidefinite \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\) is the covariance matrix of some random vector.

Proof. Symmetry holds because \(\Sigma_{ij}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\mathrm{Cov}(X_j,X_i)=\Sigma_{ji}\). For positive semidefiniteness, fix \(\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^p\). By the quadratic-form identity Equation 4.4, \[ \mathbf{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{a}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X})\ge 0, \] since a variance is the expectation of a square and hence nonnegative. As this holds for every \(\mathbf{a}\), the characterization Theorem 3.3 gives \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succeq 0\).

For the converse, let \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succeq 0\) be symmetric. By the spectral theorem Theorem 3.2 write \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p)\); by Theorem 3.3 every \(\lambda_i\ge 0\), so the symmetric square root \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}:=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_p})\,Q^{\top}\) is a well-defined symmetric matrix with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). Let \(\mathbf{Z}=(Z_1,\dots,Z_p)^{\top}\) have independent components with \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i]=0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Z_i)=1\), so that \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z})=I_p\), and set \(\mathbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]=\mathbf{0}\) and, using bilinearity of covariance under linear maps, \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2} =\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}I_p\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}.\qquad\square \]

Remark. Why this matters. This theorem lets us treat “a covariance matrix” and “a positive semidefinite matrix” as the same object. Practically, it guarantees that any valid covariance we estimate can be factored as \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) and simulated from, and it is the reason the normal-approximation likelihoods of network meta-analysis, whose inputs are estimated covariance matrices of log-effects, are coherent probability models rather than formal expressions.

The converse construction \(\mathbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}\), with \(\mathbf{Z}\) a vector of standard normals, is exactly how we will build the general multivariate normal in Section 4.12.

4.10 Moment generating functions

How can we find the distribution of a sum of independent variables without grinding through a convolution integral, and how can we read off moments without integrating against the density each time? Both are answered by a single transform. A distribution is often most conveniently encoded not through its density but through a transform that turns convolution into multiplication and differentiation into moment extraction.

Definition 4.7 (Moment generating function) The moment generating function (MGF) of a random vector \(X\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is \[ M_X(\mathbf{t}):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}X}\bigr],\qquad\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^p, \] defined for those \(\mathbf{t}\) at which the expectation is finite. We say the MGF exists if it is finite for all \(\mathbf{t}\) in some open neighborhood of the origin. For a scalar \(X\) this reads \(M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\).

Remark. Reader translation. The moment generating function is a calculation device, not a modeling assumption. It repackages a whole distribution as one function of a dummy variable \(\mathbf{t}\), with two payoffs: differentiating it at the origin reads off the moments (Theorem 4.10), and multiplying two of them yields the distribution of a sum (Theorem 4.12) without the difficult convolution that densities would require. We use it precisely to avoid repeated density integrations when establishing facts about the Poisson, the normal, and the multivariate normal. Following the notation chapter we keep the subscript unbolded, \(M_X\), even when \(X\) is a vector.

The name is justified by the next result: where the MGF exists, it determines all moments by differentiation at the origin.

Theorem 4.10 (Moments from the MGF) Suppose the scalar MGF \(M_X\) is finite on \((-\delta,\delta)\) for some \(\delta>0\). Then \(X\) has finite moments of every order, \(M_X\) is infinitely differentiable on \((-\delta,\delta)\), and \[ M_X^{(k)}(0)=\mathbb{E}[X^k],\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots. \]

Proof. Fix \(0<r<\delta\). The elementary bound \(|x|^k\le k!\,r^{-k}e^{r|x|}\), valid because \(e^{r|x|}\ge (r|x|)^k/k!\), together with \(e^{r|x|}\le e^{rx}+e^{-rx}\), gives \(|X|^k\le k!\,r^{-k}\bigl(e^{rX}+e^{-rX}\bigr)\). The right side is integrable, as \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\pm rX}]=M_X(\pm r)<\infty\), so \(\mathbb{E}|X|^k<\infty\) for every \(k\). To differentiate under the expectation, fix \(t\in(-\delta,\delta)\) and choose \(r'\) with \(|t|<r'<\delta\). For real \(h\) with \(0<|h|<r'-|t|\) the difference quotient \(\frac{e^{(t+h)X}-e^{tX}}{h}\) equals \(Xe^{\theta X}\) for some \(\theta\) between \(t\) and \(t+h\), by the mean value theorem, and since \(|\theta|<r'\) it is bounded in absolute value by \(|X|e^{r'|X|}\). Choosing \(r''\) with \(r'<r''<\delta\), the elementary bound \(u\,e^{r'u}\le C\,e^{r''u}\) for \(u\ge 0\), valid with \(C=\frac{1}{e\,(r''-r')}\) because the function \(u\mapsto u\,e^{-(r''-r')u}\) attains its maximum over \(u\ge 0\) at \(u=1/(r''-r')\) with value \(C\), gives \(|X|e^{r'|X|}\le C\bigl(e^{r''X}+e^{-r''X}\bigr)\), a dominating variable that is integrable because \(M_X(\pm r'')<\infty\). As \(h\to 0\) the quotients converge pointwise to \(Xe^{tX}\), so the dominated convergence theorem Theorem 4.2 yields \(M_X'(t)=\mathbb{E}[Xe^{tX}]\). Applying the same argument to \(x\mapsto x^{j}e^{tx}\), whose increments are dominated by \(|X|^{j+1}e^{r'|X|}\le C_j\bigl(e^{r''X}+e^{-r''X}\bigr)\), gives by induction \(M_X^{(k)}(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^k e^{tX}]\) for every \(k\) and every \(t\in(-\delta,\delta)\). Evaluating at \(t=0\) gives \(M_X^{(k)}(0)=\mathbb{E}[X^k]\). \(\square\)

The MGF also identifies the law and turns independent sums into products. The first property is deep and is cited; the second we prove.

Theorem 4.11 (Uniqueness and factorization (uniqueness cited)) If two random vectors \(X,Y\) have MGFs that are finite and equal on a common neighborhood of the origin, then \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same law. Consequently, if the joint MGF of \((X,Y)\) factors as \(M_{(X,Y)}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})=M_X(\mathbf{s})\,M_Y(\mathbf{t})\) on a neighborhood of the origin, then \(X\perp Y\).

Proof. The uniqueness statement is classical and is proved by analytic continuation of the MGF to the complex plane, where it becomes the characteristic function whose inversion recovers the law; see Billingsley (1995), §30. For the factorization consequence, let \(\widetilde{P}=P_X\otimes P_Y\) be the product of the marginal laws, the law of an independent pair \((\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})\) with the given marginals. By Theorem 4.8, \[ \mathbb{E}_{\widetilde P}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{s}^{\top}\widetilde X+\mathbf{t}^{\top}\widetilde Y}\bigr] =\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{s}^{\top}X}\bigr]\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}Y}\bigr] =M_X(\mathbf{s})\,M_Y(\mathbf{t}), \] which by hypothesis equals the joint MGF of \((X,Y)\) near the origin. By the uniqueness statement applied to the pair, the joint law of \((X,Y)\) equals the product law \(\widetilde{P}\), which is the definition of \(X\perp Y\). \(\square\)

Theorem 4.12 (MGF of a sum of independent variables) If \(X\perp Y\) are scalar random variables whose MGFs are finite on a common neighborhood of the origin, then \(M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t)\) there. More generally, for mutually independent \(X_1,\dots,X_n\), \(M_{X_1+\cdots+X_n}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t)\).

Proof. By independence and the factorization of expectations of products of functions of independent variables, \[ M_{X+Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{t(X+Y)}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{tX}e^{tY}\bigr] =\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\,\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]=M_X(t)M_Y(t), \] the middle equality justified by Theorem 4.8 applied to the nonnegative integrand \(e^{tx}e^{ty}\) against the product law. The \(n\)-fold statement follows by induction, since a partial sum \(X_1+\cdots+X_{n-1}\) is independent of \(X_n\). \(\square\)

We record two further elementary identities used repeatedly. For an affine transform, \(M_{a+bX}(t)=e^{at}M_X(bt)\), immediate from \(\mathbb{E}[e^{t(a+bX)}]=e^{at}\mathbb{E}[e^{(tb)X}]\); and for a vector \(X\) and matrix \(B\), \(M_{BX}(\mathbf{t})=M_X(B^{\top}\mathbf{t})\). These power the multivariate normal calculus of Section 4.12.

4.11 Core distributions

We pause the abstract development to collect the concrete distributions we will quote later, computing the mean, variance, and moment generating function of each once so the arithmetic need not be repeated. We then return to the multivariate normal in Section 4.12, which leans on the univariate normal recorded here. The arithmetic in this section is elementary and worth carrying out once.

Definition 4.8 (Bernoulli) \(X\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\), \(p\in[0,1]\), takes the value \(1\) with probability \(p\) and \(0\) with probability \(1-p\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X]=p\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=p(1-p)\), and \(M_X(t)=(1-p)+pe^{t}\).

The mean is \(p\cdot 1+(1-p)\cdot 0=p\); the second moment is \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]=p\) since \(X^2=X\), so the variance is \(p-p^2=p(1-p)\); the MGF is \((1-p)e^{0}+pe^{t}\).

Definition 4.9 (Binomial) \(X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\) is the number of successes in \(n\) independent \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) trials, with probability mass function \(P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\) for \(k=0,\dots,n\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X]=np\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p)\), and \(M_X(t)=\bigl((1-p)+pe^{t}\bigr)^n\).

Writing \(X=\sum_{i=1}^n B_i\) with \(B_i\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) independent, linearity gives \(\mathbb{E}[X]=np\), independence gives \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sum_i\mathrm{Var}(B_i)=np(1-p)\), and Theorem 4.12 gives \(M_X(t)=\prod_i\bigl((1-p)+pe^t\bigr)=\bigl((1-p)+pe^t\bigr)^n\).

Definition 4.10 (Poisson) \(X\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\), \(\lambda>0\), has \(P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\) for \(k=0,1,2,\dots\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\lambda\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda\), and \(M_X(t)=\exp\bigl(\lambda(e^t-1)\bigr)\).

The MGF is \(M_X(t)=\sum_{k\ge 0}e^{tk}e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k! =e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k\ge 0}(\lambda e^t)^k/k!=e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda e^t}=\exp(\lambda(e^t-1))\), the interchange of sum and the implicit expectation being an absolutely convergent series. Then \(M_X'(t)=\lambda e^t M_X(t)\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[X]=M_X'(0)=\lambda\), and \(M_X''(0)=\lambda+\lambda^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]\) gives \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda\), by Theorem 4.10. The Poisson is the law of rare events, made precise through the MGF.

Theorem 4.13 (Poisson limit of the binomial) Fix \(\lambda>0\). If \(X_n\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\lambda/n)\), then \(M_{X_n}(t)\to\exp(\lambda(e^t-1))\) for every \(t\), and hence \(X_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\).

Proof. By Definition 4.9, \[ M_{X_n}(t)=\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\lambda}{n}+\tfrac{\lambda}{n}e^t\Bigr)^n =\Bigl(1+\tfrac{\lambda(e^t-1)}{n}\Bigr)^n\longrightarrow\exp\bigl(\lambda(e^t-1)\bigr), \] using the standard limit \((1+a/n)^n\to e^a\) with \(a=\lambda(e^t-1)\). The limit is the MGF of \(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\) from Definition 4.10, finite in a neighborhood of \(0\); by the continuity theorem for moment generating functions, convergence of MGFs on a neighborhood of the origin implies convergence in distribution (see Billingsley (1995), §30). \(\square\)

The continuity theorem is cited here; a quantitative total-variation version of this Poisson approximation, due to Le Cam, is proved in the multilevel network meta-regression chapters of Part V. The next distribution generalizes the binomial by allowing the success probabilities to differ across trials, and it is the law of an aggregate event count when individual risks vary, which is exactly the situation in covariate-adjusted meta-analysis.

Definition 4.11 (Poisson binomial) Let \(\mathbf{p}=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\in[0,1]^n\) and let \(B_1,\dots,B_n\) be independent with \(B_i\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_i)\). The law of \(X=\sum_{i=1}^n B_i\) is the Poisson binomial distribution \(\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf{p})\). Its probability mass function is \[ P(X=k)=\sum_{\substack{A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\\ |A|=k}}\ \prod_{i\in A}p_i\prod_{i\notin A}(1-p_i), \qquad k=0,\dots,n, \] its mean and variance are \[ \mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i(1-p_i), \] and its MGF is \(M_X(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n\bigl((1-p_i)+p_i e^t\bigr)\).

Remark. Reader translation. The Poisson binomial is the law of a head count when each coin has its own bias. If \(100\) patients each carry their own baseline risk \(p_i\) of an event, the total number of events is \(\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf{p})\), not \(\mathrm{Bin}(100,\bar p)\); the binomial is only the special case of equal risks. This is exactly the situation in covariate-adjusted meta-analysis, where individual risks vary with covariates, and it is why aggregate event counts there are modeled as Poisson binomial.

The mass function lists the disjoint ways to obtain exactly \(k\) successes; the mean and variance follow from linearity and independence as in the binomial case; the MGF follows from Theorem 4.12 applied to the Bernoulli MGFs of Definition 4.8. When all \(p_i\) are equal to a common \(p\), every quantity reduces to its \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\) counterpart. For a fixed mean \(\sum_i p_i\), the variance \(\sum_i p_i(1-p_i) =\sum_i p_i-\sum_i p_i^2\) is largest when the \(p_i\) are equal, by convexity of \(p\mapsto p^2\); spreading the risks apart reduces dispersion in the count. We verify all of this numerically in Section 4.13.

Definition 4.12 (Normal) \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\), \(\mu\in\mathbb{R}\), \(\sigma^2>0\), has density \[ f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}. \] Then \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2\), and \(M_X(t)=\exp\bigl(\mu t+\tfrac12\sigma^2t^2\bigr)\). The case \(\mu=0,\sigma^2=1\) is the standard normal, with density \(\phi\) and CDF \(\Phi\).

Remark. Reader translation. The normal is the familiar bell curve; its two parameters are the mean \(\mu\) and the variance \(\sigma^2\), not the standard deviation. Beware a convention shift coming in Section 4.12: the univariate \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) takes a variance as its second argument, while the multivariate \(\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) takes a covariance matrix, and the subscript \(p\) flags the dimension.

Proof. That \(f_X\) integrates to \(1\) is the Gaussian integral \(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-u^2/2}\,du=\sqrt{2\pi}\), established by the polar-coordinates evaluation of its square. For the MGF, substitute and complete the square in the exponent: \[ tx-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} =-\frac{\bigl(x-(\mu+\sigma^2 t)\bigr)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\mu t+\frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}, \] an algebraic identity verified by expanding both sides. Therefore \[ M_X(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}\,dx =e^{\mu t+\sigma^2 t^2/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma} e^{-(x-(\mu+\sigma^2 t))^2/2\sigma^2}\,dx =e^{\mu t+\sigma^2 t^2/2}, \] the remaining integral being that of a \(\mathcal{N}(\mu+\sigma^2 t,\sigma^2)\) density, which equals \(1\). Differentiating, \(M_X'(t)=(\mu+\sigma^2 t)M_X(t)\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[X]=M_X'(0)=\mu\), and \(M_X''(0)=\sigma^2+\mu^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]\) gives \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2\), by Theorem 4.10. \(\square\)

4.12 The multivariate normal

The multivariate normal is the joint distribution that the central limit theorem produces for sums and that linear models assume for errors; its closure under affine maps and conditioning is what makes normal-theory inference tractable. We define it through its MGF, which is the cleanest route and which handles singular covariance matrices without special cases.

Definition 4.13 (Multivariate normal) A random vector \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) has the multivariate normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), with mean vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) and symmetric positive semidefinite covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\), if its moment generating function is \[ M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t})=\exp\!\Bigl(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu} +\tfrac12\,\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{t}\Bigr),\qquad\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^p. \tag{4.13}\]

Remark. Reader translation. We define the multivariate normal by its moment generating function rather than a density for one practical reason: the formula Equation 4.13 makes sense even when \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is singular, whereas the density Equation 4.14 needs \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\) and breaks down. Singular covariance is not exotic; it is exactly what arises when one component is a deterministic linear combination of the others, the probabilistic face of perfect collinearity in a design matrix. The MGF definition handles the collinear and the full-rank cases with a single formula.

The definition is consistent: such a law exists and is unique. Uniqueness is Theorem 4.11. Existence is the construction \(\mathbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}\) with \(\mathbf{Z}=(Z_1,\dots,Z_p)^{\top}\) a vector of independent standard normals, exactly the construction of Theorem 4.9. Indeed its MGF is, by independence and the standard-normal MGF \(\mathbb{E}[e^{sZ_i}]=e^{s^2/2}\), \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z})}\bigr] =e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}}\prod_{i=1}^p\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t})_i Z_i}\bigr] =e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}}\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac12\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t}\|_2^2\Bigr) =\exp\!\Bigl(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}\Bigr), \] since \(\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t}\|_2^2 =\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t} =\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}\). Thus Equation 4.13 is the MGF of an honest random vector, whose mean is \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and whose covariance is \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) by Theorem 4.9.

When \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\) is nonsingular the law has a density, obtained by the change of variables from \(\mathbf{Z}\).

Proposition 4.2 (Density of a nonsingular multivariate normal) If \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\), then \(\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) has density \[ f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=(2\pi)^{-p/2}\,\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1/2} \exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\Bigr), \qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^p. \tag{4.14}\]

Proof. The standard normal vector \(\mathbf{Z}\) has density \((2\pi)^{-p/2}\exp(-\tfrac12\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\), being a product of \(p\) standard normal densities. Since \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\), the symmetric square root \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) is invertible, so \(\mathbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}\) is an invertible affine map with inverse \(\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\) and Jacobian determinant \(\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2})=\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1/2}\). The change-of-variables formula for densities gives \[ f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=(2\pi)^{-p/2}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac12\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\|_2^2\Bigr)\,\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1/2}, \] and \(\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\|_2^2 =(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\), which is Equation 4.14. \(\square\)

The defining MGF makes the behavior under affine maps a one-line computation, and that single fact yields marginals, sums, and the normality of every linear combination.

Theorem 4.14 (Affine maps of a multivariate normal) Let \(\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), let \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}\), and let \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^q\). Then \[ \mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{b}+B\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_q\bigl(\mathbf{b}+B\boldsymbol{\mu},\,B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top}\bigr). \] In particular every subvector of \(\mathbf{X}\) is multivariate normal, and for any \(\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) the scalar \(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a})\).

Proof. Compute the MGF of \(\mathbf{Y}\) for \(\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^q\), using the affine and matrix identities recorded after Theorem 4.12 and the defining MGF Equation 4.13 of \(\mathbf{X}\): \[ M_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{b}+B\mathbf{X})}\bigr] =e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}\,M_{\mathbf{X}}(B^{\top}\mathbf{t}) =e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}\exp\!\Bigl((B^{\top}\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu} +\tfrac12(B^{\top}\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(B^{\top}\mathbf{t})\Bigr). \] The exponent equals \(\mathbf{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{b}+B\boldsymbol{\mu})+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}^{\top}(B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top})\mathbf{t}\), so \(M_{\mathbf{Y}}\) is the MGF Equation 4.13 with mean \(\mathbf{b}+B\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance \(B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top}\), the latter symmetric positive semidefinite because \(\mathbf{s}^{\top}B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top}\mathbf{s}=(B^{\top}\mathbf{s})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(B^{\top}\mathbf{s})\ge 0\). By Theorem 4.11, \(\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}_q(\mathbf{b}+B\boldsymbol{\mu},B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top})\). The marginal claim is the case \(B=[\,I_k\ \ 0\,]\), \(\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\), and the scalar claim is the case \(B=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\). \(\square\)

Example 4.5 (A two-dimensional normal, previewed) To break the abstraction before the conditional-distribution proof, take the bivariate normal we will carry through Section 4.13, \[ \boldsymbol{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}. \] By Theorem 4.14 the first coordinate is \(X_1\sim\mathcal{N}(1,2)\), read straight off the diagonal, and the sum \(X_1+X_2\) is the affine image with \(B=(1,\ 1)\), giving \(X_1+X_2\sim\mathcal{N}(3,5)\) since \(B\boldsymbol{\mu}=3\) and \(B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top}=5\). No densities or integrals are needed; one matrix multiplication delivers the law of any linear summary. The conditioning, density, and Jensen-gap calculations for this same matrix are completed in Section 4.13.

For the conditional distribution we need the special feature of normality that uncorrelated jointly normal blocks are actually independent, a phenomenon that fails for general distributions.

Lemma 4.3 (Uncorrelated jointly normal blocks are independent) Partition \(\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) as \(\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_1,\mathbf{X}_2)\) with covariance blocks \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\). If \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=0\), then \(\mathbf{X}_1\perp\mathbf{X}_2\).

Proof. Write \(\mathbf{t}=(\mathbf{t}_1,\mathbf{t}_2)\) conformably. When \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=0\) (hence \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}=0\)), the quadratic form in Equation 4.13 splits, \(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{t} =\mathbf{t}_1^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\mathbf{t}_1+\mathbf{t}_2^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\mathbf{t}_2\), and the linear form splits as \(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{t}_1^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\mathbf{t}_2^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_2\). Hence the joint MGF factors, \[ M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t}_1,\mathbf{t}_2) =\exp\!\Bigl(\mathbf{t}_1^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}_1^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\mathbf{t}_1\Bigr) \exp\!\Bigl(\mathbf{t}_2^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_2+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}_2^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\mathbf{t}_2\Bigr) =M_{\mathbf{X}_1}(\mathbf{t}_1)\,M_{\mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{t}_2), \] where the marginal MGFs are read off from Theorem 4.14. By the factorization criterion of Theorem 4.11, \(\mathbf{X}_1\perp\mathbf{X}_2\). \(\square\)

Theorem 4.15 (Conditional distribution of a multivariate normal) Partition \(\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) as above with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\succ 0\). Then the conditional distribution of \(\mathbf{X}_1\) given \(\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\) is multivariate normal, \[ \mathbf{X}_1\mid\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2), \ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\Bigr). \tag{4.15}\] The conditional covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1\mid 2}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\) is the Schur complement of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\) (the residual covariance left in \(\mathbf{X}_1\) after subtracting the part linearly explained by \(\mathbf{X}_2\)) and does not depend on \(\mathbf{x}_2\).

Proof. Define the regression residual \(\mathbf{U}:=\mathbf{X}_1-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_2\). The pair \((\mathbf{U},\mathbf{X}_2)\) is the affine image of \(\mathbf{X}\) under \[ \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\\mathbf{X}_2\end{pmatrix} =\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\\0&I\end{pmatrix}}_{=:A} \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}_1\\\mathbf{X}_2\end{pmatrix}, \] so by Theorem 4.14 it is jointly multivariate normal. Its cross-covariance vanishes: \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{X}_2) =\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_1,\mathbf{X}_2)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_2,\mathbf{X}_2) =\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}=0, \] so \(\mathbf{U}\perp\mathbf{X}_2\) by Lemma 4.3. The mean and covariance of \(\mathbf{U}\) are \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}]=\boldsymbol{\mu}_1-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_2, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{U})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} +\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} =\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1\mid 2}, \] the cross terms combining because \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}\). Now recover \(\mathbf{X}_1=\mathbf{U}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_2\) and compute the conditional MGF given \(\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\). Since the factor \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_2\) becomes the constant \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_2\) under the conditioning, and since \(\mathbf{U}\perp\mathbf{X}_2\) implies \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{U}}\mid\mathbf{X}_2]=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{U}}]\) (this is the one step that genuinely uses normality: for a general distribution \(\mathbf{U}\) could be uncorrelated with \(\mathbf{X}_2\) yet still dependent, but Lemma 4.3 upgrades the vanishing cross-covariance to full independence, so conditioning on \(\mathbf{X}_2\) leaves the law of \(\mathbf{U}\) untouched), \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_1}\,\big|\,\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\bigr] =e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_2}\, \mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{U}}\bigr] =\exp\!\Bigl(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\bigr) +\tfrac12\mathbf{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1\mid 2}\mathbf{t}\Bigr), \] where the last step substitutes the MGF Equation 4.13 of \(\mathbf{U}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}],\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1\mid 2})\) and collects the linear terms. This is the MGF of the normal law on the right of Equation 4.15, so by Theorem 4.11 the conditional law is as claimed. \(\square\)

The conditional mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\) is an affine function of the conditioning value, the population analogue of a linear regression of \(\mathbf{X}_1\) on \(\mathbf{X}_2\), and the conditional covariance is constant in \(\mathbf{x}_2\). These two features, linear conditional mean and homoskedastic conditional covariance, are the exact distributional content of the linear model of Chapter 6 and recur in every normal-approximation likelihood used in the network meta-analysis chapters.

Remark. Why this matters. The conditional mean of a multivariate normal is linear in the conditioning value, the population blueprint of linear regression: the regression coefficients are the population object \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\). For indirect comparison this is the object we integrate over a target population’s covariate distribution to produce a population-average prediction, the move at the heart of ML-NMR in Part V. The constant (homoskedastic) conditional covariance is why the normal-theory likelihoods of network meta-analysis can carry a single residual variance.

4.13 A fully worked example

Where the toy trial of Example 4.1 gave us a discrete thread, we now run a continuous one. We carry a single bivariate normal through every theorem of Section 4.9 and Section 4.12, with arithmetic the reader can reproduce by hand, and then verify the Poisson binomial mass function of Definition 4.11 on a small example.

4.13.1 A bivariate normal

Let \(\mathbf{X}=(X_1,X_2)^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}_2(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) with \[ \boldsymbol{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Positive definiteness (Theorem 4.9). The matrix is symmetric. Its eigenvalues solve \(\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)(1-\lambda)-1=\lambda^2-3\lambda+1=0\), giving \(\lambda=\tfrac{3\pm\sqrt5}{2}\approx 2.618\) and \(0.382\), both positive, so \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\); its determinant is \(\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=2\cdot 1-1\cdot 1=1\). We may also confirm positive semidefiniteness through Equation 4.4 on a specific direction: for \(\mathbf{a}=(1,-1)^{\top}\), \[ \mathbf{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a} =\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}=1 =\mathrm{Var}(X_1-X_2)=\mathrm{Var}(X_1)+\mathrm{Var}(X_2)-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=2+1-2=1\ge 0, \] where \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{a}=(2-1,\,1-1)^{\top}=(1,0)^{\top}\).

Marginals and correlation (Theorem 4.14, Theorem 4.7). Reading off the diagonal, \(X_1\sim\mathcal{N}(1,2)\) and \(X_2\sim\mathcal{N}(2,1)\). The correlation is \(\rho=\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2)/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_1)\mathrm{Var}(X_2)}=1/\sqrt{2\cdot 1}=1/\sqrt2\approx 0.707\), and Cauchy-Schwarz is the statement \(\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2)^2=1\le 2=\mathrm{Var}(X_1)\,\mathrm{Var}(X_2)\), that is \(\rho^2=\tfrac12\le 1\).

An affine map (Theorem 4.14). Let \(Y=X_1+X_2=B\mathbf{X}\) with \(B=(1,\ 1)\). Then \(Y\sim\mathcal{N}(B\boldsymbol{\mu},B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top})\) with mean \(B\boldsymbol{\mu}=1+2=3\) and variance \(B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top}=(1,1)\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}(1,1)^{\top}=(3,2)(1,1)^{\top}=5\), so \(Y\sim\mathcal{N}(3,5)\). The direct check is \(\mathrm{Var}(X_1+X_2)=2+1+2\cdot 1=5\).

The conditional distribution (Theorem 4.15). With \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}=2\), \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=1\), \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}=1\), the conditional law of \(X_1\) given \(X_2=x_2\) has mean \(\mu_1+\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2)=1+1\cdot(x_2-2)=x_2-1\) and variance \(\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}=2-1\cdot 1\cdot 1=1\). Hence \[ X_1\mid X_2=x_2\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}(x_2-1,\ 1),\qquad\text{and at }x_2=4,\quad X_1\mid X_2=4\sim\mathcal{N}(3,1). \] Note that the conditional variance \(1\) is smaller than the marginal variance \(2\): conditioning on the correlated \(X_2\) removes part of the uncertainty in \(X_1\). This is the law of total variance Corollary 4.2 in action, \[ \mathrm{Var}(X_1)=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X_1\mid X_2)]+\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X_1\mid X_2]) =1+\mathrm{Var}(X_2-1)=1+1=2,\ \checkmark \] because \(\mathrm{Var}(X_1\mid X_2)\equiv 1\) is constant and \(\mathbb{E}[X_1\mid X_2]=X_2-1\) has variance \(\mathrm{Var}(X_2)=1\).

The density (Proposition 4.2). Since \(\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=1\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\) (one checks \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=I\)), the density is \[ f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac12\bigl[(x_1-1)^2-2(x_1-1)(x_2-2)+2(x_2-2)^2\bigr]\Bigr), \] and at the mean \(\mathbf{x}=\boldsymbol{\mu}=(1,2)^{\top}\) this is \(f_{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=1/(2\pi)\approx 0.159\).

The Jensen gap (Theorem 4.6). Apply the convex \(\varphi(u)=u^2\) to \(X_1\sim\mathcal{N}(1,2)\). The left side of Equation 4.11 is \(\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X_1])=1^2=1\); the right side is \(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_1)]=\mathbb{E}[X_1^2]=\mathrm{Var}(X_1)+(\mathbb{E}X_1)^2=2+1=3\). The Jensen gap is \(3-1=2\), which is exactly \(\mathrm{Var}(X_1)\), as anticipated after Theorem 4.6.

4.13.2 A Poisson binomial count

Take \(n=3\) trials with heterogeneous success probabilities \(\mathbf{p}=(0.2,\,0.5,\,0.8)\), so \(X\sim\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf{p})\) with failure probabilities \((0.8,\,0.5,\,0.2)\). The mass function of Definition 4.11 enumerates subsets: \[ \begin{aligned} P(X=0)&=0.8\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.2=0.08,\\ P(X=1)&=0.2\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.2+0.8\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.2+0.8\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.8=0.02+0.08+0.32=0.42,\\ P(X=2)&=0.2\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.2+0.2\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.8+0.8\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.8=0.02+0.08+0.32=0.42,\\ P(X=3)&=0.2\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.8=0.08, \end{aligned} \] which sum to \(1\). The mean is \(\mathbb{E}[X]=0.2+0.5+0.8=1.5\), confirmed from the mass function as \(0(0.08)+1(0.42)+2(0.42)+3(0.08)=1.5\). The variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=0.2(0.8)+0.5(0.5)+0.8(0.2)=0.16+0.25+0.16=0.57\), confirmed as \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2=(0.42+4\cdot 0.42+9\cdot 0.08)-1.5^2=2.82-2.25=0.57\). For contrast, a binomial \(\mathrm{Bin}(3,0.5)\) has the same mean \(1.5\) but the larger variance \(3(0.5)(0.5)=0.75\); spreading the per-trial risks apart, at fixed total risk, has reduced the dispersion of the count, in keeping with the convexity remark after Definition 4.11.
