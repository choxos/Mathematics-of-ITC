5  Statistical Inference

Every indirect treatment comparison reduces, in the end, to a statement about numbers we estimated from finite data and the uncertainty attached to them. A network meta-analysis combines log odds ratios, each of which is a nonlinear function of fitted model parameters; its credible or confidence intervals come from the curvature of a likelihood. A matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC, Notation) solves a system of weighting equations and then reports a standard error that must remain valid even though the weighting model is almost certainly misspecified. Multilevel network meta-regression fits a likelihood over individual and aggregate data and inverts the Fisher information to quantify precision. None of these methods can be stated, let alone trusted, without the machinery of estimation theory: what an estimator is, when it is unbiased, how its variance behaves, what the likelihood and the score and the Fisher information are, and how an estimator behaves as the sample grows.

This chapter builds that machinery from first principles. We define statistical models and estimators and the three numbers that summarize an estimator’s accuracy (bias, variance, mean squared error). We introduce the likelihood, the score function, and the Fisher information, and prove the information equality that ties them together. We define the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE), prove its functional invariance, and then prove the two results on which all asymptotic inference rests: the MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal. We prove the Cramér-Rao lower bound, which says no unbiased estimator can beat the inverse Fisher information, and we show the MLE attains it asymptotically. We prove the three asymptotic tools used throughout the book: the continuous mapping theorem, Slutsky’s theorem, and the delta method (catalogue results A.13, A.12, A.11). We then generalize from maximum likelihood to M-estimation and define the sandwich variance estimator, the robust standard error that Chapter 18 needs for MAIC. We close with the Wald, score, and likelihood-ratio tests and the confidence intervals obtained by inverting them.

Throughout, the data are \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), modeled as independent and identically distributed (iid) draws from a distribution indexed by a parameter \(\boldsymbol\theta\). The reader should keep one picture in mind: the log-likelihood is a random function of \(\boldsymbol\theta\); its slope (the score) vanishes at the MLE; its curvature (the information) determines how sharply the data pin down the parameter; and asymptotic normality is the statement that, viewed through a magnifying glass of width \(1/\sqrt n\), this random function looks like a parabola whose vertex is Gaussian.

That picture, drawn once, is worth carrying through the whole chapter:

   l_n(theta)   (the log-likelihood: how well a value of theta fits the data)
       ^
       |                 _______
       |               /    |    \      <-- peak at theta_hat, the MLE:
       |              /     |     \          the SLOPE here is zero (the score)
       |             /      |      \
       |            /       |       \    <-- the CURVATURE here is the information:
       |          _/        |        \_      a sharper peak means more information
       |       __/          |          \__     and a smaller standard error

A word on the data. We write the observations as \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), but a single \(X_i\) can be many things depending on the model: one yes/no outcome (did patient \(i\) respond?), a covariate vector (age, sex, biomarker), or an entire patient record (covariates, treatment arm, and outcome together). The theory below never cares which; it only needs the \(X_i\) to be iid draws from \(f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\).

5.1 Statistical models, estimators, and risk

We work on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, P)\) in the sense of Definition 4.1. A statistical model is a family of candidate distributions for the data, indexed by a parameter.

Example 5.1 (A running example: a two-arm trial and an indirect comparison) We return to one concrete study at every step. A randomized trial of a new treatment records, for each of \(n = 25\) patients, whether the patient responded (\(X_i = 1\)) or not (\(X_i = 0\)):

Patient \(i\) 1 2 3 4 \(\cdots\) 24 25 total
Response \(X_i\) 1 0 1 0 \(\cdots\) 1 0 \(\sum_i X_i = 10\)

Ten of the \(25\) patients respond. The single number we want is the response probability \(p\), together with an honest measure of how uncertain our estimate of \(p\) is. Every tool in this chapter is, in the end, a way to answer one of two questions about this trial: what is our best guess for \(p\)? (estimation) and how far off could that guess be? (uncertainty).

The destination is an indirect treatment comparison. Imagine a second trial of a different treatment against the same control, reporting its own response probabilities. Neither trial compares the two new treatments head to head, yet we will want the contrast between them on the log-odds scale, with a valid standard error. By the end we assemble exactly that (Example 5.3), using the likelihood to estimate each \(p\), the Fisher information for its precision, and the delta method to move to the log-odds scale and combine the two trials. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 5.1 (Parametric statistical model) Let \(\mathcal X\) be a sample space carrying a \(\sigma\)-finite dominating measure \(\nu\) (Lebesgue measure for continuous data, counting measure for discrete data). A parametric statistical model is a family \[ \mathcal F = \bigl\{\, f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta) : \boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta \,\bigr\}, \qquad \Theta \subseteq \mathbb R^{p}, \] where each \(f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta)\) is a probability density (or mass) function with respect to \(\nu\). The set \(\Theta\) is the parameter space. The model is identifiable if the map \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta)\) is injective, that is, if \(\boldsymbol\theta \ne \boldsymbol\theta'\) implies \(f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta) \ne f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta')\) on a set of positive \(\nu\)-measure.

We observe \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) iid with common density \(f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) for some unknown true parameter \(\boldsymbol\theta_0 \in \Theta\), and write \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\), \(\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}\), \(P_{\boldsymbol\theta}\) for expectation, variance, and probability computed under \(f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. A statistical model is a menu of candidate distributions for the data, one for each value of \(\boldsymbol\theta\); estimation is the job of picking the menu item that best matches what we saw. Two pieces of jargon in the definition:

  • A \(\sigma\)-finite dominating measure \(\nu\) is just a fixed reference yardstick that lets the word “density” mean something. For continuous data it is ordinary length or area (Lebesgue measure), so a density is the height of a curve; for discrete data it is counting measure, so a “density” is simply a probability mass \(P(X = x)\). You never have to choose \(\nu\) in practice: it is Lebesgue for continuous outcomes and counting for counts or yes/no data.
  • Identifiable says the map \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol\theta)\) is injective (different inputs give different outputs): no two parameter values produce the same distribution of observable data. If two did, no amount of data could ever tell them apart. For a non-identifiable example, suppose a model only ever affects the data through a product \(\mu = \alpha\beta\) but carries \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) separately; then \((\alpha,\beta) = (2,3)\) and \((6,1)\) give identical data distributions and are indistinguishable.

A note on \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\). Calling it the true parameter assumes the model is correctly specified, that the data really were generated by some member of the menu. Real outcome models are usually simplifications. We keep the clean “true parameter” language while developing maximum likelihood, and in Section 5.8 we drop it: there the target becomes whatever parameter our estimating equation aims at, whether or not any menu item is exactly right. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 5.2 (Estimator, bias, variance, mean squared error) An estimator of a quantity \(g(\boldsymbol\theta) \in \mathbb R\) is a measurable function \(T_n = T(X_1, \dots, X_n)\) of the data alone (it may not depend on \(\boldsymbol\theta\)). Its bias, variance, and mean squared error (MSE) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\) are \[ \mathrm{Bias}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T_n] - g(\boldsymbol\theta), \qquad \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n), \qquad \mathrm{MSE}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\bigl[(T_n - g(\boldsymbol\theta))^2\bigr]. \] The estimator is unbiased if \(\mathrm{Bias}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n) = 0\) for every \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta\). For a vector estimator \(\mathbf T_n\) of \(g(\boldsymbol\theta) \in \mathbb R^q\), the variance is replaced by the covariance matrix \(\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T_n)\) and the MSE by the matrix \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[(\mathbf T_n - g)(\mathbf T_n - g)^\top]\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. An estimator is a recipe that turns the data into a guess, with no peeking at the unknown \(\boldsymbol\theta\). In the running example the obvious estimator of \(p\) is the sample proportion \(\hat p = \tfrac1n\sum_i X_i = 10/25 = 0.40\). Bias is the systematic part of the error (does the recipe aim at the right target on average?), variance is the random scatter from sample to sample, and mean squared error combines the two. These three numbers are exactly how competing estimators are graded in the simulation studies of Chapter 29. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The bias and the variance measure two different failures: bias is a systematic offset, variance is random scatter. The MSE combines them, and the decomposition below shows exactly how.

Proposition 5.1 (Bias-variance decomposition) For any estimator \(T_n\) with \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T_n^2] < \infty\), \[ \mathrm{MSE}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n) = \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n) + \bigl(\mathrm{Bias}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n)\bigr)^2 . \tag{5.1}\] For a vector estimator \(\mathbf T_n\) of \(g(\boldsymbol\theta)\in\mathbb R^q\), the matrix MSE equals \(\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T_n) + \mathbf b\,\mathbf b^\top\) with \(\mathbf b = \mathrm{Bias}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T_n)\), and taking traces gives the scalar identity \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\|\mathbf T_n - g\|_2^2 = \mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T_n) + \|\mathbf b\|_2^2\).

Proof. Write \(m = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T_n]\) and \(b = m - g(\boldsymbol\theta)\). Then \[ \mathrm{MSE}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl[(T_n - m + b)^2\bigr] = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl[(T_n - m)^2\bigr] + 2 b\, \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T_n - m] + b^2 . \] The cross term vanishes because \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T_n - m] = 0\), and the first term is \(\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n)\) by definition, giving Equation 5.1. The vector statement is identical with \(\mathbf m = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[\mathbf T_n]\) and \(\mathbf b = \mathbf m - g\): expand \((\mathbf T_n - g)(\mathbf T_n - g)^\top = (\mathbf T_n - \mathbf m)(\mathbf T_n - \mathbf m)^\top + (\mathbf T_n - \mathbf m)\mathbf b^\top + \mathbf b(\mathbf T_n - \mathbf m)^\top + \mathbf b\mathbf b^\top\) and take \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\); the two middle terms vanish. The trace identity follows from \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf b\mathbf b^\top) = \|\mathbf b\|_2^2\) and linearity of the trace. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. The decomposition is the rule for trading bias against variance. A more complicated adjustment model (more covariates in a regression, a more flexible weighting model in MAIC) usually lowers bias but raises variance; Equation 5.1 is the scoreboard on which that trade is settled, and it is why a slightly biased estimator can be the more accurate one (Exercise 5.6). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Two notions of “the estimator gets it right as data accumulate” recur throughout the book. The first is asymptotic and concerns a sequence of estimators.

Definition 5.3 (Consistency) A sequence of estimators \(T_n\) is (weakly) consistent for \(g(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) if \(T_n \xrightarrow{p} g(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) as \(n \to \infty\), that is, for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(P_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\!\bigl(|T_n - g(\boldsymbol\theta_0)| > \varepsilon\bigr) \to 0\). It is strongly consistent if \(T_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} g(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Consistency is the promise that with enough data the estimator lands on the truth. Weak consistency (convergence in probability) is all we use in this book; strong consistency (almost-sure convergence, from Chapter 4) is the stronger statement that the estimator settles down for almost every possible infinite data stream, not merely with high probability at each \(n\). For the running example, consistency is the guarantee that \(\hat p \to p\) as the trial grows; in later chapters it is the guarantee that network meta-analysis and MAIC estimates stop being systematically wrong as studies get larger. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Unbiasedness and consistency are logically independent. The sample mean \(\bar X_n\) of iid data with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance is both unbiased (\(\mathbb E[\bar X_n] = \mu\)) and consistent (by the weak law of large numbers below). But \(T_n = X_1\) is unbiased for \(\mu\) yet not consistent, and a biased estimator whose bias and variance both vanish (for example a shrunken mean \(\tfrac{n}{n+1}\bar X_n\)) is consistent: its bias is \(\tfrac{n}{n+1}\mu - \mu = -\mu/(n+1) \to 0\) and its variance is \(\bigl(\tfrac{n}{n+1}\bigr)^2 \sigma^2/n \to 0\), so by Equation 5.1 its MSE vanishes. The MSE in Equation 5.1 is the right single-number summary of finite-sample accuracy, because \(\mathrm{MSE}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T_n)\to 0\) is equivalent to \(L^2\) convergence, which implies consistency by Chebyshev’s inequality. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.2 Asymptotic tools: continuous mapping, Slutsky, and the delta method

Why this section, and why now. Here is the concrete problem these three tools solve. In the running example we will estimate \(p\), but a network meta-analysis never reports a probability; it reports a log odds ratio, a nonlinear function of fitted probabilities. So the real question is: given the uncertainty in \(\hat p\), what is the uncertainty in \(\log\frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p}\), or in a contrast of two such quantities across trials? The delta method below is the machine that answers this, and it is the single most-used result of the chapter, because every effect measure in the book (log odds ratio, log hazard ratio, risk difference) is a smooth function of fitted parameters. The continuous mapping theorem and Slutsky’s theorem are the two lemmas the delta method (and the later limit theory of the MLE) is built from. If you read this section for one thing, read it for the delta method and Example 5.3. One honest word about ordering: we prove these tools before we have an estimator in hand because the consistency and normality proofs will call them repeatedly; keep the running example’s \(\hat p\) in mind as the thing they will eventually be applied to.

Before we can analyze any estimator as \(n \to \infty\) we need three theorems about how limits in distribution interact with continuous functions and with constants. We will use them constantly, so we prove them here, near the front. We take as given two classical results from probability, which belong to the foundations of Chapter 4 and which we cite rather than reprove; see Bingham and Fry (2010) and a standard graduate probability text for proofs.

Remark (Prerequisites cited from probability). We use the following standard facts about iid random vectors \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) in \(\mathbb R^p\).

  1. Weak law of large numbers (WLLN). If \(\mathbb E\|X_1\| < \infty\) with mean \(\boldsymbol\mu\), then \(\bar X_n = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\mu\).

  2. Multivariate central limit theorem (CLT). If, in addition, \(X_1\) has finite covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), then \(\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \boldsymbol\mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\).

  3. Portmanteau theorem. \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) if and only if \(\mathbb E[h(X_n)] \to \mathbb E[h(X)]\) for every bounded continuous \(h\); equivalently, if and only if \(\mathbb E[h(X_n)] \to \mathbb E[h(X)]\) for every bounded function \(h\) that is continuous at \(P_X\)-almost every point.

  4. Cramér-Wold device. \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) in \(\mathbb R^p\) if and only if \(\mathbf t^\top X_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf t^\top X\) in \(\mathbb R\) for every \(\mathbf t \in \mathbb R^p\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (The \(O_p\) and \(o_p\) shorthand (read once, use everywhere)). Two of the proofs below use a compact notation for “stochastically small” and “stochastically bounded,” listed in Notation. A sequence of random vectors \(V_n\) is \(o_p(1)\) if \(V_n \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf 0\) (it vanishes), and \(O_p(1)\) if it is bounded in probability: for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is a constant \(K\) with \(\sup_n P(\|V_n\| > K) < \varepsilon\) (it does not run off to infinity). Three rules are all we need, and each is elementary:

  1. A sequence that converges in distribution is \(O_p(1)\) (a convergent limit law cannot put mass at infinity).
  2. \(o_p(1) \times O_p(1) = o_p(1)\): something vanishing times something bounded vanishes.
  3. \(o_p(1) + o_p(1) = o_p(1)\) and \(O_p(1) + O_p(1) = O_p(1)\).

These are exactly the steps that let us discard remainder terms in the delta method and in Wilks’ theorem. You do not need their proofs to follow the chapter; treat them as arithmetic for random sequences. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The first tool says continuous functions preserve convergence in distribution. It corresponds to A.13 in the proof catalogue.

Theorem 5.1 (Continuous mapping theorem) Let \(X_n, X\) be random vectors in \(\mathbb R^p\) and let \(g : \mathbb R^p \to \mathbb R^q\) be measurable. Let \(D_g\) be the set of discontinuity points of \(g\) and suppose \(P(X \in D_g) = 0\). Then:

  1. \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X \;\Rightarrow\; g(X_n) \xrightarrow{d} g(X)\);

  2. \(X_n \xrightarrow{p} X \;\Rightarrow\; g(X_n) \xrightarrow{p} g(X)\);

  3. \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X \;\Rightarrow\; g(X_n) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} g(X)\).

Proof. (i) Let \(h : \mathbb R^q \to \mathbb R\) be bounded and continuous. The composition \(h \circ g\) is bounded by \(\|h\|_\infty\), and it is continuous at every point where \(g\) is continuous, hence at every point of \(\mathbb R^p \setminus D_g\), a set of \(P_X\)-probability one. By the second (almost-everywhere) form of the Portmanteau theorem, \(\mathbb E[h(g(X_n))] = \mathbb E[(h\circ g)(X_n)] \to \mathbb E[(h\circ g)(X)] = \mathbb E[h(g(X))]\). As \(h\) was an arbitrary bounded continuous function, the first form of Portmanteau gives \(g(X_n) \xrightarrow{d} g(X)\).

(iii) If \(X_n(\omega) \to X(\omega)\) and \(X(\omega) \notin D_g\), then \(g(X_n(\omega)) \to g(X(\omega))\) by continuity of \(g\) at \(X(\omega)\). This holds for all \(\omega\) outside the union of the null set \(\{X_n \not\to X\}\) and the null set \(\{X \in D_g\}\), which is null; hence \(g(X_n) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} g(X)\).

(ii) Suppose \(g(X_n) \overset{p}{\nrightarrow} g(X)\). Then there exist \(\varepsilon, \eta > 0\) and a subsequence along which \(P(\|g(X_n) - g(X)\| > \varepsilon) > \eta\). Along that subsequence \(X_n \xrightarrow{p} X\) still holds, so it has a further subsequence with \(X_{n_k} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\) (a standard fact: convergence in probability implies almost-sure convergence along a subsequence; see Bingham and Fry (2010)). Intuitively, a sequence converges in probability if and only if every subsequence contains a further subsequence that converges almost surely, and the contradiction we now derive exploits exactly that escape hatch. By part (iii), \(g(X_{n_k}) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} g(X)\), hence \(g(X_{n_k}) \xrightarrow{p} g(X)\), contradicting \(P(\|g(X_{n_k}) - g(X)\| > \varepsilon) > \eta\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. This is the “plug-in” theorem: if an estimate converges, a continuous transformation of it converges too. It is what lets us pass from a converging \(\hat p\) to a converging odds \(\hat p/(1 - \hat p)\), or from a converging log odds ratio to its converging exponential, the odds ratio. One-line example: if \(\bar X_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu\) then \(\bar X_n^2 \xrightarrow{p} \mu^2\). What to remember: continuity transfers convergence, provided the function is well behaved at the limit. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The second tool lets us combine a sequence that converges in distribution with one that converges to a constant. It corresponds to A.12.

Theorem 5.2 (Slutsky’s theorem) Let \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) in \(\mathbb R^p\) and let \(Y_n \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf c\) for a constant \(\mathbf c \in \mathbb R^p\) (respectively a constant matrix \(C_n \xrightarrow{p} C\)). Then \[ X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + \mathbf c, \qquad Y_n^\top X_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf c^\top X, \qquad C_n X_n \xrightarrow{d} C\,X . \] If \(p = 1\) and \(\mathbf c \ne 0\), then \(X_n / Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X/\mathbf c\).

Proof. The key step is that joint convergence holds: \((X_n, Y_n) \xrightarrow{d} (X, \mathbf c)\) in \(\mathbb R^{2p}\). To see this, take a bounded uniformly continuous \(h:\mathbb R^{2p}\to\mathbb R\) with modulus of continuity \(\omega_h(\delta) = \sup\{|h(u)-h(v)| : \|u-v\|\le\delta\}\), which satisfies \(\omega_h(\delta) \to 0\) as \(\delta \downarrow 0\). For any \(\delta > 0\), \[ \bigl|\mathbb E[h(X_n, Y_n)] - \mathbb E[h(X_n, \mathbf c)]\bigr| \le \omega_h(\delta) + 2\|h\|_\infty\, P(\|Y_n - \mathbf c\| > \delta). \] This inequality splits the difference by where \(Y_n\) lands. On the good event \(\{\|Y_n - \mathbf c\| \le \delta\}\) the two points \((X_n, Y_n)\) and \((X_n, \mathbf c)\) are within \(\delta\) of each other, so \(h\) differs between them by at most the modulus \(\omega_h(\delta)\). On the bad event \(\{\|Y_n - \mathbf c\| > \delta\}\) we cannot control the difference, but \(h\) is bounded by \(\|h\|_\infty\), so the difference is at most \(2\|h\|_\infty\), weighted by the probability \(P(\|Y_n - \mathbf c\| > \delta)\) of that event. Letting \(n \to \infty\) and using \(Y_n \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf c\) kills the last term, leaving \(\limsup_n |\mathbb E[h(X_n, Y_n)] - \mathbb E[h(X_n, \mathbf c)]| \le \omega_h(\delta)\); since \(\delta\) is arbitrary, the limsup is zero. Meanwhile \(\mathbb E[h(X_n, \mathbf c)] \to \mathbb E[h(X, \mathbf c)]\) because \(u \mapsto h(u, \mathbf c)\) is bounded and continuous and \(X_n \xrightarrow d X\). Hence \(\mathbb E[h(X_n, Y_n)] \to \mathbb E[h(X, \mathbf c)]\) for all bounded uniformly continuous \(h\), which by the Portmanteau theorem (uniformly continuous test functions suffice) gives \((X_n, Y_n) \xrightarrow{d} (X, \mathbf c)\).

Now apply the continuous mapping theorem (Theorem 5.1). The maps \((x, y) \mapsto x + y\), \((x, y) \mapsto y^\top x\), and (matrix version) \((x, C) \mapsto C x\) are continuous everywhere, so \(X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + \mathbf c\), \(Y_n^\top X_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf c^\top X\), and \(C_n X_n \xrightarrow{d} C X\). The map \((x, y) \mapsto x/y\) is continuous on \(\mathbb R \times (\mathbb R \setminus \{0\})\), and the limit \((X, \mathbf c)\) lands in that set with probability one when \(\mathbf c \ne 0\), so \(X_n/Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X/\mathbf c\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. Slutsky lets us replace an unknown constant by a consistent estimate without changing a limiting distribution. This is why a \(z\)- or \(t\)-statistic still works when we plug an estimated standard error into the denominator: the numerator has a central limit theorem, the denominator converges to a constant, and Slutsky glues them. One-line example: if \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) and \(S_n \xrightarrow{p} \sigma\), then \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)/S_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0, 1)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The third tool transfers a \(\sqrt n\) central limit theorem through a smooth transformation. It is the single most-used result of this chapter in later applications, because every effect measure in the book (log odds ratio, log hazard ratio, risk difference) is a smooth function of fitted parameters. It corresponds to A.11.

Theorem 5.3 (Delta method) Let \(\mathbf T_n\) be a sequence of \(\mathbb R^p\)-valued estimators and \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \mathbb R^p\) a constant with \[ \sqrt n\,(\mathbf T_n - \boldsymbol\theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma). \] Let \(g : \mathbb R^p \to \mathbb R^q\) be differentiable at \(\boldsymbol\theta\) with Jacobian matrix \(\mathbf D = \bigl[\partial g_a / \partial \theta_b\bigr]_{a,b}\) evaluated at \(\boldsymbol\theta\). Then \[ \sqrt n\,\bigl(g(\mathbf T_n) - g(\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_q\!\bigl(\mathbf 0,\; \mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf D^\top\bigr). \tag{5.2}\] In the scalar case \(p = q = 1\) this reads \(\sqrt n\,(g(T_n) - g(\theta)) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N\!\bigl(0,\, g'(\theta)^2 \sigma^2\bigr)\).

Proof. First note that \(\sqrt n\,(\mathbf T_n - \boldsymbol\theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) implies \(\mathbf T_n - \boldsymbol\theta = n^{-1/2}\bigl[\sqrt n(\mathbf T_n - \boldsymbol\theta)\bigr] \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf 0\) by Slutsky (Theorem 5.2) with the constant sequence \(n^{-1/2} \to 0\); hence \(\mathbf T_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta\).

Differentiability of \(g\) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\) means \[ g(\mathbf t) = g(\boldsymbol\theta) + \mathbf D\,(\mathbf t - \boldsymbol\theta) + R(\mathbf t), \qquad \frac{\|R(\mathbf t)\|}{\|\mathbf t - \boldsymbol\theta\|} \to 0 \ \text{ as } \mathbf t \to \boldsymbol\theta, \] with the convention \(R(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbf 0\). Define the function \(\rho(\mathbf t) = \|R(\mathbf t)\| / \|\mathbf t - \boldsymbol\theta\|\) for \(\mathbf t \ne \boldsymbol\theta\) and \(\rho(\boldsymbol\theta) = 0\); then \(\rho\) is continuous at \(\boldsymbol\theta\). Multiplying the expansion at \(\mathbf t = \mathbf T_n\) by \(\sqrt n\), \[ \sqrt n\,\bigl(g(\mathbf T_n) - g(\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr) = \mathbf D\,\underbrace{\sqrt n\,(\mathbf T_n - \boldsymbol\theta)}_{=:\,\mathbf Z_n} \;+\; \sqrt n\, R(\mathbf T_n). \] The remainder is controlled by \(\|\sqrt n\,R(\mathbf T_n)\| = \rho(\mathbf T_n)\,\|\mathbf Z_n\|\). Since \(\mathbf T_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta\) and \(\rho\) is continuous at \(\boldsymbol\theta\) with \(\rho(\boldsymbol\theta) = 0\), the continuous mapping theorem (Theorem 5.1) gives \(\rho(\mathbf T_n) \xrightarrow{p} 0\); and \(\|\mathbf Z_n\| \xrightarrow{d} \|\mathbf Z\|\) where \(\mathbf Z \sim \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\), so \(\|\mathbf Z_n\| = O_p(1)\) (rule 1 of the \(O_p/o_p\) remark above: a convergent-in-distribution sequence is bounded in probability). Hence \(\sqrt n\,R(\mathbf T_n) = \rho(\mathbf T_n)\,\|\mathbf Z_n\| \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf 0\), the product of an \(o_p(1)\) and an \(O_p(1)\) sequence (rule 2). Applying Slutsky once more, \[ \sqrt n\,\bigl(g(\mathbf T_n) - g(\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr) = \mathbf D\,\mathbf Z_n + o_p(1) \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf D\,\mathbf Z . \] Finally \(\mathbf D \mathbf Z \sim \mathcal N_q(\mathbf 0, \mathbf D\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf D^\top)\) because an affine image of a Gaussian is Gaussian with the transformed covariance (Theorem 4.14). This is Equation 5.2. \(\square\)

Remark. The hypothesis \(g'(\theta) \ne 0\) (more generally, \(\mathbf D\) of full row rank) is what makes the limit nondegenerate. If \(g'(\theta) = 0\) the first-order term vanishes and the correct rate is \(n\) rather than \(\sqrt n\), with a \(\chi^2\)-type limit driven by the second derivative; this second-order delta method underlies the asymptotics of the likelihood-ratio statistic in Section 5.9. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why this matters. This is the result that produces a standard error for almost every effect measure you will report. When you fit a model and then transform a coefficient (a probability into a log odds, a rate into a log rate, two coefficients into a ratio), the delta-method formula Equation 5.2 gives the variance of the transform: multiply the variance of the fitted parameter by the squared derivative (scalar case), or sandwich the covariance between the Jacobian \(\mathbf D\) and its transpose (vector case). It is the source of the familiar “standard error of a log odds ratio” and, in Example 5.3, of the standard error of an indirect comparison. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example 5.2 (Delta method for a log odds (a first taste)) Anticipating the running example, the central limit theorem for a proportion gives \(\sqrt n\,(\hat p - p) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0,\, p(1 - p))\). We want the uncertainty of the log odds \(g(p) = \log\frac{p}{1 - p}\), whose derivative is \(g'(p) = \frac{1}{p(1 - p)}\). The scalar delta method (Theorem 5.3) gives \[ \sqrt n\,\bigl(g(\hat p) - g(p)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N\!\Bigl(0,\; g'(p)^2\, p(1 - p)\Bigr) = \mathcal N\!\Bigl(0,\; \tfrac{1}{p(1 - p)}\Bigr), \] so \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\bigl(g(\hat p)\bigr) \approx \frac{1}{n\,\hat p(1 - \hat p)} = \frac{1}{n\hat p} + \frac{1}{n(1 - \hat p)} = \frac{1}{\#\text{responders}} + \frac{1}{\#\text{non-responders}}\). For our trial (\(n = 25\), \(10\) responders) this is \(\tfrac1{10} + \tfrac1{15} = 0.1\overline 6\), so the log odds has standard error \(\sqrt{0.1\overline 6} = 0.408\). The reading is concrete: a parameter on an awkward scale (a probability, bounded in \([0,1]\)) is moved to a convenient unbounded scale (the log odds) and its standard error comes along for free. The full computation, with confidence intervals, returns in Example 5.4. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example 5.3 (A toy indirect treatment comparison) Here is the chapter’s subject in miniature. Two randomized trials share a common control \(A\). Trial 1 compares a new treatment \(B\) against \(A\); trial 2 compares a different treatment \(C\) against \(A\). No trial compares \(B\) with \(C\), yet we want that contrast on the log-odds-ratio scale.

Trial Arm Responders Non-responders
1 \(B\) 20 30
1 \(A\) 10 40
2 \(C\) 18 42
2 \(A\) 15 45

Each arm gives a log odds \(\log\frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p}\) whose variance, by Example 5.2, is (\(1/\text{responders} + 1/\text{non-responders}\)). A log odds ratio is a difference of two independent arm log odds, so its variance is the sum of the four reciprocals. For the direct contrasts, \[ \begin{aligned} \widehat d_{BA} &= \log\tfrac{20/30}{10/40} = \log\tfrac{20\cdot 40}{30\cdot 10} = 0.9808, & \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat d_{BA}) &= \tfrac1{20} + \tfrac1{30} + \tfrac1{10} + \tfrac1{40} = 0.20833,\\ \widehat d_{CA} &= \log\tfrac{18/42}{15/45} = \log\tfrac{18\cdot 45}{42\cdot 15} = 0.2513, & \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat d_{CA}) &= \tfrac1{18} + \tfrac1{42} + \tfrac1{15} + \tfrac1{45} = 0.16825. \end{aligned} \] The indirect contrast of \(B\) versus \(C\) is the difference \(\widehat d_{BC} = \widehat d_{BA} - \widehat d_{CA} = 0.9808 - 0.2513 = 0.7295\). Because the two trials are independent, the variances add (there is no covariance term across independent studies in Equation 5.2): \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat d_{BC}) = 0.20833 + 0.16825 = 0.37658\), so the standard error is \(\sqrt{0.37658} = 0.6137\). A 95% interval is \(0.7295 \pm 1.96 \times 0.6137 = (-0.4733,\, 1.9323)\), and on the odds-ratio scale \((e^{-0.4733}, e^{1.9323}) = (0.62,\, 6.91)\). The interval covers \(1\), so the indirect comparison is inconclusive; and note its standard error (\(0.61\)) exceeds either direct one (\(0.46\) and \(0.41\)): combining two estimates adds uncertainty. This is the Bucher indirect comparison, developed in full in Chapter 14; every number above came from the delta method plus the independence of the two trials. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.3 Likelihood, score, and Fisher information

The likelihood reverses the usual reading of a density. Where \(f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\) is, for fixed \(\boldsymbol\theta\), a function of the data \(x\), the likelihood fixes the observed data and varies the parameter.

Definition 5.4 (Likelihood and log-likelihood) Given observed data \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) from the model Definition 5.1, the likelihood function is \[ L_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i;\boldsymbol\theta), \qquad \boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta, \] viewed as a (random) function of \(\boldsymbol\theta\). The log-likelihood is \[ \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \log L_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f(X_i;\boldsymbol\theta). \] For a single observation we write \(\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; x) = \log f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\), so \(\ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_i \ell(\boldsymbol\theta; X_i)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The likelihood asks, of each candidate \(\boldsymbol\theta\), “how probable were the data we actually saw, if this were the parameter?” High likelihood means good fit. Taking logs turns the product over independent patients into a sum, which is why every formula below is additive over the sample. For the running example, \(\ell_n(p) = \bigl(\textstyle\sum_i X_i\bigr)\log p + \bigl(n - \textstyle\sum_i X_i\bigr)\log(1 - p) = 10\log p + 15\log(1 - p)\), a function of \(p\) alone once the data are in hand. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The slope of the log-likelihood in \(\boldsymbol\theta\) is the score; the MLE is found by setting it to zero.

Definition 5.5 (Score function) The (per-observation) score is the gradient of the single-observation log-likelihood in the parameter, \[ \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; x) = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\,\log f(x;\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}{f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)} \in \mathbb R^{p}, \] defined wherever \(f(x;\boldsymbol\theta) > 0\). The sample score is \[ \mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\,\ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X_i). \tag{5.3}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The score \(\mathbf s_n\) is the slope of the log-likelihood hill in the parameter (\(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\) is the gradient, the vector of partial derivatives in each coordinate of \(\boldsymbol\theta\)). It points in the direction that would increase the fit, and it is zero exactly at the top of the hill, the MLE. Read it as “how strongly, and in which direction, the data push each parameter.” We compute the Bernoulli score just below, in Lemma 5.1. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

All of the clean identities about the score require an interchange of differentiation and integration, which is not automatic. We isolate the needed conditions in one definition and refer back to it.

Definition 5.6 (Regularity conditions) The model Definition 5.1 is regular at an interior point \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \mathrm{int}\,\Theta\) if:

(R1) Common support. The set \(\{x : f(x;\boldsymbol\theta) > 0\}\) does not depend on \(\boldsymbol\theta\).

(R2) Smoothness. For \(\nu\)-almost every \(x\), the map \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\) is twice continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of \(\boldsymbol\theta\).

(R3) Differentiation under the integral. The first two derivatives in \(\boldsymbol\theta\) of \(\int f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\nu(x) = 1\) may be taken inside the integral; concretely there are \(\nu\)-integrable envelopes dominating \(\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\|\) and \(\|\nabla^2_{\boldsymbol\theta} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\|\) uniformly in a neighborhood, so the dominated convergence theorem applies.

(R4) Finite, positive-definite information. The Fisher information Definition 5.7 exists, is finite, and is positive definite at \(\boldsymbol\theta\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation: what R1-R4 are for. These are safety checks that make the log-likelihood smooth enough, and its tails light enough, for calculus to behave. R1 (common support) says the set of possible outcomes does not move as \(\boldsymbol\theta\) changes: true for the Bernoulli, where outcomes are always \(\{0, 1\}\), but false for Uniform\((0,\theta)\), whose range grows with \(\theta\) (Exercise 5.8). R2 (smoothness) says the log-likelihood has a well-defined slope and curvature. R3 (differentiation under the integral) is the license to swap a derivative with an integral, which is what every score identity below secretly uses; the “integrable envelopes” are a domination condition that makes the swap legal. R4 (positive-definite information) says no direction of the parameter is left totally undetermined by the data. Read R1-R4 as “the curve is smooth, its tails are tame, and the parameter is locally pinned down”; the rest is the machinery that turns those words into theorems. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Under (R1)-(R3) the score has mean zero. This is the cornerstone identity from which everything else (the information equality, the Cramér-Rao bound, the asymptotics of the MLE) follows.

Lemma 5.1 (The score has mean zero) If the model is regular (Definition 5.6) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\), then \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl[\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\bigr] = \mathbf 0\).

Proof. Because \(f(\cdot;\boldsymbol\theta)\) integrates to \(1\) for every \(\boldsymbol\theta\), differentiating the identity \(\int f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = 1\) and moving the gradient inside the integral (licensed by (R3)), \[ \mathbf 0 = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} \int f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \int \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \int \frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}{f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}\, f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl[\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\bigr], \] where the third equality multiplies and divides by \(f\) on the set \(\{f > 0\}\), which carries all the mass by (R1). Condition (R1) is doing real work here: because the support \(\{f > 0\}\) does not move with \(\boldsymbol\theta\), differentiating the integral produces no extra term from a \(\boldsymbol\theta\)-moving boundary, and the interchange of \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\) with the integral is clean. (When the support does move with \(\boldsymbol\theta\), as for Uniform\((0,\theta)\) in Exercise 5.8, this lemma fails.) \(\square\)

Remark. What this means, and the Bernoulli score. The score is the slope of the log-likelihood. Saying its average is zero at the truth means: the true parameter is, on average, the value at which the data give no reason to nudge the estimate up or down. That is exactly why the MLE, found by setting the score to zero, aims at the truth. For the running example the single-observation log-likelihood is \(\ell(p; x) = x\log p + (1 - x)\log(1 - p)\), so the score is \[ s(p; x) = \frac{x}{p} - \frac{1 - x}{1 - p} = \frac{x - p}{p(1 - p)}, \] and since \(\mathbb E[X] = p\) its mean is \(\mathbb E[s(p; X)] = (p - p)/(p(1 - p)) = 0\), confirming Lemma 5.1. The numerator \(x - p\) shows the score is positive when a success pushes the estimate up and negative when a failure pulls it down. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Since the score has mean zero, its covariance is just its second moment. That second moment is the Fisher information.

Definition 5.7 (Fisher information) For a regular model the Fisher information matrix (in one observation) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\) is the covariance of the score, \[ \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl(\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\bigr) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\bigl[\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\,\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)^\top\bigr] \in \mathbb R^{p\times p}. \tag{5.4}\] The information in the full sample of size \(n\) is \(\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta))\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Fisher information measures how much one observation tells you about \(\boldsymbol\theta\). There are two equivalent readings, made precise in Theorem 5.4 below: it is the variance of the score (how much the slope of the log-likelihood jumps around from sample to sample) and it is the average curvature of the log-likelihood (how sharply peaked it is). A sharply peaked log-likelihood pins down the parameter tightly, so large information means small standard error. For the Bernoulli, \(\mathcal I(p) = 1/[p(1 - p)]\), which is smallest at \(p = 1/2\) (a 50/50 outcome is the least informative) and blows up near \(0\) or \(1\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Fisher information is always positive semidefinite (it is a covariance matrix, Theorem 4.9), and (R4) asks that it be strictly positive definite, which is the analytic statement that the parameter is locally identifiable: no nonzero direction leaves the log-likelihood flat in expectation.

Theorem 5.4 (Information equality) If the model is regular (Definition 5.6) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\), then the Fisher information equals minus the expected Hessian of the log-likelihood: \[ \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\bigl[\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\,\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)^\top\bigr] = -\,\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\bigl[\nabla^2_{\boldsymbol\theta}\,\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\bigr]. \tag{5.5}\]

Proof. The first equality is Equation 5.4 together with Lemma 5.1 (mean-zero makes the second moment equal the covariance). For the second, start from \(\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; x) = \nabla f / f\) and compute the Hessian of \(\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; x) = \log f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\) by the quotient rule for gradients (the vector version of the scalar quotient rule; if it helps, read the display one component of \(\boldsymbol\theta\) at a time, with \(\nabla^2\) the Hessian, the matrix of second partial derivatives): \[ \nabla^2_{\boldsymbol\theta}\,\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; x) = \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\left(\frac{\nabla f}{f}\right) = \frac{\nabla^2 f}{f} - \frac{(\nabla f)(\nabla f)^\top}{f^2} = \frac{\nabla^2 f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}{f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)} - \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; x)\,\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; x)^\top, \] using \(\nabla f / f = \mathbf s\) in the last term. Take \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\) of both sides. The first term integrates to zero: \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\left[\frac{\nabla^2 f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)}{f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)}\right] = \int \frac{\nabla^2 f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}{f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}\, f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \int \nabla^2 f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \nabla^2 \int f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \nabla^2 (1) = \mathbf 0, \] where the second-derivative interchange is licensed by (R3). Therefore \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[\nabla^2 \ell(\boldsymbol\theta; X)] = \mathbf 0 - \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[\mathbf s \mathbf s^\top] = -\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\), which rearranges to Equation 5.5. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. The information equality is a duality: how variable the slope of the log-likelihood is across samples (the score variance, left side) equals how curved the log-likelihood is on average (minus the expected Hessian, right side). This is the hinge of the whole chapter. It is why the same matrix \(\mathcal I\) appears both as the variance in the central limit theorem for the score and as the curvature that converts that noise into estimator noise; it makes the MLE’s variance equal to \(\mathcal I^{-1}\) (Theorem 5.6), it powers the Cramér-Rao bound, and its failure under misspecification is exactly what forces the sandwich variance of Section 5.8. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The information in independent data adds. This additivity is why precision scales linearly with sample size, and it is the reason the per-observation information \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\) is the natural unit.

Corollary 5.1 (Additivity of information) For iid data the sample score \(\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_i \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X_i)\) has mean zero and \[ \mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl(\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr) = n\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta). \]

Proof. By Lemma 5.1 each summand has mean zero, so \(\mathbb E[\mathbf s_n] = \mathbf 0\). The summands are iid, hence uncorrelated, so the covariance of the sum is the sum of the covariances: \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf s_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X_i)) = n\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\) by Definition 5.7. \(\square\)

Remark (Per-observation information versus sample information (a common source of confusion)). Two informations are now in play, and standard errors depend on getting the factor of \(n\) right. Keep them straight with this table.

Symbol Name Formula Refers to
\(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\) per-observation information \(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X))\) a single observation
\(\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\) sample information \(n\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\) the whole sample of size \(n\)

The variance of the MLE is governed by the sample information: in large samples \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) \approx \mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta)^{-1} = \tfrac1n\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)^{-1}\), and the standard error is the square root of a diagonal entry. The per-observation \(\mathcal I\) is the fixed “information rate”; multiplying by \(n\) and inverting is what makes the standard error shrink like \(1/\sqrt n\). For the running example, \(\mathcal I(p) = 1/[p(1 - p)]\), the sample information is \(\mathcal I_n(p) = n/[p(1 - p)]\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat p) \approx p(1 - p)/n\). At \(\hat p = 0.4\), \(n = 25\): \(\mathcal I_n(\hat p) = 25/0.24 = 104.17\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat p) = 0.24/25 = 0.0096\). A recurring novice slip is to invert \(\mathcal I\) instead of \(\mathcal I_n\), which inflates the standard error by a factor of \(\sqrt n\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.4 Maximum likelihood and its invariance

Definition 5.8 (Maximum likelihood estimator) A maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is any parameter value maximizing the likelihood, equivalently the log-likelihood, \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta} L_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta} \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta). \] When \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) lies in the interior of \(\Theta\) and \(\ell_n\) is differentiable there, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) solves the likelihood (score) equations \(\mathbf s_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The MLE is the parameter value under which the data we actually saw would have been most probable: the top of the log-likelihood hill, where the score (slope) is zero. For the running example this is just \(\hat p = \bar X_n = 0.40\), the sample proportion. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Maximization is invariant under reparameterization, which is one of the most useful practical properties of the MLE: to estimate any function of the parameter by maximum likelihood, just plug in.

Proposition 5.2 (Invariance of the MLE) Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) be an MLE of \(\boldsymbol\theta\).

  1. If \(h : \Theta \to \mathrm H\) is a bijection and the model is reparameterized by \(\boldsymbol\eta = h(\boldsymbol\theta)\), then \(h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) is an MLE of \(\boldsymbol\eta\).

  2. For an arbitrary (not necessarily injective) function \(h : \Theta \to \mathrm H\), define the induced (profile) likelihood \(L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta) = \sup\{L_n(\boldsymbol\theta) : h(\boldsymbol\theta) = \boldsymbol\eta\}\). Then \(h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) maximizes \(L_n^{*}\); we call \(h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) the MLE of \(\boldsymbol\eta = h(\boldsymbol\theta)\).

Proof. (i) Write the likelihood in the new parameterization as \(\tilde L_n(\boldsymbol\eta) = L_n(h^{-1}(\boldsymbol\eta))\). Since \(h^{-1}\) is a bijection of \(\mathrm H\) onto \(\Theta\), ranging \(\boldsymbol\eta\) over \(\mathrm H\) ranges \(h^{-1}(\boldsymbol\eta)\) over all of \(\Theta\), so \(\sup_{\boldsymbol\eta} \tilde L_n(\boldsymbol\eta) = \sup_{\boldsymbol\theta} L_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = L_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\), and the supremum is attained at \(\boldsymbol\eta = h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) because \(\tilde L_n(h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)) = L_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\).

(ii) By construction \(L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta) \le \sup_{\boldsymbol\theta} L_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = L_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) for every \(\boldsymbol\eta\), since each \(L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta)\) is a supremum over a subset of \(\Theta\). Evaluating at \(\boldsymbol\eta^{*} = h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\), the defining set \(\{\boldsymbol\theta : h(\boldsymbol\theta) = \boldsymbol\eta^{*}\}\) contains \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\), so \(L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta^{*}) \ge L_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\). The two inequalities force \(L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta^{*}) = L_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \max_{\boldsymbol\eta} L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta)\), so \(\boldsymbol\eta^{*} = h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) maximizes \(L_n^{*}\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters, and what “profile” means. Part (i) is the workhorse behind every transformed estimate in the book: to get the MLE of an odds ratio, a risk ratio, a hazard ratio, or a marginal effect, you fit the model once and plug the MLE into the transformation; no separate maximization is needed. Part (ii) handles the case where the transformation \(h\) throws away some parameters (nuisance parameters). The profile likelihood \(L_n^{*}(\boldsymbol\eta)\) is built by maximizing out everything except the target \(\boldsymbol\eta\), tracing a one-dimensional ridge (“profile”) across the full likelihood surface; it is the object inverted to get the profile-likelihood confidence intervals of Section 5.9, which is why we record it now even though we do not use it until then. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.5 Consistency of the MLE

Why prove this? As a practitioner you may take for granted that more data means a better estimate. Consistency is the theorem that makes that faith legitimate: it is the guarantee that, as trials grow, the MLE, and with it the network meta-analysis and MAIC estimates of later chapters, stops being systematically wrong and settles on the true value. Without it, none of the asymptotic standard errors below would describe anything real.

We now prove that the MLE converges to the truth. The argument has three clean pieces: a population criterion uniquely maximized at \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) (the information inequality), a uniform law of large numbers transferring the sample criterion to the population one, and an argmax-continuity lemma. The first piece relies on Jensen’s inequality (Theorem 4.6).

Lemma 5.2 (Information inequality) Let \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) be the true parameter and suppose the model is identifiable (Definition 5.1). For every \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta\) with \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\bigl|\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr| < \infty\), \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\!\bigl[\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr] \;\ge\; \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\!\bigl[\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr], \] with equality if and only if \(\boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\). Equivalently, the Kullback-Leibler divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(f_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\,\|\,f_{\boldsymbol\theta}) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\log(f(X;\boldsymbol\theta_0)/f(X;\boldsymbol\theta))]\) is nonnegative and vanishes only at \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\).

Proof. Consider the ratio \(r(X) = f(X;\boldsymbol\theta) / f(X;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\), defined where \(f(X;\boldsymbol\theta_0) > 0\). The function \(\varphi(u) = -\log u\) is strictly convex on \((0,\infty)\). By Jensen’s inequality (Theorem 4.6), \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\!\bigl[-\log r(X)\bigr] \;\ge\; -\log \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[r(X)]. \] The right-hand expectation is \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[r(X)] = \int_{\{f_{\boldsymbol\theta_0} > 0\}} \frac{f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)}{f(x;\boldsymbol\theta_0)}\, f(x;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\, d\nu(x) = \int_{\{f_{\boldsymbol\theta_0} > 0\}} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) \le 1, \] so \(-\log \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[r(X)] \ge 0\). Therefore \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[-\log r(X)] = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta_0)] - \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)] \ge 0\), which is the claimed inequality and shows \(\mathrm{KL} \ge 0\).

For equality: strict convexity of \(\varphi\) makes Jensen an equality only if \(r(X)\) is \(P_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\)-almost surely constant, say \(r(X) = c\). Then \(f(X;\boldsymbol\theta) = c\, f(X;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) a.s., and integrating both sides forces \(c = 1\) (both are densities), so \(f(\cdot;\boldsymbol\theta) = f(\cdot;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) \(\nu\)-almost everywhere. By identifiability this means \(\boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\). Conversely \(\boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\) gives equality trivially. \(\square\)

The population criterion is \(M(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)]\) and the sample criterion is \(M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \tfrac1n \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \tfrac1n \sum_i \log f(X_i;\boldsymbol\theta)\). For each fixed \(\boldsymbol\theta\) the WLLN gives \(M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) \xrightarrow{p} M(\boldsymbol\theta)\); to control the maximizer we need this uniformly in \(\boldsymbol\theta\). We state the uniform law as a cited prerequisite.

Lemma 5.3 (Uniform law of large numbers) Let \(\Theta\) be compact, let \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto \log f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\) be continuous for \(\nu\)-almost every \(x\), and suppose the envelope condition \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\bigl[\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta} |\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)|\bigr] < \infty\) holds. Then \(M\) is continuous on \(\Theta\) and \[ \sup_{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta} \bigl|M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) - M(\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr| \xrightarrow{p} 0 . \]

Proof. This is a standard consequence of compactness, almost-everywhere continuity, and the integrable envelope; the proof covers \(\Theta\) by finitely many small balls, applies the pointwise WLLN at the centers, and controls the oscillation inside each ball with the envelope and dominated convergence. See Bingham and Fry (2010) and a standard text on asymptotic statistics; we take it as given. \(\square\)

Lemma 5.4 (Argmax consistency) Let \(M : \Theta \to \mathbb R\) be continuous on a compact set \(\Theta\) with a unique maximizer \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\). Let \(M_n\) be random functions with \(\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta} |M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) - M(\boldsymbol\theta)| \xrightarrow{p} 0\), and let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \in \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta} M_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\). Then \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta_0\).

Proof. Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\) and let \(B_\varepsilon = \{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta : \|\boldsymbol\theta - \boldsymbol\theta_0\| \ge \varepsilon\}\). This set is closed, hence compact, so the continuous \(M\) attains its maximum over \(B_\varepsilon\) at some point; because \(\boldsymbol\theta_0 \notin B_\varepsilon\) and \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) is the unique maximizer of \(M\), this maximum is strictly below \(M(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\). Set \[ \delta = M(\boldsymbol\theta_0) - \sup_{\boldsymbol\theta \in B_\varepsilon} M(\boldsymbol\theta) > 0 . \] Let \(U_n = \sup_{\boldsymbol\theta} |M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) - M(\boldsymbol\theta)|\) and work on the event \(\{U_n < \delta/2\}\). On this event, for any \(\boldsymbol\theta \in B_\varepsilon\), \[ M_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) \ge M_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) > M(\boldsymbol\theta_0) - \tfrac{\delta}{2} = \sup_{B_\varepsilon} M + \tfrac{\delta}{2} \ge M(\boldsymbol\theta) + \tfrac{\delta}{2} > M_n(\boldsymbol\theta), \] using, in order, that \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) maximizes \(M_n\), the bound \(U_n < \delta/2\) at \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\), the definition of \(\delta\), and again \(U_n < \delta/2\) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\). Thus no point of \(B_\varepsilon\) can be the maximizer of \(M_n\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \notin B_\varepsilon\), that is \(\|\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0\| < \varepsilon\). Hence \[ P\bigl(\|\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0\| \ge \varepsilon\bigr) \le P\bigl(U_n \ge \tfrac{\delta}{2}\bigr) \to 0 , \] by the uniform convergence hypothesis. As \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta_0\). \(\square\)

Theorem 5.5 (Consistency of the MLE) Suppose \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are iid from \(f(\cdot;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) and:

(C1) \(\Theta \subseteq \mathbb R^p\) is compact and \(\boldsymbol\theta_0 \in \Theta\);

(C2) the model is identifiable (Definition 5.1);

(C3) \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto \log f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\) is continuous for \(\nu\)-almost every \(x\);

(C4) the envelope condition \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\bigl[\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\Theta}|\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)|\bigr] < \infty\) holds.

Then any MLE sequence \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) satisfies \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta_0\).

Proof. The MLE maximizes \(M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \tfrac1n \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\) (dividing the log-likelihood by \(n\) does not move the argmax). By Lemma 5.2 and identifiability (C2), the population criterion \(M(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\log f(X;\boldsymbol\theta)]\) has a unique maximizer at \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\); the envelope (C4) makes \(M\) finite, and with (C3) it is continuous by Lemma 5.3, which simultaneously gives the uniform convergence \(\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta}|M_n(\boldsymbol\theta) - M(\boldsymbol\theta)| \xrightarrow{p} 0\). Compactness (C1), continuity, the unique maximum, and uniform convergence are exactly the hypotheses of Lemma 5.4, which yields \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta_0\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters, with the running example. For the Bernoulli the hypotheses are easy to check: \(\Theta = [0, 1]\) is compact (C1), the model is identifiable since different \(p\) give different distributions (C2), \(\log f(x; p)\) is continuous in \(p\) (C3), and the envelope holds, so the theorem gives \(\hat p_n \xrightarrow{p} p_0\). In words, the trial’s response rate converges to the true response rate. Translated to the book’s subject, this is what guarantees that, with enough patients, an indirect comparison built from such estimates converges to the true contrast rather than to a biased value. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Compactness of \(\Theta\) (C1) is the only restrictive condition, and it can be relaxed. The standard device is to show the log-likelihood is eventually larger at \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) than outside a compact set (so the maximizer cannot escape to the boundary), then apply the theorem on the compact core. Alternatively, the Cramér approach abandons the global maximizer and proves that the score equation \(\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbf 0\) has, with probability tending to one, a root within \(O_p(n^{-1/2})\) of \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\); that local root is consistent and asymptotically normal even when the global MLE is ill-behaved. We use the global form here for definiteness. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.6 Asymptotic normality of the MLE

Consistency says the MLE lands near \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\). Asymptotic normality describes the distribution of the residual error \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0\), rescaled by \(\sqrt n\). The proof is the canonical “Taylor-expand the score” argument: the score vanishes at the MLE, expand it about the truth, and read off a ratio of a central-limit term over an information term.

Theorem 5.6 (Asymptotic normality of the MLE) Suppose \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta_0\) (for instance by Theorem 5.5), and in addition:

(A1) \(\boldsymbol\theta_0 \in \mathrm{int}\,\Theta\) and the model is regular (Definition 5.6) on a neighborhood of \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\), so \(\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; x)\) is twice continuously differentiable there;

(A2) the Fisher information \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) is finite and positive definite;

(A3) the Hessian satisfies a local uniform law of large numbers: there is a neighborhood \(\mathcal U\) of \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) with \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}\bigl[\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathcal U}\|\nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\theta;X)\|\bigr] < \infty\), so that \(\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathcal U}\|\tfrac1n\sum_i \nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\theta;X_i) - \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\theta;X)]\| \xrightarrow{p} 0\).

Then, with probability tending to one \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) solves \(\mathbf s_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0\), and \[ \sqrt n\,\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p\!\bigl(\mathbf 0,\; \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\bigr). \tag{5.6}\]

Proof. Step 1 (the estimator solves the score equation). Since \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta_0\) and \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) is interior (A1), with probability tending to one \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) lies in the open neighborhood \(\mathcal U\) where \(\ell_n\) is differentiable and is an interior maximizer, so the first-order condition \(\mathbf s_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \nabla\ell_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0\) holds. We work on this event henceforth.

Step 2 (exact first-order expansion with integral remainder). Apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to the gradient field \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto \mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\) along the segment from \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\), both eventually in \(\mathcal U\). (This is the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to each component of the vector field \(\mathbf s_n\) along the path; the bracketed matrix \(\bar{\mathbf H}_n\) is the Hessian averaged over the segment, playing the role of the mean-value remainder familiar from the scalar Taylor expansion.) With \(\mathbf H_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \nabla^2 \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \nabla \mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\), \[ \mathbf 0 = \mathbf s_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) + \underbrace{\left[\int_0^1 \mathbf H_n\bigl(\boldsymbol\theta_0 + t(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr)\, dt\right]}_{=:\; \bar{\mathbf H}_n}\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0). \] Define \(\mathbf A_n = -\tfrac1n \bar{\mathbf H}_n\). Then, multiplying by \(-1/n\) and rearranging, \[ \mathbf A_n\,\sqrt n\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\,\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0). \tag{5.7}\]

Step 3 (the score term is asymptotically normal). Write \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = \sqrt n\,\bigl(\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta_0; X_i) - \mathbf 0\bigr)\). The per-observation scores \(\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta_0; X_i)\) are iid with mean \(\mathbf 0\) (Lemma 5.1) and covariance \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) (Definition 5.7), finite by (A2). The multivariate CLT gives \[ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\,\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf Z, \qquad \mathbf Z \sim \mathcal N_p\bigl(\mathbf 0,\, \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr). \tag{5.8}\]

Step 4 (the averaged Hessian converges to the information). Each intermediate point \(\boldsymbol\theta_0 + t(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\) lies between \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\), hence converges in probability to \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) uniformly in \(t \in [0,1]\). By the local uniform law (A3), the map \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto -\tfrac1n\sum_i \nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; X_i)\) converges in probability, uniformly over \(\mathcal U\), to the continuous limit \(\boldsymbol\theta \mapsto -\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta_0}[\nabla^2\ell(\boldsymbol\theta; X)]\), which at \(\boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\) equals \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) by the information equality (Theorem 5.4). Combining uniform convergence with \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p}\boldsymbol\theta_0\) and continuity of the limit, \[ \mathbf A_n = -\tfrac1n \int_0^1 \mathbf H_n\bigl(\boldsymbol\theta_0 + t(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr)\, dt \xrightarrow{p} \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0). \tag{5.9}\]

Step 5 (solve and apply Slutsky). By (A2) the limit \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) is invertible. Matrix inversion is continuous at any nonsingular matrix, so the continuous mapping theorem (Theorem 5.1) gives \(\mathbf A_n^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\) (in particular \(\mathbf A_n\) is invertible with probability tending to one). Solving Equation 5.7 and applying Slutsky (Theorem 5.2) to the product of the convergent-in-probability matrix \(\mathbf A_n^{-1}\) and the convergent-in-distribution vector Equation 5.8, \[ \sqrt n\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0) = \mathbf A_n^{-1}\, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\,\mathbf Z . \] Finally, the affine (here linear) image of a Gaussian is Gaussian (Theorem 4.14): with \(\mathbf B = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\) and \(\mathbf Z \sim \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0))\), \[ \mathbf B\mathbf Z \sim \mathcal N_p\bigl(\mathbf 0,\; \mathbf B\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\,\mathbf B^\top\bigr) = \mathcal N_p\bigl(\mathbf 0,\; \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\bigr) = \mathcal N_p\bigl(\mathbf 0,\; \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\bigr), \] using the symmetry of \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\). This is Equation 5.6. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. This theorem is the source of the ubiquitous “estimate \(\pm 1.96\) standard errors” interval. It says the MLE is approximately \(\mathcal N_p(\boldsymbol\theta_0,\, \tfrac1n\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1})\) in large samples, so every Wald confidence interval and \(z\)-test in a regression printout is an application of Equation 5.6. The idea behind the proof, stripped of detail: at the maximum the slope (score) is zero; near the truth the slope is approximately linear, with slope equal to the curvature (information); solving that linear relation turns the central-limit noise in the score into Gaussian noise in the estimator, scaled by the inverse information. For the running example, \(\sqrt n(\hat p - p) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0,\, p(1 - p))\) because \(\mathcal I(p)^{-1} = p(1 - p)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (Estimating the asymptotic variance). Equation 5.6 says \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) is approximately \(\mathcal N_p(\boldsymbol\theta_0,\, \tfrac1n \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1})\) in large samples, so a standard error for the \(b\)-th coordinate is the square root of the \((b,b)\) entry of \(\tfrac1n \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\). In practice \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) is unknown, so one of two consistent plug-ins is used: the expected information evaluated at the estimate, \(\mathcal I(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\), or the observed information \(\mathbf J_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = -\nabla^2 \ell_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\), which by Step 4 satisfies \(\tfrac1n \mathbf J_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) \xrightarrow{p} \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\). Either yields a valid asymptotic variance estimate by Slutsky; the observed information requires no expectation and is what most software reports. The two agree in large samples; they can differ in small samples or under departures from the model, where the observed form uses the actual curvature at your estimate while the expected form averages curvature over hypothetical datasets. Concretely, what software prints as a “standard error” is the square root of a diagonal entry of the inverted observed information \(\mathbf J_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)^{-1}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.7 The Cramér-Rao lower bound and efficiency

Why a lower bound matters for indirect comparisons. Every method in this book reports a standard error, and a natural question is whether it could have done better with the same data. The Cramér-Rao bound is the yardstick: it is the smallest variance any unbiased estimator can achieve under a correctly specified regular model, so when MAIC or a network meta-analysis reports a precision you can ask whether it is near this optimum or is leaving information on the table. It is a theoretical benchmark, not something you compute on each dataset, but it explains why the inverse Fisher information is the natural ideal variance.

How small can the variance of an unbiased estimator be? The Cramér-Rao bound answers: no smaller than the inverse Fisher information. The proof is a single application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Theorem 2.1), using that the score has mean zero.

Theorem 5.7 (Cramér-Rao lower bound) Let the model be regular (Definition 5.6) at \(\boldsymbol\theta\), and let \(T = T(X)\) be a statistic with \(\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T) < \infty\) whose mean \(g(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T]\) is differentiable with the differentiation passing under the integral sign.

  1. Scalar parameter (\(p = 1\)). Then \[ \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T) \;\ge\; \frac{g'(\boldsymbol\theta)^2}{\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)} . \tag{5.10}\] In particular, if \(T\) is unbiased for \(\boldsymbol\theta\) (so \(g(\boldsymbol\theta) = \boldsymbol\theta\), \(g' = 1\)), then \(\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T) \ge 1/\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\). Equality holds if and only if \(T - g(\boldsymbol\theta)\) is, almost surely, a (deterministic) multiple of the score \(\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\).

  2. Vector parameter. If \(\mathbf T\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \mathbb R^p\), then in the Loewner (positive-semidefinite) order \[ \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T) \;\succeq\; \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)^{-1} , \tag{5.11}\] that is, \(\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T) - \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)^{-1}\) is positive semidefinite. Here \(A \succeq B\) (the Loewner order) means \(A - B\) is positive semidefinite; concretely it says every scalar combination \(\mathbf v^\top\mathbf T\) has variance at least \(\mathbf v^\top\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)^{-1}\mathbf v\), so the estimator beats the bound in no direction.

Proof. (i) Differentiate the identity \(g(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T] = \int T(x)\, f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x)\) under the integral sign: \[ g'(\boldsymbol\theta) = \int T(x)\, \frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta} f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \int T(x)\, \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; x)\, f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\nu(x) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\bigl[T\,\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\bigr], \] using \(\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta} f = \mathbf s\, f\). Because the score has mean zero (Lemma 5.1), \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T\,\mathbf s] = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T, \mathbf s)\), so \(g'(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T, \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X))\). The space of mean-zero square-integrable random variables under \(P_{\boldsymbol\theta}\) is an inner product space with \(\langle U, V\rangle = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(U, V)\); applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Theorem 2.1) to \(T - g(\boldsymbol\theta)\) and the score, \[ g'(\boldsymbol\theta)^2 = \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T, \mathbf s)^2 \le \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T)\,\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf s) = \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(T)\, \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta) . \] Dividing by \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta) > 0\) gives Equation 5.10. Cauchy-Schwarz is an equality exactly when the two vectors are linearly dependent, that is when \(T - g(\boldsymbol\theta) = a(\boldsymbol\theta)\,\mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\) almost surely for some constant \(a(\boldsymbol\theta)\).

(ii) Let \(\mathbf s = \mathbf s(\boldsymbol\theta; X)\) and center \(\mathbf U = \mathbf T - \boldsymbol\theta\) (mean zero by unbiasedness). The componentwise version of the scalar computation gives, for each pair \((a, b)\), \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[U_a\, s_b] = \partial_{\theta_b}\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[T_a] = \partial_{\theta_b}\theta_a = \mathbb 1\{a = b\}\), so the cross-covariance is the identity: \(\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T, \mathbf s) = \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\theta}[\mathbf U \mathbf s^\top] = I_p\). Stack \(\mathbf U\) and \(\mathbf s\) into a \(2p\)-vector; its covariance matrix is positive semidefinite (Theorem 4.9): \[ \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf U \\ \mathbf s\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf T) & I_p \\[2pt] I_p & \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta) \end{pmatrix} =: \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_T & I_p \\ I_p & \mathcal I \end{pmatrix} \succeq 0 . \] Since \(\mathcal I \succ 0\), evaluate the quadratic form of this block matrix on the cleverly chosen vector \((\,\mathbf v^\top,\; -(\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf v)^\top)^\top\) for arbitrary \(\mathbf v \in \mathbb R^p\). The second block \(-\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf v\) is engineered so that the identity cross-blocks cancel, leaving only the difference \(\Sigma_T - \mathcal I^{-1}\); this cancellation is the algebraic content of the Schur complement of the \(\mathcal I\) block: \[ 0 \le \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf v \\ -\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf v\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\! \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_T & I_p \\ I_p & \mathcal I \end{pmatrix}\! \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf v \\ -\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf v\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf v^\top \Sigma_T \mathbf v - 2\,\mathbf v^\top \mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf v + \mathbf v^\top \mathcal I^{-1}\mathcal I\,\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf v = \mathbf v^\top\bigl(\Sigma_T - \mathcal I^{-1}\bigr)\mathbf v . \] As \(\mathbf v\) was arbitrary, \(\Sigma_T - \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)^{-1} \succeq 0\), which is Equation 5.11. (This is precisely the Schur-complement positivity of the block matrix.) \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. Read the scalar bound Equation 5.10 as: precision is capped by information. You cannot make an unbiased estimator arbitrarily precise; the best achievable variance is \(1/\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = 1/[n\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)]\), which is why “more information” and “more data” both mean “smaller standard error.” When a later method attains this bound (the MLE does, asymptotically), you know no regular competitor can do better with the same model and data. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The bound motivates a precise notion of “best possible” estimator.

Definition 5.9 (Efficiency and asymptotic efficiency) An unbiased estimator \(T\) of a scalar \(g(\boldsymbol\theta)\) is (Cramér-Rao) efficient if it attains the bound Equation 5.10 with equality for all \(\boldsymbol\theta\). A consistent estimator sequence \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) with \(\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \mathbf V(\boldsymbol\theta_0))\) is asymptotically efficient if \(\mathbf V(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\), the smallest asymptotic covariance permitted (in the Loewner order) for regular estimators. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Corollary 5.2 (The MLE is asymptotically efficient) Under the hypotheses of Theorem 5.6, the MLE is asymptotically efficient: its asymptotic covariance \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\) equals the per-observation Cramér-Rao bound.

Proof. Immediate by comparing Equation 5.6 with Definition 5.9: the asymptotic covariance of \(\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\) is \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. Asymptotic efficiency is the precise sense in which maximum likelihood is “as good as it gets”: no regular estimator can have a smaller asymptotic variance than the MLE’s \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\). The practical payoff is reassurance: when a systematic review uses likelihood-based methods (most network meta-analyses, and ML-NMR), it is not leaving precision on the table relative to any competing regular estimator built from the same model. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The matrix bound Equation 5.11 is a finite-sample statement about unbiased estimators, while Definition 5.9’s asymptotic optimality is a statement about the limiting distribution of possibly biased estimators. The rigorous bridge that no regular estimator can have asymptotic covariance below \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\) is the Hájek-Le Cam convolution theorem and the local asymptotic minimax theorem; we cite these (see Bingham and Fry (2010) and the asymptotic-statistics literature) rather than prove them, as they require the local asymptotic normality framework beyond this chapter. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.8 M-estimation and the sandwich variance

Maximum likelihood is the special case, with \(\boldsymbol\psi\) equal to the score, of a far more general construction: define an estimator as the solution of an estimating equation. This generality is exactly what later chapters need. A MAIC estimator (Chapter 18) solves a weighting equation that is not the score of any correctly specified likelihood, yet it is asymptotically normal, and its correct standard error is the sandwich variance defined below.

Definition 5.10 (M-estimator and estimating equation) Let \(\boldsymbol\psi(x; \boldsymbol\theta) \in \mathbb R^p\) be a known estimating function. An M-estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) is a solution of the estimating equation \[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0 . \tag{5.12}\] The target (or estimand) is the value \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\) solving the population equation \(\mathbb E_{P}[\boldsymbol\psi(X; \boldsymbol\theta^{*})] = \mathbf 0\), where \(P\) is the true data-generating distribution, which need not belong to the working model. The MLE is the M-estimator with \(\boldsymbol\psi = \mathbf s\), the score, in which case \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*} = \boldsymbol\theta_0\) under correct specification. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. An M-estimator is defined not by maximizing something but by solving an equation \(\sum_i \boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0\). Choosing \(\boldsymbol\psi\) to be the score recovers maximum likelihood, but \(\boldsymbol\psi\) can be any sensible estimating function, even one with no likelihood behind it. The crucial shift is the target \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\): it is whatever value the equation aims at in the real world \(P\), which need not be the “true parameter” of any correct model. This is the honest setting for MAIC, whose weighting equation is a computational device, not a believed model. A one-covariate MAIC balancing equation has the schematic form \(\sum_i w_i(\boldsymbol\theta)\,(x_i - \bar x^{*}) = 0\), “choose weights so the weighted covariate mean equals the target population mean \(\bar x^{*}\)”; Chapter 18 makes this precise. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The asymptotic distribution of an M-estimator follows the same Taylor expansion as the MLE, but the variance no longer collapses to a single information matrix; it is a sandwich of two matrices.

Theorem 5.8 (Asymptotic normality of M-estimators) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be iid from \(P\), let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) solve Equation 5.12, and suppose \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol\theta^{*}\), where \(\mathbb E_P[\boldsymbol\psi(X; \boldsymbol\theta^{*})] = \mathbf 0\). Assume \(\boldsymbol\psi(x; \cdot)\) is continuously differentiable near \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\), the matrix \[ \mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}) = -\,\mathbb E_P\!\bigl[\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\psi(X; \boldsymbol\theta^{*})\bigr] \quad(\text{the ``bread''}) \] is nonsingular, the matrix \(\mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}) = \mathbb E_P\!\bigl[\boldsymbol\psi(X; \boldsymbol\theta^{*})\,\boldsymbol\psi(X; \boldsymbol\theta^{*})^\top\bigr]\) (the ``meat’’) is finite, and \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\psi\) obeys a local uniform law of large numbers near \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\): there is a neighborhood \(\mathcal U^{*}\) of \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\) with \(\mathbb E_P\bigl[\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathcal U^{*}}\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\psi(X;\boldsymbol\theta)\|\bigr] < \infty\) and \(\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathcal U^{*}}\bigl\|\tfrac1n\sum_i \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\psi(X_i;\boldsymbol\theta) - \mathbb E_P[\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\psi(X;\boldsymbol\theta)]\bigr\| \xrightarrow{p} 0\), exactly parallel to (A3) of Theorem 5.6. Then \[ \sqrt n\,\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta^{*}\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p\!\Bigl(\mathbf 0,\; \mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})^{-1}\,\mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})\,\mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})^{-\top}\Bigr) , \tag{5.13}\] where \(\mathbf A^{-\top}\) abbreviates \((\mathbf A^{-1})^\top = (\mathbf A^\top)^{-1}\).

Proof. Write \(\boldsymbol\Psi_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \boldsymbol\theta)\), so \(\boldsymbol\Psi_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0\). As in Step 2 of Theorem 5.6, the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment from \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\) to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) gives \[ \mathbf 0 = \boldsymbol\Psi_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \boldsymbol\Psi_n(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}) + \Bigl[\textstyle\int_0^1 \nabla\boldsymbol\Psi_n\bigl(\boldsymbol\theta^{*} + t(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta^{*})\bigr)\, dt\Bigr](\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta^{*}), \] hence with \(\mathbf A_n = -\tfrac1n\int_0^1 \nabla\boldsymbol\Psi_n(\boldsymbol\theta^{*} + t(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta^{*}))\,dt\), \[ \mathbf A_n\,\sqrt n\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta^{*}) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\,\boldsymbol\Psi_n(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}). \] The summands \(\boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \boldsymbol\theta^{*})\) are iid with mean \(\mathbf 0\) (by the definition of \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}\)) and covariance \(\mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})\), so the multivariate CLT gives \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\boldsymbol\Psi_n(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}))\). By consistency, the local uniform law, and continuity (exactly as in Step 4 of Theorem 5.6), \(\mathbf A_n \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})\), which is nonsingular, so \(\mathbf A_n^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})^{-1}\) by continuous mapping (Theorem 5.1). Slutsky (Theorem 5.2) and Theorem 4.14 give \[ \sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta^{*}) = \mathbf A_n^{-1}\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\boldsymbol\Psi_n(\boldsymbol\theta^{*}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf A^{-1}\mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \mathbf B) = \mathcal N_p\bigl(\mathbf 0, \mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf B\,\mathbf A^{-\top}\bigr), \] which is Equation 5.13. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters, and the “bread”/“meat” nicknames. When the model may be wrong, the MLE variance \(\mathcal I^{-1}\) is no longer correct; the right variance is the sandwich \(\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf B\,\mathbf A^{-\top}\). The names are conventional and purely mnemonic, not technical terms: \(\mathbf A\) (the “bread”) measures how sensitive the estimating equation is to the parameter (the average slope of \(\boldsymbol\psi\)), while \(\mathbf B\) (the “meat”) measures how variable the estimating equation itself is (the covariance of \(\boldsymbol\psi\)). The two outer copies of \(\mathbf A^{-1}\) are the bread, the central \(\mathbf B\) is the meat; together they describe the shape \(\mathbf A^{-1}\,\mathbf B\,\mathbf A^{-\top}\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 5.11 (Sandwich (robust) variance estimator) Under Theorem 5.8, the sandwich variance estimator of \(\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\) is the empirical plug-in of Equation 5.13, \[ \widehat{\mathbb V}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \tfrac1n\,\widehat{\mathbf A}_n^{-1}\,\widehat{\mathbf B}_n\,\widehat{\mathbf A}_n^{-\top}, \tag{5.14}\] with \[ \widehat{\mathbf A}_n = -\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n), \qquad \widehat{\mathbf B}_n = \tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\,\boldsymbol\psi(X_i; \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)^\top . \] The name records the structure \(\widehat{\mathbf A}_n^{-1}\) (bread), \(\widehat{\mathbf B}_n\) (meat), \(\widehat{\mathbf A}_n^{-\top}\) (bread). Under the regularity above, the law of large numbers and the continuous mapping theorem give \(\widehat{\mathbf A}_n \xrightarrow{p}\mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})\) and \(\widehat{\mathbf B}_n \xrightarrow{p}\mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta^{*})\), so \(n\,\widehat{\mathbb V}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) \xrightarrow{p}\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf B\,\mathbf A^{-\top}\): the sandwich estimator is consistent for the true asymptotic variance even when the working model is misspecified. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation, with a picture. The estimator is three matrices multiplied in a row: plug in the empirical bread \(\widehat{\mathbf A}_n\) and meat \(\widehat{\mathbf B}_n\) computed from the data at the fitted \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\), and you get a standard error valid even when the working model is wrong.

When the model is right, the information equality forces bread \(=\) meat \(= \mathcal I\) and the sandwich collapses to \(\mathcal I^{-1}\), the ordinary inverse-information variance (next corollary). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The information equality is exactly the statement that, under correct specification, the sandwich collapses to the familiar inverse-information variance.

Corollary 5.3 (Correct specification collapses the sandwich) Suppose \(\boldsymbol\psi = \mathbf s\) is the score of a correctly specified regular model, so \(P = f(\cdot;\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) and \(\boldsymbol\theta^{*} = \boldsymbol\theta_0\). Then \(\mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = \mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\), and the sandwich variance Equation 5.13 reduces to \(\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\), recovering Theorem 5.6.

Proof. The meat is \(\mathbf B(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = \mathbb E[\mathbf s\,\mathbf s^\top] = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) by Definition 5.7. The bread is \(\mathbf A(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = -\mathbb E[\nabla\mathbf s] = -\mathbb E[\nabla^2 \ell] = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) by the information equality (Theorem 5.4). Hence \(\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf B\,\mathbf A^{-\top} = \mathcal I^{-1}\mathcal I\,\mathcal I^{-1} = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\). \(\square\)

Remark (The sandwich and MAIC). When the model is misspecified, \(\mathbf A \ne \mathbf B\) in general, and using the naive inverse-bread or inverse-information variance understates (or overstates) the truth. The sandwich Equation 5.14 is the universal fix.

This is the precise reason Chapter 18 reports robust standard errors for MAIC. In a matching-adjusted indirect comparison the individual-level study fits weights \(w_i = \exp(\alpha + \boldsymbol\beta^\top \mathbf x_i)\) by an estimating equation that forces the weighted covariate means to match the aggregate target population, schematically \(\sum_i w_i(\boldsymbol\beta)\,(\mathbf x_i - \bar{\mathbf x}^{*}) = \mathbf 0\); the treatment effect is then estimated on the weighted sample. The “working model” here is the weighting scheme together with the outcome model on the reweighted data, and there is no reason for it to be correctly specified: the weights are a device for balancing covariates, not a belief about how the data arose. Maximum-likelihood (inverse-information) standard errors would therefore be wrong, and only the sandwich form, with \(\boldsymbol\psi\) stacking the weighting equations and the outcome equation, is guaranteed consistent for the sampling variance of the population-adjusted effect. Carry this picture into Chapter 18: weights solve an estimating equation, the model is a working approximation, and the sandwich is the honest standard error. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.9 Tests and confidence intervals

We close with the three classical large-sample tests of a null hypothesis \(H_0 : \boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\) (or, after reparameterization, a value of a subvector), and the confidence intervals obtained by inverting them. All three statistics have the same limiting \(\chi^2_p\) distribution and are first-order equivalent; they differ in whether they need the estimate, the null value, or both. Throughout, \(\mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta) = n\,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)\) is the sample information (Corollary 5.1).

In a health application the practical question is which test to report for an indirect comparison. The short answer: the three agree in large samples (Theorem 5.9), but near a boundary or in small studies the likelihood-ratio and score versions are usually preferred (see the remark after Theorem 5.9). The table contrasts what each test looks at.

Test What it evaluates Needs the MLE \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\)? Needs the null fit? Intuitive question
Wald distance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) from \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\), scaled by curvature at \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) yes no “How many standard errors is the estimate from the null?”
Score (Rao) slope of the log-likelihood evaluated at \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) no yes “Is the log-likelihood still climbing steeply at the null?”
Likelihood ratio drop in log-likelihood from \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) down to \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) yes yes “How much worse does the null fit than the best fit?”

Definition 5.12 (Wald, score, and likelihood-ratio statistics) For testing \(H_0 : \boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\) with MLE \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\):

  • the Wald statistic is \[ W_n = (\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)^\top\, \mathcal I_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0); \tag{5.15}\]

  • the score (Rao) statistic is \[ R_n = \mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^\top\, \mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0)^{-1}\, \mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0); \tag{5.16}\]

  • the likelihood-ratio statistic is \[ \Lambda_n = 2\bigl[\ell_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) - \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr] . \tag{5.17}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. All three compare a null value \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) to what the data prefer, using the geometry of the log-likelihood near its peak (recall the parabola picture from the start of the chapter). Wald measures the horizontal distance from the peak to the null; the score measures the slope at the null; the likelihood ratio measures the vertical drop in height. Near the peak a smooth function looks like a parabola, and for a parabola these three are the same measurement up to scaling, which is why Theorem 5.9 finds them asymptotically equal. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 5.9 (Limiting null distribution (Wilks)) Under \(H_0\) and the hypotheses of Theorem 5.6, each of \(W_n\), \(R_n\), and \(\Lambda_n\) converges in distribution to \(\chi^2_p\), and the three are asymptotically equivalent: \(W_n - \Lambda_n \xrightarrow{p} 0\) and \(R_n - \Lambda_n \xrightarrow{p} 0\).

Proof. Abbreviate \(\mathcal I = \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta_0)\) and let \(\mathbf Z_n = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\mathbf s_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf Z \sim \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, \mathcal I)\) by Equation 5.8. From the proof of Theorem 5.6, \[ \sqrt n\,(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0) = \mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z_n + o_p(1). \tag{5.18}\]

Score statistic. Directly, \(R_n = \mathbf Z_n^\top (\tfrac1n \mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0))^{-1}\mathbf Z_n = \mathbf Z_n^\top \mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z_n\), and by continuous mapping (Theorem 5.1) applied to the continuous map \(\mathbf z \mapsto \mathbf z^\top\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf z\), \(R_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf Z^\top \mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z\). Writing \(\mathbf Z = \mathcal I^{1/2}\mathbf G\) with \(\mathbf G \sim \mathcal N_p(\mathbf 0, I_p)\) (the symmetric PSD square root, Theorem 4.9), \(\mathbf Z^\top\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z = \mathbf G^\top\mathbf G = \|\mathbf G\|_2^2 \sim \chi^2_p\). Hence \(R_n \xrightarrow{d}\chi^2_p\).

Wald statistic. Since \(\tfrac1n\mathcal I_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathcal I(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) \xrightarrow{p}\mathcal I\) (continuity of \(\mathcal I\) and consistency), substituting Equation 5.18, \[ W_n = \bigl[\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr]^\top \mathcal I(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\bigl[\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\bigr] = (\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z_n + o_p(1))^\top \mathcal I (\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z_n + o_p(1)) = \mathbf Z_n^\top\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z_n + o_p(1), \] using Slutsky to absorb the \(o_p(1)\) terms (the leading factors are \(O_p(1)\)). Thus \(W_n = R_n + o_p(1)\), so \(W_n \xrightarrow{d}\chi^2_p\) and \(W_n - R_n \xrightarrow{p} 0\).

Likelihood-ratio statistic. Take a second-order Taylor expansion of \(\ell_n\) about \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\), using \(\mathbf s_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) = \mathbf 0\) and the integral form of the remainder: \[ \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0) = \ell_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) + \mathbf 0 + \tfrac12 (\boldsymbol\theta_0 - \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)^\top \mathbf H_n(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)(\boldsymbol\theta_0 - \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n), \] for \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) on the segment, so \[ \Lambda_n = 2[\ell_n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) - \ell_n(\boldsymbol\theta_0)] = (\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)^\top\bigl[-\mathbf H_n(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n)\bigr](\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0). \] Now \(-\tfrac1n \mathbf H_n(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n) \xrightarrow{p}\mathcal I\) (Step 4 of Theorem 5.6, since \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p}\boldsymbol\theta_0\)), so \(\Lambda_n = [\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)]^\top(-\tfrac1n\mathbf H_n)[\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)] = \mathbf Z_n^\top\mathcal I^{-1}\mathbf Z_n + o_p(1)\) by the same substitution (both the averaged Hessian at \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) here and the one at \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n\) in the Wald step converge in probability to the same \(\mathcal I\), because \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p}\boldsymbol\theta_0\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p}\boldsymbol\theta_0\), and we substitute Equation 5.18 for \(\sqrt n(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_n - \boldsymbol\theta_0)\)). Therefore \(\Lambda_n = R_n + o_p(1) \xrightarrow{d}\chi^2_p\), and \(\Lambda_n - R_n \xrightarrow{p} 0\), \(\Lambda_n - W_n \xrightarrow{p} 0\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. All three tests give the same answer in large samples, so for a single large indirect comparison it rarely matters which you quote. What to remember: they are the three natural ways to ask “is the effect different from the null,” and a confidence interval is simply the set of null values that none of them rejects (next remark). The picture is the parabola: the distance, slope, and height of a parabola all encode the same curvature, which is why the statistics coincide. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark (Confidence intervals by inversion). A level-\(\alpha\) test of \(H_0 : \boldsymbol\theta = \boldsymbol\theta_0\) rejects when the statistic exceeds the upper-\(\alpha\) quantile \(\chi^2_{p, 1-\alpha}\). The set of null values not rejected is a \((1-\alpha)\) confidence region. For a scalar parameter the Wald region is the familiar interval \[ \hat\theta_n \pm z_{1 - \alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat\theta_n), \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat\theta_n) = \bigl(\mathcal I_n(\hat\theta_n)\bigr)^{-1/2}, \] where \(z_{1-\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\) and \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function (Notation). Inverting the likelihood-ratio test instead gives the profile-likelihood interval \(\{\theta : 2[\ell_n(\hat\theta_n) - \ell_n(\theta)] \le \chi^2_{1, 1-\alpha}\}\), which respects the shape of the log-likelihood and is not forced to be symmetric. The three intervals agree to first order by Theorem 5.9 but can differ markedly in small samples or near a boundary, where the likelihood-ratio and score intervals are generally preferred. Concretely, to build a confidence interval by hand you can sweep candidate null values \(\theta_0\), run the test at each, and keep those it does not reject; the retained values form the interval. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

5.10 A fully worked example

We tie the chapter together on the simplest nontrivial model, the Bernoulli, where every quantity can be computed by hand, and then illustrate the sandwich variance on a deliberately misspecified model. Most pieces have already appeared in context, threaded through the running example: the score in Lemma 5.1, the information in Corollary 5.1, the log-odds delta method in Example 5.2, and the indirect comparison in Example 5.3. Here we assemble them on the one dataset \(n = 25\), \(\sum_i X_i = 10\).

Example 5.4 (Maximum likelihood for a Bernoulli proportion) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be iid \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) with \(\theta = p \in (0,1)\). Suppose we observe \(n = 25\) trials with \(\sum_i X_i = 10\) successes.

Likelihood, score, MLE. The single-observation density is \(f(x; p) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}\), so \[ \ell_n(p) = \Bigl(\textstyle\sum_i X_i\Bigr)\log p + \Bigl(n - \textstyle\sum_i X_i\Bigr)\log(1-p) = 10\log p + 15\log(1-p) \] (dropping the constant binomial coefficient, which does not depend on \(p\)). The score is \[ s_n(p) = \frac{10}{p} - \frac{15}{1-p}, \qquad s_n(p) = 0 \iff 10(1-p) = 15 p \iff \hat p = \tfrac{10}{25} = 0.40 . \] This matches Definition 5.8: \(\hat p_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \bar X_n\).

Fisher information and standard error. The per-observation score is \(s(p; x) = x/p - (1-x)/(1-p)\), and by the information equality (Theorem 5.4), with \(\partial_p^2 \log f = -x/p^2 - (1-x)/(1-p)^2\) and \(\mathbb E[X] = p\), \[ \mathcal I(p) = -\mathbb E_p\bigl[\partial_p^2 \log f\bigr] = \frac{p}{p^2} + \frac{1-p}{(1-p)^2} = \frac1p + \frac{1}{1-p} = \frac{1}{p(1-p)} . \] At \(\hat p = 0.4\), \(\mathcal I(\hat p) = 1/(0.4\cdot 0.6) = 1/0.24 = 4.1\overline{6}\), the sample information is \(\mathcal I_n(\hat p) = 25 \times 4.1\overline 6 = 104.1\overline 6\), and the asymptotic variance of \(\hat p\) is \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat p) = \frac{1}{\mathcal I_n(\hat p)} = \frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n} = \frac{0.24}{25} = 0.0096, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat p) = \sqrt{0.0096} = 0.09798 . \] The Wald 95% confidence interval (Section 5.9) is \(0.40 \pm 1.96 \times 0.09798 = 0.40 \pm 0.19204 = (0.2080,\, 0.5920)\).

Delta method for the log-odds. The log-odds is \(g(p) = \log\!\frac{p}{1-p}\) with \(g'(p) = \frac{1}{p(1-p)}\). By the delta method (Theorem 5.3), \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\bigl(g(\hat p)\bigr) = g'(\hat p)^2\,\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat p) = \Bigl(\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\Bigr)^2 \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \frac{1}{n\,p(1-p)}\Big|_{p = \hat p} = \frac{1}{25 \times 0.24} = \frac16 = 0.1\overline 6 . \] Notice the algebra collapses to \(\tfrac{1}{n\hat p(1-\hat p)} = \tfrac{1}{n\hat p} + \tfrac{1}{n(1-\hat p)} = \tfrac{1}{\#\text{success}} + \tfrac{1}{\#\text{fail}} = \tfrac{1}{10} + \tfrac{1}{15} = 0.1\overline 6\), the textbook standard error of an empirical log-odds. Thus \(g(\hat p) = \log(0.4/0.6) = -0.40546\) with \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}} = \sqrt{1/6} = 0.40825\), giving a 95% interval for the log-odds of \(-0.40546 \pm 1.96 \times 0.40825 = (-1.2056,\, 0.3947)\) and, exponentiating, an odds-ratio-scale interval \((e^{-1.2056}, e^{0.3947}) = (0.2995,\, 1.4830)\).

Three tests of \(H_0 : p = 0.5\). Using Definition 5.12 with \(\boldsymbol\theta_0 = 0.5\):

  • Score test. \(s_n(0.5) = 10/0.5 - 15/0.5 = -10\) and \(\mathcal I_n(0.5) = 25/(0.5\cdot 0.5) = 100\), so \(R_n = (-10)^2/100 = 1.000\).

  • Wald test. \(W_n = (\hat p - 0.5)^2\,\mathcal I_n(\hat p) = (-0.1)^2 \times 104.1\overline 6 = 0.01/0.0096 = 1.0417\).

  • Likelihood-ratio test. \(\ell_n(0.4) = 10\log 0.4 + 15\log 0.6 = -9.16291 - 7.66238 = -16.82529\) and \(\ell_n(0.5) = 25\log 0.5 = -17.32868\), so \(\Lambda_n = 2(-16.82529 + 17.32868) = 2 \times 0.50339 = 1.0068\).

All three statistics cluster near \(1.0\), confirming the first-order equivalence of Theorem 5.9. Against the critical value \(\chi^2_{1, 0.95} = 3.841\), none rejects \(H_0 : p = 0.5\); equivalently, \(0.5\) lies inside the Wald interval \((0.208, 0.592)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example 5.5 (When the model is wrong: the sandwich earns its name) Suppose we estimate a population mean \(\mu\) by fitting a working model that treats the data as \(\mathcal N(\mu, 1)\) with the variance fixed (incorrectly) at \(1\), while the true distribution \(P\) has mean \(\mu_0\) and variance \(\sigma_0^2 \ne 1\). The working score is \(\psi(x; \mu) = \partial_\mu\bigl[-\tfrac12(x - \mu)^2\bigr] = x - \mu\), so the estimating equation \(\sum_i (X_i - \hat\mu_n) = 0\) yields the M-estimator \(\hat\mu_n = \bar X_n\), which targets \(\mu^{*} = \mu_0\).

By Theorem 5.8, the bread and meat are \[ \mathbf A = -\mathbb E_P[\partial_\mu \psi] = -\mathbb E_P[-1] = 1, \qquad \mathbf B = \mathbb E_P[\psi^2] = \mathbb E_P[(X - \mu_0)^2] = \sigma_0^2, \] so the correct asymptotic variance is \(\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf B\,\mathbf A^{-1}/n = \sigma_0^2/n\), exactly the variance of \(\bar X_n\). The naive model-based variance, which trusts the working model and uses the inverse working information \(\mathbf A^{-1}/n = 1/n\), is wrong by the factor \(\sigma_0^2\).

Concrete numbers. Take \(n = 40\) observations with sample mean \(\bar X_n = 2.5\) and \(\sum_i (X_i - \bar X_n)^2 = 160\). Then \(\widehat{\mathbf A}_n = 1\), \(\widehat{\mathbf B}_n = 160/40 = 4.0\), and the sandwich variance Equation 5.14 is \[ \widehat{\mathbb V}(\hat\mu_n) = \tfrac1n\,\widehat{\mathbf A}_n^{-1}\,\widehat{\mathbf B}_n\,\widehat{\mathbf A}_n^{-1} = \frac{4.0}{40} = 0.10, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{se}}_{\text{robust}} = \sqrt{0.10} = 0.3162, \] whereas the naive standard error is \(\sqrt{1/40} = 0.1581\), understating the true uncertainty by a factor of \(\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbf B}_n} = 2\). This is the mechanism, in miniature, that makes the sandwich estimator mandatory for MAIC in Chapter 18: the weighting “model” is a computational device, not a believed truth, and only the robust variance is consistent. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The estimation theory of this chapter is classical and is developed, with the regression emphasis this book shares, in Bingham and Fry (2010); the worked Bernoulli example and the three-test comparison follow that source’s treatment of likelihood inference. The notation for likelihood, score, Fisher information, the MLE, and the sandwich variance matches Notation.

The asymptotic tools of Section 5.2 correspond to the proof catalogue: the delta method is A.11, Slutsky’s theorem is A.12, and the continuous mapping theorem is A.13 (corresponds to A.11-A.13 in the proof catalogue). Our statements and proofs align with the companion manuscript’s treatment in manuscript/01_probability.md; we have expanded the catalogue’s one-line sketches into full proofs and have generalized the delta method and Slutsky’s theorem to the multivariate case via the Cramér-Wold device. The continuous mapping and Slutsky proofs rest on the Portmanteau theorem, which we cite as a standard result of weak-convergence theory.

The consistency proof (Theorem 5.5) is the Wald argmax argument: the Kullback-Leibler information inequality (Lemma 5.2, a consequence of Jensen’s inequality Theorem 4.6) identifies \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\) as the unique maximizer of the population log-likelihood, a uniform law of large numbers (Lemma 5.3, cited) transfers this to the sample, and the argmax-continuity lemma (Lemma 5.4, proved in full) concludes. The asymptotic-normality proof (Theorem 5.6) is the standard Taylor expansion of the score, assembled from the information equality (Theorem 5.4), the multivariate central limit theorem, and Slutsky’s theorem, with the affine-image property of the multivariate normal (Theorem 4.14 from Chapter 4) delivering the Gaussian limit. The Cramér-Rao bound (Theorem 5.7) is proved via the covariance Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Theorem 2.1), with the matrix version following from positive-semidefiniteness of a joint covariance (Theorem 4.9) and a Schur-complement computation.

M-estimation and the sandwich variance (Definition 5.11) generalize maximum likelihood; the collapse of the sandwich to the inverse information under correct specification (Corollary 5.3) is a direct corollary of the information equality, and is the conceptual bridge to the robust variance used for MAIC in Chapter 18. The convolution-theorem justification of asymptotic efficiency is cited, not proved, as it requires the local asymptotic normality machinery outside the scope of a foundations chapter.

No result in this chapter is tagged machine-checked. The companion Coq development depends on mathcomp-analysis for measure-theoretic foundations and, per the manuscript, treats the asymptotic results A.11-A.13 at the level of cited library facts rather than re-derived theorems; the pen-and-paper proofs here are the authoritative versions. Candidates for future formalization are the information equality and the M-estimator sandwich, both of which are purely algebraic given the differentiation-under-the-integral hypotheses.

5.12 Exercises

The exercises progress from warm-ups and interpretation to computation and proof. A starred exercise (\(\star\)) has a full worked solution in Appendix F; the first warm-up below is solved inline so you can calibrate your approach before attempting the rest.

Exercise 5.1 (Warm-up (solved): a log odds and its interval from one arm) In a single-arm cohort, \(20\) of \(50\) patients respond. (a) Give the MLE \(\hat p\). (b) Give the log odds \(g(\hat p) = \log\frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p}\) and its delta-method standard error. (c) Report a 95% confidence interval on the log-odds scale and on the odds scale.

Solution. (a) \(\hat p = 20/50 = 0.40\). (b) The log odds is \(g(\hat p) = \log(0.40/0.60) = \log(0.6667) = -0.4055\). By Example 5.2 the variance of an empirical log odds is \(\tfrac{1}{\#\text{responders}} + \tfrac{1}{\#\text{non-responders}} = \tfrac1{20} + \tfrac1{30} = 0.08333\), so the standard error is \(\sqrt{0.08333} = 0.2887\). (c) On the log-odds scale, \(-0.4055 \pm 1.96 \times 0.2887 = (-0.9713,\, 0.1603)\); exponentiating gives the odds-scale interval \((e^{-0.9713}, e^{0.1603}) = (0.379,\, 1.174)\). The interval includes odds \(1\) (probability \(0.5\)), so the data are consistent with a coin-flip response rate. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Exercise 5.2 (Warm-up (interpretation): where is a proportion hardest to estimate?) Using \(\mathcal I(p) = 1/[p(1 - p)]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat p) \approx p(1 - p)/n\): (a) for fixed \(n\), at what value of \(p\) is \(\hat p\) most variable, and where is it most precise? (b) A trial wants the standard error of \(\hat p\) below \(0.02\) when the true \(p\) is near \(0.5\); roughly how many patients are needed? (c) In one sentence, relate your answer to why the Fisher information is largest near \(p = 0\) or \(p = 1\).

Exercise 5.3 (Interpretation: the cost of going indirect) In Example 5.3 the direct contrasts had standard errors \(0.456\) and \(0.410\), while the indirect contrast of \(B\) versus \(C\) had standard error \(0.614\). (a) Show that the indirect variance is the sum of the two direct variances and confirm the number. (b) Explain in one sentence why an indirect comparison is necessarily less precise than the direct comparisons it is built from. (c) If a third trial directly compared \(B\) and \(C\) with standard error \(0.30\), which estimate would you trust more, and why?

Exercise 5.4 (Poisson information and efficiency) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be iid \(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\). (a) Derive the MLE \(\hat\lambda_n\). (b) Compute the score, show it has mean zero, and compute the Fisher information \(\mathcal I(\lambda)\) two ways (as the variance of the score and as minus the expected second derivative), confirming the information equality. (c) Show \(\hat\lambda_n\) is unbiased and attains the Cramér-Rao bound exactly, hence is efficient.

Exercise 5.5 (Reparameterization and invariance) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be iid Exponential with rate \(\lambda > 0\), density \(f(x;\lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) on \(x > 0\). (a) Find the MLE of \(\lambda\). (b) Using invariance (Proposition 5.2), write down the MLE of the mean \(\eta = 1/\lambda\) and of the median \(m = (\log 2)/\lambda\). (c) Use the delta method (Theorem 5.3) to give the asymptotic variance of \(\hat\eta_n\), and verify it equals the Cramér-Rao bound for estimating \(\eta\).

Exercise 5.6 (Bias can lower MSE) For iid \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) data with known \(\sigma^2\), consider the shrinkage estimator \(\hat\mu_c = c\,\bar X_n\) for a constant \(c \in [0,1]\). (a) Using Proposition 5.1, write \(\mathrm{MSE}_\mu(\hat\mu_c)\) as a function of \(c\). (b) Minimize over \(c\) and show the optimal \(c^{*}\) is strictly less than \(1\) whenever \(\mu \ne 0\), so a biased estimator strictly beats the unbiased \(\bar X_n\) in MSE. (c) Explain why this does not contradict the Cramér-Rao bound.

Exercise 5.7 (Score versus Wald in finite samples) Repeat the three tests of Example 5.4 for the data \(n = 25\), \(\sum_i X_i = 10\) against the null \(H_0 : p = 0.3\). Report \(W_n\), \(R_n\), and \(\Lambda_n\) to three decimals and state which (if any) reject at level \(0.05\). Comment on the ordering \(W_n\), \(R_n\), \(\Lambda_n\) you observe.

Exercise 5.8 (Where the information equality fails (\(\star\))) The information equality (Theorem 5.4) requires regularity (Definition 5.6), in particular a common support not depending on the parameter. Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be iid Uniform\((0, \theta)\), whose support \((0, \theta)\) does depend on \(\theta\). (a) Show that the naive score \(\partial_\theta \log f = -1/\theta\) has nonzero expectation, so Lemma 5.1 fails. (b) Show the MLE is \(\hat\theta_n = \max_i X_i\), that \(n(\theta - \hat\theta_n)/\theta \xrightarrow{d} \mathrm{Exp}(1)\), and hence the rate is \(n\) rather than \(\sqrt n\) and the limit is not normal. (c) Explain precisely which hypotheses of Theorem 5.6 are violated. (Solution in Appendix F.)

Exercise 5.9 (Sandwich variance for a misspecified Poisson (\(\star\))) Suppose the true data are overdispersed counts with mean \(\mu_0\) and variance \(v_0 = \kappa\,\mu_0\) for some \(\kappa > 1\) (so \(\kappa = 1\) would be Poisson), but we fit a working Poisson model with log-likelihood contribution \(x\log\mu - \mu\). (a) Show the working score is \(\psi(x;\mu) = x/\mu - 1\) and the M-estimator is \(\hat\mu_n = \bar X_n\), targeting \(\mu^{*} = \mu_0\). (b) Compute the bread \(\mathbf A(\mu_0)\) and meat \(\mathbf B(\mu_0)\) and show the sandwich asymptotic variance is \(\kappa\,\mu_0/n\), whereas the naive model-based variance is \(\mu_0/n\). (c) Conclude that the naive Poisson standard error understates the truth by the factor \(\sqrt\kappa\), and relate this to Corollary 5.3. (Solution in Appendix F.)

Exercise 5.10 (Multivariate delta method for a ratio) Let \((\hat\alpha_n, \hat\beta_n)\) be jointly asymptotically normal, \(\sqrt n\bigl((\hat\alpha_n, \hat\beta_n) - (\alpha, \beta)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N_2(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \sigma_\alpha^2 & \sigma_{\alpha\beta} \\ \sigma_{\alpha\beta} & \sigma_\beta^2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) and \(\beta \ne 0\). Using Theorem 5.3, derive the asymptotic variance of the ratio \(\hat\rho_n = \hat\alpha_n / \hat\beta_n\). Specialize to the independent case \(\sigma_{\alpha\beta} = 0\) and comment on the well-known instability when \(\beta\) is near zero.
