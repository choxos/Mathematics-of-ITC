21  Multilevel Network Meta-Regression II: Discrete Outcomes and Approximation Theory

21.1 Introduction

Chapter 20 built the engine of multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) on a single idea, the aggregation principle: to honor an aggregate-data (AgD) study we write the individual-level model (Definition 20.1), push each individual’s linear predictor through the inverse link, and average on the response scale before comparing with the reported summary (Definition 20.2, Theorem 20.1). For a continuous outcome on the identity scale that average is itself the linear predictor evaluated at the covariate mean (Theorem 20.3), and the aggregate likelihood is an ordinary normal: the arithmetic was gentle because summation and the identity link commute. This chapter takes up the outcomes for which they do not, the counts and the binary events that dominate health-technology assessment, whose aggregate is not a smooth average of a single quantity but a sum of many small, non-identical random contributions, one per patient.

Example 21.1 (A running example: an oncology responder arm) Throughout this chapter we return to one aggregate-data (AgD) arm from a hypothetical oncology network. Study \(AB\) compares drug \(A\) against drug \(B\); for the drug-\(A\) arm it reports only the number of responders out of the patients treated, a responder being a binary outcome (tumor shrinkage past a fixed threshold). We do not see individual records for this arm, only summary covariates (baseline severity, prior lines of therapy). The ML-NMR model of Chapter 20, fitted to the individual-patient-data (IPD) studies elsewhere in the network, assigns any covariate profile \(x\) a response probability \(p=\operatorname{expit}(\eta(x))\), where \(\operatorname{expit}(z)=e^{z}/(1+e^{z})\) is the inverse of the logit link (Definition 7.2).

Two facts make the aggregate awkward. First, patients differ: a frail, heavily pretreated patient may have response probability near \(0.1\), a fit, treatment-naive patient near \(0.7\). Second, we must turn those unequal per-patient probabilities into a single likelihood for the reported total. The naive move is to average the risks to a common \(\bar p\) and write \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,\bar p)\); much of this chapter is about what that shortcut gets wrong and how to repair it.

To keep every number checkable by hand, our concrete instance uses five equally weighted covariate profiles with model-predicted response probabilities \[ \mathbf p=(p_1,\dots,p_5)=(0.1,\ 0.3,\ 0.4,\ 0.5,\ 0.7), \qquad \bar p=0.4, \] the vector we carry all the way to the fully worked Example 21.5. These five are profiles that summarize the covariate distribution (the numerical integration nodes of Chapter 22), not necessarily five real patients. Whether we hold them fixed or regard the arm as a random sample from the distribution they summarize is exactly the conditional-versus-marginal choice that organizes everything below. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Two questions organize the chapter. First, what is the exact law of that sum? Second, since the exact law is in general intractable, what tractable distribution approximates it, and how large is the error? The answers split cleanly by outcome type, and the split is itself instructive.

For count outcomes the news is good and then subtle. A sum of independent Poisson variables is again Poisson, with rate equal to the sum of the rates, and this holds whether or not the rates are identical (Lemma 21.1); the Poisson family is closed under convolution with addition of parameters. So conditional on the patients’ covariates the aggregate count is exactly Poisson (Theorem 21.1), and no approximation is needed at that level. The subtlety is that the covariates are themselves random draws from the study population, so the rate of the conditional Poisson is random, and marginalizing over it yields not a Poisson but a mixed Poisson (a Poisson whose rate is itself random, drawn from a distribution and then averaged out), which carries the same mean and a strictly larger variance. The excess, the overdispersion, is the count-data fingerprint of the within-study covariate variation that ML-NMR exists to respect, and we compute it exactly under Gaussian covariates.

For binary outcomes the news is that the obvious guess is wrong. A sum of independent Bernoulli variables with a common success probability is binomial, but a sum of Bernoullis with different success probabilities, exactly the situation when each patient’s event risk \(p_i=\operatorname{expit}(\eta(x_i))\) depends on that patient’s covariates, is not binomial. It is the Poisson binomial distribution (Definition 4.11), whose exact probability mass function is a sum over all \(\binom{n}{y}\) subsets of size \(y\) and is hopeless to evaluate directly for the sample sizes of a real trial. We derive its structure cleanly through the probability generating function (Theorem 21.2), and then face the practical need for a tractable surrogate. Two classical surrogates answer that need, and they are genuinely different, a point the early ML-NMR literature was careful to separate and we are careful to keep separate here. Le Cam’s approximation replaces the Poisson binomial by a Poisson with matched mean and controls the total-variation error by the elegant bound \(\sum_i p_i^2\); we give the full coupling proof (Theorem 21.3), one of the few places in Part V where a sharp inequality, rather than a numerical recipe, carries the argument. The adjusted two-parameter binomial instead matches the first two moments exactly, producing a binomial whose count is automatically bounded above by its sample size and whose dispersion is correct (Theorem 21.4); it is the surrogate used in practice for binary ML-NMR. We close with a fully worked example that computes an aggregate binary likelihood three ways, by integration to a marginal binomial, by the exact Poisson binomial, and by the adjusted binomial, with the Le Cam Poisson alongside for contrast, and shows by hand which is faithful and which is not.

Throughout, the notation is that of Chapter 20 and of Notation: a study indexed \(j\), an arm or treatment indexed \(k\), individuals indexed \(i\), an outcome \(Y_{ijk}\) with conditional mean \(\theta_{ijk}=g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(x_{ijk}))\) for the link \(g\) (Definition 7.2) and linear predictor \(\eta_{jk}\) of Definition 20.1, and the aggregate mean \(\theta_{\bullet jk}=\int_{\mathcal X}g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(x))\,f_j(x)\,dx\) of Definition 20.2. We write \(Y_{\bullet jk}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}Y_{ijk}\) for the aggregate count over the \(n_{jk}\) individuals of arm \(k\) in study \(j\), and we drop the study and arm subscripts to \(Y_\bullet\), \(n\), \(\theta_\bullet\), \(\eta\), \(\mathbf x\) wherever a single arm is under discussion. This chapter corresponds to results H.8 to H.11 of the proof catalogue.

21.2 The aggregate count and its two laws

Fix a single AgD arm with \(n\) individuals. The individual-level model gives, conditional on patient \(i\)’s covariate vector \(x_i\), a mean \(\theta_i=g^{-1}(\eta(x_i))\) on the outcome scale; the outcomes \(Y_i\) are conditionally independent given the covariates. The object the AgD study reports is some function of the aggregate count \(Y_\bullet=\sum_i Y_i\), typically the total number of events \(y_\bullet=\sum_i y_i\) out of \(n\), and the aggregate likelihood is the law of \(Y_\bullet\) evaluated at \(y_\bullet\). There are two distinct probability laws in play, and conflating them is the principal hazard of this subject, so we isolate them now.

Remark. Conditional versus marginal. The conditional law of \(Y_\bullet\) fixes the covariate values \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) of the \(n\) specific patients and treats only the outcomes as random; it is the law of a sum of independent variables with parameters \(\theta_i=g^{-1}(\eta(x_i))\) that differ from patient to patient. The marginal law additionally treats each \(x_i\) as an independent draw from the study covariate distribution \(f_j\) and integrates it out, so that the patients become exchangeable and each single outcome has the aggregate mean \(\theta_\bullet=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim f_j}[g^{-1}(\eta(X))]\) of Definition 20.2. The marginal law is the mixture of the conditional laws over random covariate configurations. The two have the same mean, \(n\theta_\bullet\), but in general different variances, because randomizing the covariates injects extra variability; the gap between them is the recurring theme of this chapter, appearing as overdispersion for counts (Theorem 21.1) and as the difference between the integrated and adjusted binomials for binary events (Example 21.5). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Before any machinery, here is the whole distinction in two patients and numbers a reader can check in a minute. It is the seed of the fully worked example at the end of the chapter.

Example 21.2 (Two patients, two laws, by hand) Take the two extreme profiles of the running example (Example 21.1): a frail patient with response probability \(0.1\) and a fit patient with response probability \(0.7\), and suppose the study population is exactly these two profiles in equal proportion (a single binary effect modifier, frail versus fit, prevalence one half). Consider an arm of \(n=2\).

Conditional law (profiles held fixed). Fix one patient at each profile. Then \(Y_\bullet=Y_1+Y_2\) with \(Y_1\sim\mathrm{Ber}(0.1)\) and \(Y_2\sim\mathrm{Ber}(0.7)\) independent, so \[ P(Y_\bullet=0)=0.9\cdot 0.3=0.27,\quad P(Y_\bullet=1)=0.1\cdot 0.3+0.9\cdot 0.7=0.66,\quad P(Y_\bullet=2)=0.1\cdot 0.7=0.07, \] with mean \(0.1+0.7=0.8\) and variance \(0.1(0.9)+0.7(0.3)=0.30\).

Marginal law (patients drawn at random). Now let each of the two patients be an independent draw from the population, so marginally each is \(\mathrm{Ber}(0.4)\) (the average risk) and \(Y_\bullet\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2,0.4)\): \[ P(Y_\bullet=0)=0.36,\quad P(Y_\bullet=1)=0.48,\quad P(Y_\bullet=2)=0.16, \] with mean \(0.8\) and variance \(2(0.4)(0.6)=0.48\).

The two laws share the mean \(0.8\) but differ in variance, \(0.30\) against \(0.48\). The extra \(0.18\) is the price of randomizing which profile each patient occupies; it equals \(n\,s_p^2\) with \(s_p^2=\tfrac12\big[(0.1-0.4)^2+(0.7-0.4)^2\big]=0.09\) the variance of the two risks. This single pair of numbers is the entire conditional-versus-marginal theme in miniature, and it returns, with five profiles instead of two, as Step 7 of Example 21.5. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

For binary outcomes the marginal law is immediate and is the likelihood ML-NMR uses by default, so we record it before turning to the finer conditional analysis.

Proposition 21.1 (The integrated binomial aggregate likelihood) Suppose the individual outcomes are Bernoulli, \(Y_i\mid X_i\sim\mathrm{Ber}(g^{-1}(\eta(X_i)))\), that the covariates \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are independent and identically distributed draws from \(f_j\), and that the outcomes are conditionally independent given the covariates. Then marginally the \(Y_i\) are independent and identically distributed \(\mathrm{Ber}(\theta_\bullet)\) with \(\theta_\bullet\) the aggregate mean of Definition 20.2, and the aggregate count is exactly binomial, \[ Y_\bullet=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\ \sim\ \mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta_\bullet), \qquad \theta_\bullet=\int_{\mathcal X}g^{-1}(\eta(x))\,f_j(x)\,dx . \tag{21.1}\]

Proof. By the tower property, that averaging a conditional probability over the conditioning variable returns the unconditional probability, for each \(i\), \(P(Y_i=1)=\mathbb{E}_{X_i}\!\big[P(Y_i=1\mid X_i)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{X_i}\!\big[g^{-1}(\eta(X_i))\big] =\theta_\bullet\), the last equality being the definition of the aggregate mean (Definition 20.2), which exists by Theorem 20.1. The pairs \((X_i,Y_i)\) are independent across \(i\) and identically distributed, so the marginal outcomes \(Y_i\) are independent and identically distributed \(\mathrm{Ber}(\theta_\bullet)\); their sum is \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta_\bullet)\) by the definition of the binomial as a sum of independent identical Bernoullis (Definition 4.9). \(\square\)

Remark. Reader translation. Integrating the covariate out first says: treat every future patient from this arm’s target population as carrying the same average response chance \(\theta_\bullet\), even though any one patient’s risk would differ if we saw their covariates. The aggregate count is then an ordinary binomial, exactly as if the arm had run \(n\) identical coin flips with success probability \(\theta_\bullet\). Nothing is approximated; the only assumption is that the \(n\) patients are an exchangeable sample from \(f_j\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why this is not the end of the story. Proposition Proposition 21.1 is exact, requires no approximation, and is the binary aggregate likelihood of the original ML-NMR (Phillippo et al. (2020)): compute \(\theta_\bullet\) by numerical integration (Chapter 22) and treat the reported events as \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta_\bullet)\). Its one assumption is that the \(n\) patients are an exchangeable sample from \(f_j\), so that integrating the covariate out is legitimate before forming the count. The conditional view, which keeps the per-patient risks \(p_i\) distinct, is needed for three purposes that occupy the rest of the chapter: to understand the structure of an aggregate event count as a sum of unequal risks (Theorem 21.2); to quantify how a Poisson surrogate for that count behaves (Theorem 21.3); and to build the moment-matched binomial that is preferred when one works directly with the integration nodes that represent \(f_j\) as a fixed design rather than as a random sample (Theorem 21.4). The gap between the marginal law Equation 21.1 and the conditional law is exactly the overdispersion we now compute for counts. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

21.3 A roadmap of the aggregate laws

It is tempting to ask which single likelihood is the binary ML-NMR likelihood. The honest answer is that there are two, and they are not rivals: they are the correct laws for two different ways of representing an AgD study’s covariates. If the arm’s patients are regarded as an exchangeable random sample from the covariate distribution, one integrates the covariate out first and the exact aggregate is the integrated binomial \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta_\bullet)\) of Proposition 21.1. If instead the covariate distribution is represented by a fixed set of integration nodes carrying distinct risks \(p_i\) (the numerical grid of Chapter 22), one conditions on those nodes and the aggregate is a Poisson binomial (Section 21.5), for which the adjusted binomial of Section 21.7 is the practical two-moment surrogate. The per-node risks \(p_i\) are themselves produced by the numerical integration of Chapter 22, which represents the covariate density \(f_j\) by nodes drawn through a Gaussian copula \(C_\Omega\); throughout this chapter we take the \(p_i\) as given inputs and study only what to do with them.

The count case splits the same way, and Table 21.1 collects all five situations. With covariates held fixed the aggregate is exact (Poisson or Poisson binomial); only when the covariates are treated as random does the story change, into overdispersion for counts and into a plain binomial for binary events. The right-hand column points to the section that fills in each row.

A reader who wants only the practical upshot may read Table 21.1, the worked Example 21.5, and the closing remark of Section 21.8, then skip the proofs on a first pass. The proofs are where the exactness and error claims in the table are earned.

21.4 Count outcomes: the Poisson aggregate

Count outcomes, modeled with the log link so that \(g^{-1}(\eta)=e^{\eta}\), are the easy case at the conditional level and the instructive case at the marginal level. The conditional ease rests on a closure property of the Poisson family that the binomial family lacks, so we prove it first and in full. The reason to dwell on so elementary a fact is the contrast it draws: the very same closure fails for the Bernoulli, and that single failure is what forces the approximation theory of the second half of the chapter (Section 21.6, Section 21.7).

Lemma 21.1 (A sum of independent Poissons is Poisson) Let \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) be independent with \(Y_i\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_i)\), \(\lambda_i>0\), the rates not necessarily equal. Then \[ \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poi}\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\Big). \tag{21.2}\]

Proof. It suffices to treat \(n=2\) and induct, since \(Y_1+\cdots+Y_{n}=(Y_1+\cdots+Y_{n-1})+Y_n\) is a sum of two independent Poissons once the inductive hypothesis identifies the first summand as \(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_{n-1})\). For \(X\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\) and \(Y\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)\) independent, the convolution formula for the law of a sum of independent nonnegative integer variables gives, for \(k=0,1,2,\dots\), \[ P(X+Y=k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}P(X=j)\,P(Y=k-j) =\sum_{j=0}^{k}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{j!}\,e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^{k-j}}{(k-j)!}. \] Factor out \(e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\) and multiply and divide by \(k!\): \[ P(X+Y=k)=\frac{e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\lambda^{j}\mu^{k-j} =\frac{e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}}{k!}\,(\lambda+\mu)^{k}, \] the inner sum being \((\lambda+\mu)^k\) by the binomial theorem. This is the mass function of \(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda+\mu)\) (Definition 4.10), proving the case \(n=2\) and, by induction, Equation 21.2.

A second proof records the structural reason, and it is this mechanism, not the conclusion, that we reuse: it shows in Section 21.5 precisely why the Bernoulli analogue breaks. By Definition 4.10 the moment generating function (MGF, Definition 4.7) of \(Y_i\) is \(M_{Y_i}(t)=\exp(\lambda_i(e^{t}-1))\), finite for all \(t\). By the product rule for MGFs of independent sums (Theorem 4.12), \[ M_{\sum_i Y_i}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n\exp\!\big(\lambda_i(e^{t}-1)\big) =\exp\!\Big(\Big(\textstyle\sum_i\lambda_i\Big)(e^{t}-1)\Big), \] which is the MGF of \(\mathrm{Poi}(\sum_i\lambda_i)\); by the uniqueness theorem for moment generating functions (Theorem 4.11) the sum has that law. \(\square\)

Remark. The asymmetry with the binomial. In plain terms: pooling several Poisson counts just adds their rates, so there is nothing to approximate for counts, whereas pooling several Bernoulli events does not stay binomial unless the individual risks are equal. Closure under convolution with parameter addition is special. It holds for the Poisson regardless of whether the rates agree, which is why count outcomes give no trouble at the conditional level. It fails for the Bernoulli: a sum of Bernoullis is binomial only when the success probabilities are equal, and otherwise leaves the binomial family entirely for the Poisson binomial of Section 21.5. The whole approximation apparatus of this chapter, Le Cam and the adjusted binomial, exists to repair this single missing closure property in the binary case. The MGF proof makes the contrast precise: the Poisson MGF \(\exp(\lambda(e^t-1))\) multiplies into the same functional form with \(\lambda\) summed, whereas the Bernoulli MGF \(1-p+pe^t\) multiplies into \(\prod_i(1-p_i+p_ie^t)\), which is the form of a binomial MGF \(((1-p)+pe^t)^n\) only when every \(p_i\) is the same \(p\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We can now state the law of the aggregate count, both conditionally and marginally. Write \(\eta(x)=\eta_{jk}(x)\) for the arm’s linear predictor and \(W=W(X)=e^{\eta(X)}\) for the random conditional rate of a single individual, so that \(\theta_\bullet=\mathbb{E}[W]\) is the aggregate mean of Definition 20.2 and \(\sigma_W^2:=\operatorname{Var}(W)\) measures how much that rate varies across the study population. (Here \(W\) is a scalar random rate, not the vector space \(W\) of Part I.) The theorem that follows has three layers, worth holding apart: conditional on the patients’ covariates the aggregate count is exactly Poisson; marginally, once the covariates are themselves random, it is an overdispersed mixed Poisson; and under Gaussian covariates the overdispersion collapses to a closed form.

Theorem 21.1 (The Poisson aggregate likelihood and its overdispersion) Let the individual count outcomes satisfy \(Y_i\mid X_i\sim\mathrm{Poi}(W_i)\) with \(W_i=e^{\eta(X_i)}\), conditionally independent given the covariates, and let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be independent and identically distributed draws from \(f_j\).

  1. (Conditional law.) Conditional on the covariate configuration \(\mathbf X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)\), the aggregate count is exactly Poisson, \[ Y_\bullet=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\ \Big|\ \mathbf X\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poi}\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n W_i\Big). \tag{21.3}\]

  2. (Marginal law.) Marginally over the covariates, \(Y_\bullet\) has the mixed Poisson law \[ P(Y_\bullet=y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf X}\!\left[e^{-\Lambda}\frac{\Lambda^{y}}{y!}\right], \qquad \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^n W_i, \tag{21.4}\] with mean and variance \[ \mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet]=n\theta_\bullet, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet)=n\theta_\bullet+n\sigma_W^2 . \tag{21.5}\] The marginal count is therefore overdispersed relative to a Poisson of the same mean, by exactly \(n\sigma_W^2\ge 0\), with equality (a genuine Poisson) if and only if \(W\) is degenerate, that is, if and only if the linear predictor \(\eta(X)\) is almost surely constant across the population.

  3. (Gaussian covariates.) If in addition \(X\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)\) and \(\eta(x)=\mu+x^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}\) is affine in \(x\) (with \(\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}=\boldsymbol\beta_1+\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\) the total covariate slope of Definition 20.1, the prognostic slopes \(\boldsymbol\beta_1\) plus this arm’s effect-modifier interactions \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\)), then the relative overdispersion has the closed form \[ \frac{\sigma_W^2}{\theta_\bullet^2} =\exp\!\big(\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}\big)-1 . \tag{21.6}\]

Proof. Part 1. Conditional on \(\mathbf X\), the rates \(W_i=e^{\eta(X_i)}\) are fixed positive numbers and the \(Y_i\) are independent \(\mathrm{Poi}(W_i)\); Lemma 21.1 gives \(Y_\bullet\mid\mathbf X\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\sum_i W_i)\), which is Equation 21.3.

Part 2. The marginal mass function is the expectation of the conditional Poisson mass function over the law of \(\mathbf X\), which is Equation 21.4. For the moments, write \(\Lambda=\sum_i W_i\) and use the tower property and the conditional Poisson identities \(\mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet\mid\mathbf X]=\Lambda\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet\mid\mathbf X)=\Lambda\) (mean equals variance for a Poisson, Definition 4.10). For the mean, \[ \mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet\mid\mathbf X]\big]=\mathbb{E}[\Lambda] =\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[W_i]=n\theta_\bullet, \] since each \(W_i\) has mean \(\theta_\bullet=\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta(X)}]\). For the variance, the law of total variance, \[ \operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet) =\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet\mid\mathbf X)\big] +\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet\mid\mathbf X]\big) =\mathbb{E}[\Lambda]+\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda), \] holds because \(\mathbb{E}[Y^2]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y^2\mid\mathbf X]] =\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\mathbf X)+\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathbf X]^2]\) and subtracting \(\mathbb{E}[Y]^2=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathbf X]]^2\) leaves precisely the two displayed terms. Now \(\mathbb{E}[\Lambda]=n\theta_\bullet\) as above, and by independence of the \(W_i\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda)=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(W_i)=n\sigma_W^2\). Hence \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet)=n\theta_\bullet+n\sigma_W^2\), which is Equation 21.5. The excess over the mean is \(n\sigma_W^2\ge 0\), zero exactly when \(\sigma_W^2=\operatorname{Var}(e^{\eta(X)})=0\), that is when \(e^{\eta(X)}\), equivalently \(\eta(X)\), is almost surely constant.

Part 3. Under the stated Gaussian assumption \(\eta(X)=\mu+X^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}\) is an affine function of a normal vector, hence univariate normal: for \(X\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_j, \boldsymbol\Sigma_j)\) the scalar \(a+\mathbf b^\top X\) has moment generating function \(\mathbb{E}[e^{t(a+\mathbf b^\top X)}]=\exp\!\big(t(a+\mathbf b^\top\boldsymbol\mu_j) +\tfrac12 t^2\,\mathbf b^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\mathbf b\big)\), which is the MGF of a univariate normal (Definition 4.12), so \(\eta(X)\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2)\) with \(m=\mu+\boldsymbol\mu_j^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}\) and \(s^2=\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}\). Then \(W=e^{\eta(X)}\) is log-normal (a variable whose logarithm is normal), and because \(\mathbb{E}[W^{r}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{r\eta(X)}]\) is the normal MGF of \(\eta(X)\) read off at \(t=r\), its moments are values of that MGF (Definition 4.12): with \(M(t)=e^{mt+s^2t^2/2}\), \[ \theta_\bullet=\mathbb{E}[W]=\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta(X)}]=M(1)=e^{m+s^2/2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[W^2]=\mathbb{E}[e^{2\eta(X)}]=M(2)=e^{2m+2s^2}. \] The first identity is the closed form of the aggregate rate under the log link (Theorem 20.4). Therefore \[ \sigma_W^2=\mathbb{E}[W^2]-\mathbb{E}[W]^2=e^{2m+2s^2}-e^{2m+s^2}=e^{2m+s^2}\big(e^{s^2}-1\big) =\theta_\bullet^2\big(e^{s^2}-1\big), \] using \(\theta_\bullet^2=e^{2m+s^2}\). Dividing by \(\theta_\bullet^2\) gives Equation 21.6. \(\square\)

Example 21.3 (Overdispersion in a two-profile count arm) A hospitalization-count arm has \(n=40\) patients and a single binary effect modifier (say, prior hospitalization: yes or no) with prevalence one half. The log-link linear predictor gives conditional rate \(W=1\) event per patient at one profile and \(W=3\) at the other, so \[ \theta_\bullet=\mathbb{E}[W]=\tfrac12(1+3)=2,\qquad \mathbb{E}[W^2]=\tfrac12(1+9)=5,\qquad \sigma_W^2=5-2^2=1. \] By Equation 21.5 the marginal count has mean \(n\theta_\bullet=40\cdot 2=80\) and variance \(n\theta_\bullet+n\sigma_W^2=80+40=120\), a dispersion index \(120/80=1.5\). A plain \(\mathrm{Poi}(n\theta_\bullet)=\mathrm{Poi}(80)\) would claim variance \(80\); ignoring the overdispersion understates the true variance by \(40/120\approx 33\%\), so it treats the reported count as far more informative than it is, and an AgD arm modeled that way would pull the network estimate too hard. This is the count-data face of the conditional-versus-marginal gap of Example 21.2. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reading the overdispersion. The factor \(e^{s^2}-1\) in Equation 21.6 is the squared coefficient of variation of the random rate \(W\), and \(s^2=\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma_j\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}\) is the variance that the covariates induce in the linear predictor through the prognostic and effect-modifying slopes. When those slopes are small, or the covariate spread \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\) is small, \(s^2\) is near zero and \(e^{s^2}-1\approx s^2\) is negligible, so the Poisson approximation to the marginal count is excellent and one may safely use \(\mathrm{Poi}(n\theta_\bullet)\) as the aggregate likelihood. For a concrete feel, recall \(\theta_\bullet=e^{m+s^2/2}\) from the log-link identity (Theorem 20.4); if \(s^2=\boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_j \boldsymbol\beta_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.25\), the relative overdispersion is \(e^{0.25}-1\approx 0.28\), so the rate’s coefficient of variation is \(\sqrt{0.28}\approx 0.53\) and each patient contributes excess count variance \(\sigma_W^2\approx 0.28\,\theta_\bullet^2\) beyond the Poisson baseline. When the effect modifiers are strong and dispersed, the overdispersion is real and ignoring it understates the uncertainty of the aggregate count, exactly the ecological-bias phenomenon of Definition 16.2 seen now in the second moment rather than the first. The naive claim that the marginalized count is itself Poisson is false in this regime; Equation 21.5 is the correct statement. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The negative-binomial remedy. When the overdispersion \(n\sigma_W^2\) is not negligible, a Poisson aggregate likelihood is misspecified in its variance, and a two-parameter count law is wanted. The natural choice is the negative binomial, which arises precisely as a Gamma-mixed Poisson: if the random rate \(\Lambda\) were Gamma distributed, the mixture Equation 21.4 would be exactly negative binomial. The log-normal mixing implied by Gaussian covariates is not Gamma, so the match is by moments rather than in law, but a negative binomial calibrated to the mean \(n\theta_\bullet\) and variance \(n\theta_\bullet+n\sigma_W^2\) of Equation 21.5 restores correct dispersion; its extra dispersion parameter plays the role of a study-level random effect on the count scale (Definition 8.3). This is the count-data analogue of the adjusted binomial we build for binary outcomes in Section 21.7, and the two share the same logic: keep the correct first two moments, accept a parametric family that is convenient and respects the natural support. Concretely, in the two-profile arm of Example 21.3 this means calibrating a negative binomial to mean \(80\) and variance \(120\) (a dispersion index of \(1.5\)); in ML-NMR practice the same excess variance is more often absorbed by a study-level random effect on the log-rate (Definition 8.3), which keeps each covariate profile Poisson while letting the arm as a whole be overdispersed, than by switching count families outright. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This development corresponds to H.8 in the proof catalogue. The catalogue records the corrected statement, that the marginalized aggregate is a mixed Poisson with the overdispersion Equation 21.5, not a plain Poisson; Lemma 21.1 is the closure property that makes the conditional aggregate exact.

21.5 Binary outcomes: the Poisson binomial and its generating function

We turn to the binary case, where the aggregate count of events is a sum of independent but non-identical Bernoulli variables. By Lemma 21.1’s failing analogue, this sum is not binomial; it is the Poisson binomial of Definition 4.11. That definition (Chapter 4) already records the mass function, mean, variance, and MGF of the Poisson binomial. Here we re-derive its structure through the probability generating function (PGF), which is the natural transform for an integer-valued count because it stores the mass function in its Taylor coefficients and turns the convolution of independent counts into a product, exactly as the MGF does on the exponential scale. The PGF is the tool that makes the combinatorial mass function of Definition 4.11 transparent and that hands us the moments without re-summing the subset expansion.

Definition 21.1 (Probability generating function) For a random variable \(X\) taking values in \(\{0,1,2,\dots\}\), the probability generating function is \[ G_X(z):=\mathbb{E}\big[z^{X}\big]=\sum_{k\ge 0}P(X=k)\,z^{k},\qquad |z|\le 1 . \tag{21.7}\] The series converges absolutely for \(|z|\le 1\) because the coefficients are a probability distribution. The mass function is recovered by differentiation at the origin, \(P(X=k)=G_X^{(k)}(0)/k!\), and the factorial moments by differentiation at one, \(\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)\cdots(X-r+1)]=G_X^{(r)}(1)\) whenever the latter derivative exists. For independent integer-valued \(X\perp Y\), \(G_{X+Y}(z)=G_X(z)\,G_Y(z)\). The PGF and the MGF are related by \(G_X(z)=M_X(\log z)\) for \(z>0\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. A probability generating function packs an entire count distribution into one polynomial in a bookkeeping variable \(z\): the probability of the count \(k\) is simply the coefficient of \(z^{k}\). Its usefulness is that multiplying two such polynomials corresponds to adding two independent counts, so a sum of many simple Bernoulli pieces becomes a product of many simple linear factors that we can expand. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The recovery and product properties asserted in Definition 21.1 are standard and elementary: term-by-term differentiation of the power series Equation 21.7 is justified within its radius of convergence, evaluation at \(z=0\) isolates the coefficient \(P(X=k)\), evaluation of the \(r\)-th derivative at \(z=1\) gives \(\sum_k k(k-1)\cdots(k-r+1)P(X=k)=\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)\cdots(X-r+1)]\), and the product rule \(G_{X+Y}(z)=\mathbb{E}[z^{X+Y}]=\mathbb{E}[z^X]\,\mathbb{E}[z^Y]\) follows from independence exactly as the MGF product rule does (Theorem 4.12). We now specialize to the Poisson binomial.

Example 21.4 (Two unequal risks, expanded by hand) Return to the two extreme profiles of the running example, response probabilities \(p_1=0.1\) (frail) and \(p_2=0.7\) (fit). The number of responders \(Y_\bullet=Y_1+Y_2\) has generating function \[ G_{Y_\bullet}(z)=(0.9+0.1z)(0.3+0.7z)=0.27+0.66\,z+0.07\,z^2, \] so, reading off coefficients, \(P(Y_\bullet=0)=0.27\), \(P(Y_\bullet=1)=0.66\), \(P(Y_\bullet=2)=0.07\). The middle coefficient \(0.1\cdot 0.3+0.9\cdot 0.7\) is exactly “one patient responds and the other does not, either way around.” This is not \(\mathrm{Bin}(2,0.4)\), which would give \(0.36,0.48,0.16\): both laws have mean \(0.8\), but the unequal risks pile mass onto exactly one responder and thin out the extremes. These are the same conditional two-patient numbers as Example 21.2; the generating function has merely organized the bookkeeping. For five profiles the same kind of product yields the six coefficients computed in Example 21.5, and for a realistic arm of \(n=400\) patients the coefficient of \(z^{120}\) is a sum of \(\binom{400}{120}\) subset products, a number exceeding \(10^{100}\); that is why direct evaluation is hopeless and a tractable surrogate is unavoidable. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 21.2 (Poisson binomial via the probability generating function) Let \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) be independent with \(Y_i\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_i)\), \(p_i\in[0,1]\), and let \(Y_\bullet=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\sim\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\) be their Poisson binomial sum (Definition 4.11), \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\). Then:

  1. (Generating function.) The probability generating function factors over individuals, \[ G_{Y_\bullet}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^n\big(1-p_i+p_i z\big). \tag{21.8}\]

  2. (Mass function.) The probabilities are the coefficients of this polynomial, \[ P(Y_\bullet=y)=[z^{y}]\prod_{i=1}^n(1-p_i+p_i z) =\sum_{\substack{A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\\ |A|=y}}\ \prod_{i\in A}p_i\prod_{i\notin A}(1-p_i), \qquad y=0,\dots,n, \tag{21.9}\] recovering the subset expansion of Definition 4.11.

  3. (Moments.) The mean and variance are \[ \mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet]=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet)=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i(1-p_i). \tag{21.10}\]

Proof. Part 1. Each Bernoulli has PGF \(G_{Y_i}(z)=\mathbb{E}[z^{Y_i}]=(1-p_i)z^{0}+p_i z^{1}=1-p_i+p_i z\). By the product property of Definition 21.1, applied inductively to the mutually independent \(Y_i\), \(G_{Y_\bullet}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^n G_{Y_i}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^n(1-p_i+p_i z)\), which is Equation 21.8.

Part 2. By Definition 21.1 the probability \(P(Y_\bullet=y)\) is the coefficient of \(z^{y}\) in \(G_{Y_\bullet}(z)\). Expanding the product Equation 21.8, a monomial \(z^{y}\) is formed by selecting the term \(p_i z\) from each factor \(i\) in some index set \(A\) of size \(y\) and the term \(1-p_i\) from each factor \(i\notin A\); summing over all such \(A\) gives \([z^{y}]\prod_i(1-p_i+p_i z)=\sum_{|A|=y}\prod_{i\in A}p_i\prod_{i\notin A}(1-p_i)\), which is Equation 21.9 and matches the mass function of Definition 4.11.

Part 3. Differentiate Equation 21.8. By the product rule, \[ G_{Y_\bullet}'(z)=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\prod_{\ell\ne i}(1-p_\ell+p_\ell z). \] At \(z=1\) every factor \(1-p_\ell+p_\ell\cdot 1=1\), so \(G_{Y_\bullet}'(1)=\sum_i p_i\), which is the first factorial moment \(\mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet]\) by Definition 21.1. Differentiating once more, \[ G_{Y_\bullet}''(z)=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\sum_{\ell\ne i}p_\ell\prod_{m\ne i,\ell}(1-p_m+p_m z), \] and at \(z=1\) the surviving products are again \(1\), giving \(G_{Y_\bullet}''(1)=\sum_{i\ne\ell}p_i p_\ell=\big(\sum_i p_i\big)^2-\sum_i p_i^2\), the second factorial moment \(\mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet(Y_\bullet-1)]\). Therefore \[ \operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet)=G_{Y_\bullet}''(1)+G_{Y_\bullet}'(1)-\big(G_{Y_\bullet}'(1)\big)^2 =\Big[\big(\textstyle\sum_i p_i\big)^2-\sum_i p_i^2\Big]+\sum_i p_i-\big(\textstyle\sum_i p_i\big)^2 =\sum_i p_i-\sum_i p_i^2, \] which is \(\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\), establishing Equation 21.10. The identity \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=G''(1)+G'(1)-G'(1)^2\) used here is the translation of \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)]+\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2\). \(\square\)

Remark. Consistency with Chapter 4 and the binomial special case. Setting \(z=e^{t}\) in Equation 21.8 recovers the MGF \(M_{Y_\bullet}(t)=\prod_i(1-p_i+p_i e^{t})\) recorded in Definition 4.11, as the relation \(G_X(z)=M_X(\log z)\) of Definition 21.1 requires. When all \(p_i\) equal a common \(p\), Equation 21.8 collapses to \((1-p+pz)^n\), the PGF of \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\), whose coefficient of \(z^y\) is \(\binom{n}{y}p^y(1-p)^{n-y}\); the Poisson binomial is the exact generalization that permits unequal risks. For fixed mean \(\lambda=\sum_i p_i\), the variance \(\lambda-\sum_i p_i^2\) is largest when the \(p_i\) are equal, because \(\sum_i p_i^2\) is minimized at fixed \(\sum_i p_i\) by equal coordinates; spreading the individual risks apart reduces the dispersion of the count, the opposite of the intuition one might carry over from the rates of the Poisson case. The intuition is that a Bernoulli with \(p\) near \(0\) or \(1\) is nearly deterministic (its variance \(p(1-p)\) is small), while \(p=\tfrac12\) is maximally uncertain; pushing the risks toward the predictable extremes lowers the total count variance. A Poisson rate has no such ceiling, so a more variable rate always inflates the count variance (Equation 21.5). That is the structural reason counts overdisperse while spread-out binary risks underdisperse. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why an approximation is unavoidable. The mass function Equation 21.9 has \(\binom{n}{y}\) terms, and the full distribution requires expanding a degree-\(n\) polynomial; while this can be organized into an \(O(n^2)\) recursion or an \(O(n\log n)\) discrete Fourier evaluation of Equation 21.8 on the roots of unity, the result must be recomputed for every candidate value of the parameter vector \(\boldsymbol\xi\), inside every iteration of the Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler of Chapter 24, for every AgD arm. In a network with many arms and thousands of patients this is prohibitive, and worse, it does not compose with the numerical integration over the covariate distribution that ML-NMR also requires (Chapter 22). The practical path is to replace the Poisson binomial by a one- or two-parameter law that captures its salient features cheaply. The next two sections develop the two such laws and, crucially, distinguish what each one guarantees. In inference terms, a faithful surrogate is one that misstates how surprising the reported count is by as little as possible, since that surprise is exactly the AgD arm’s likelihood contribution to the network. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This development corresponds to H.9 in the proof catalogue.

21.6 The Poisson approximation: Le Cam’s total-variation bound

The first surrogate replaces \(\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\) by a Poisson with the same mean \(\lambda=\sum_i p_i\). Its justification is a sharp bound on the total-variation distance between the two laws, due to Le Cam (1960), which we prove in full by the coupling method. The bound is structural and elegant: the error is at most \(\sum_i p_i^2\), the same quantity that measures how far the individual risks are from zero. We prove a sharp bound, rather than merely assert the approximation, because the bound is a ruler: it says quantitatively when the Poisson shortcut is safe (rare events) and when it silently lies (moderate risks), which is exactly what decides whether an AgD arm’s likelihood can be trusted. We first fix the meaning of total-variation distance and assemble the two elementary facts about it that the proof uses.

The proof has four moving parts, and it helps to see them before they arrive. (i) A definition of total-variation distance, the yardstick for how different two count laws are. (ii) A three-forms lemma rewriting that distance as one half the sum of absolute mass differences, which is what we actually compute. (iii) A coupling inequality: if we can place both laws on one probability space, their distance is at most the chance they disagree. (iv) An exponential inequality \(1-e^{-p}\le p\), the sole analytic input. The plan is then to couple each Bernoulli with a Poisson so they rarely disagree, make the couplings independent, add them up (the Poisson halves sum to a Poisson by Lemma 21.1), and bound the total disagreement by a union bound.

Definition 21.2 (Total-variation distance) For probability measures \(\mu,\nu\) on a countable set \(S\), the total-variation distance is \[ \|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\sup_{A\subseteq S}\big|\mu(A)-\nu(A)\big| . \tag{21.11}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Total variation is the largest gap, over every possible set of event counts, between the probability the two laws assign to that set. If two aggregate laws are \(0.17\) apart in total variation, then for any question of the form “did the arm report a count in this range?” their answers differ by at most \(0.17\) in probability. A likelihood built from the wrong law can therefore misstate how surprising an observed count is by up to that much, and that misstatement feeds straight into how strongly the AgD arm pulls the treatment-effect estimate. A total-variation bound is thus a worst-case guarantee on the likelihood error, not an abstract nicety. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Lemma 21.2 (Three forms of total variation) For probability measures \(\mu,\nu\) on a countable set \(S\), \[ \|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} =\tfrac12\sum_{k\in S}\big|\mu(k)-\nu(k)\big| =1-\sum_{k\in S}\min\big(\mu(k),\nu(k)\big). \tag{21.12}\]

Proof. Let \(A^{+}=\{k:\mu(k)>\nu(k)\}\). For any \(A\subseteq S\), \(\mu(A)-\nu(A)=\sum_{k\in A}(\mu(k)-\nu(k))\le\sum_{k\in A^{+}}(\mu(k)-\nu(k))=\mu(A^{+})-\nu(A^{+})\), because adding indices outside \(A^{+}\) can only subtract nonnegative amounts; symmetrically the most negative value of \(\mu(A)-\nu(A)\) is attained at \(A^{-}=\{k:\mu(k)<\nu(k)\}\). Since both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are probability measures, \(\sum_k(\mu(k)-\nu(k))=0\), so the total positive discrepancy equals the total negative discrepancy: \(\sum_{k\in A^{+}}(\mu(k)-\nu(k))=\sum_{k\in A^{-}}(\nu(k)-\mu(k))=\tfrac12\sum_k|\mu(k)-\nu(k)|\). Hence the supremum in Equation 21.11 equals \(\tfrac12\sum_k|\mu(k)-\nu(k)|\). For the third form, use \(\min(a,b)=\tfrac12(a+b-|a-b|)\) to write \(\sum_k\min(\mu(k),\nu(k))=\tfrac12\sum_k(\mu(k)+\nu(k))-\tfrac12\sum_k|\mu(k)-\nu(k)| =1-\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\). \(\square\)

A coupling of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) is a pair of random variables \((X,Z)\) on a common probability space with marginal laws \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). Couplings turn distances between laws into probabilities of disagreement, by the following one-directional inequality, which is all the proof needs.

Lemma 21.3 (The coupling inequality) If \((X,Z)\) is any coupling of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), then \[ \|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le P(X\ne Z). \tag{21.13}\]

Proof. Fix \(A\subseteq S\). Since \(X\) has law \(\mu\) and \(Z\) has law \(\nu\), \[ \mu(A)-\nu(A)=P(X\in A)-P(Z\in A) =P(X\in A,\,Z\notin A)-P(Z\in A,\,X\notin A)\le P(X\in A,\,Z\notin A)\le P(X\ne Z), \] where the second equality splits each event on whether the other variable lands in \(A\) and cancels the common term \(P(X\in A,Z\in A)\), and the inequalities drop a nonnegative probability and then enlarge the event \(\{X\in A,Z\notin A\}\subseteq\{X\ne Z\}\). The same bound holds for \(\nu(A)-\mu(A)\) by symmetry, so \(|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|\le P(X\ne Z)\); taking the supremum over \(A\) gives Equation 21.13. \(\square\)

Remark. Which way the inequality runs. A coupling gives an upper bound on total variation, never a lower bound, because we are free to choose the coupling that disagrees as little as possible. (Total variation equals the disagreement of the single best coupling, a converse we do not need here.) So to bound the distance we only have to exhibit one good coupling, which is what the proof does next. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The strategy of Le Cam’s proof is to build, for each individual, a coupling of its Bernoulli law with a Poisson law that disagree only rarely, then to assemble these into a coupling of the whole Poisson binomial with a Poisson by taking the couplings independent across individuals. The independent sum of the Poisson halves is Poisson by Lemma 21.1, and the probability that the two sums disagree is bounded by the union of the individual disagreements. The single-pair coupling is explicit, and the only analytic input is the inequality \(1-e^{-p}\le p\), which we record.

Lemma 21.4 (An elementary exponential inequality) For all \(p\ge 0\), \(\,e^{-p}\ge 1-p\), equivalently \(0\le 1-e^{-p}\le p\).

Proof. Let \(h(p)=e^{-p}-(1-p)\). Then \(h(0)=0\) and \(h'(p)=1-e^{-p}\ge 0\) for \(p\ge 0\), so \(h\) is nondecreasing and \(h(p)\ge h(0)=0\); thus \(e^{-p}\ge 1-p\). Rearranging gives \(1-e^{-p}\le p\), and \(1-e^{-p}\ge 0\) since \(e^{-p}\le 1\). \(\square\)

Theorem 21.3 (Le Cam’s Poisson approximation to the Poisson binomial) Let \(Y_\bullet\sim\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\) with \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\) and \(\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\), and let \(Z\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\). Then \[ \big\|\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)-\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2 . \tag{21.14}\]

Proof. Fix an index \(i\) and write \(p=p_i\). We construct a coupling \((B_i,P_i)\) of \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) and \(\mathrm{Poi}(p)\) with a small probability of disagreement. The idea is to place as much probability as possible on the diagonal \(\{B_i=P_i\}\), so the two agree whenever they can: we match \(B_i=P_i=0\) with the largest allowed mass \(1-p\), keep \(B_i=P_i=1\) where possible, and dump the leftover Poisson mass (the outcomes \(P_i\ge 2\) that a Bernoulli can never reach, plus a sliver forced by the marginals) onto \(B_i=1\). Define the joint law of \((B_i,P_i)\) on \(\{0,1\}\times\{0,1,2,\dots\}\) by \[ \begin{aligned} &P(B_i=0,P_i=0)=1-p, \\ &P(B_i=1,P_i=1)=p\,e^{-p}, \\ &P(B_i=1,P_i=0)=e^{-p}-(1-p), \\ &P(B_i=1,P_i=k)=e^{-p}\frac{p^{k}}{k!}\quad (k\ge 2), \end{aligned} \tag{21.15}\] and all other combinations probability zero. Each entry is nonnegative: the only one in question is \(P(B_i=1,P_i=0)=e^{-p}-(1-p)\ge 0\), which is Lemma 21.4. We check the marginals. The \(P_i\)-marginal at \(0\) is \((1-p)+(e^{-p}-(1-p))=e^{-p}\); at \(1\) it is \(p\,e^{-p}\); at \(k\ge 2\) it is \(e^{-p}p^{k}/k!\); these are exactly the \(\mathrm{Poi}(p)\) probabilities (Definition 4.10). The \(B_i\)-marginal at \(0\) is \(1-p\), and at \(1\) it is \[ p\,e^{-p}+\big(e^{-p}-(1-p)\big)+\sum_{k\ge 2}e^{-p}\frac{p^{k}}{k!} =e^{-p}\sum_{k\ge 0}\frac{p^{k}}{k!}-(1-p)=e^{-p}e^{p}-(1-p)=p, \] so the \(B_i\)-marginal is \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\) (Definition 4.8). Thus Equation 21.15 is a valid coupling. Its disagreement probability is the sum of the off-diagonal masses, \[ P(B_i\ne P_i)=\big(e^{-p}-(1-p)\big)+\sum_{k\ge 2}e^{-p}\frac{p^{k}}{k!} =\big(e^{-p}-1+p\big)+\big(1-e^{-p}-p\,e^{-p}\big)=p-p\,e^{-p}=p\big(1-e^{-p}\big), \] where \(\sum_{k\ge 2}e^{-p}p^{k}/k!=1-e^{-p}-p\,e^{-p}\) is the Poisson tail beyond \(k=1\). By Lemma 21.4, \(1-e^{-p}\le p\), so \[ P(B_i\ne P_i)=p_i\big(1-e^{-p_i}\big)\le p_i^2 . \tag{21.16}\]

For a concrete rate, \(p_i=0.2\) gives per-pair disagreement \(0.2(1-e^{-0.2})\approx 0.0363\), just under the bound \(p_i^2=0.04\): the coupled Bernoulli and Poisson agree about \(96\%\) of the time.

Now take the \(n\) couplings \((B_1,P_1),\dots,(B_n,P_n)\) to be independent across \(i\), on a common product probability space. Then \(B_1,\dots,B_n\) are independent with \(B_i\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_i)\), so \(Y_\bullet:=\sum_i B_i\sim\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\); and \(P_1,\dots,P_n\) are independent with \(P_i\sim\mathrm{Poi}(p_i)\), so by Lemma 21.1 \(Z:=\sum_i P_i\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\sum_i p_i)=\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\). The pair \((Y_\bullet,Z)\) is thus a coupling of \(\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\) and \(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\). If \(B_i=P_i\) for every \(i\) then \(Y_\bullet=Z\), so \(\{Y_\bullet\ne Z\}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^n\{B_i\ne P_i\}\), and the union bound with Equation 21.16 gives \[ P(Y_\bullet\ne Z)\le\sum_{i=1}^n P(B_i\ne P_i)\le\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2 . \] By the coupling inequality (Lemma 21.3) applied to this coupling, \(\|\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)-\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le P(Y_\bullet\ne Z)\le\sum_i p_i^2\), which is Equation 21.14. \(\square\)

Remark. The classical law of small numbers as a special case. When every \(p_i=p\), the Poisson binomial is \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\) and \(\lambda=np\), so Equation 21.14 reads \(\|\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)-\mathrm{Poi}(np)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le np^2\). This is the quantitative form of the Poisson limit of the binomial (Theorem 4.13): if \(p=\lambda/n\), the bound is \(\lambda^2/n\to 0\), recovering convergence in distribution and adding a rate. Le Cam’s theorem is thus the finite-sample refinement, valid for unequal risks, of the limit theorem proved in Chapter 4. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Sharpness, refinements, and when the bound is useful. The bound Equation 21.14 is of the right order: Barbour and Hall (1984) proved the sharper estimate \(\|\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)-\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le\min(1,\lambda^{-1})\sum_i p_i^2\) together with a matching lower bound of the same form up to a universal constant, via Stein’s method for Poisson approximation (the operator approach pioneered by Chen in 1975). The factor \(\min(1,\lambda^{-1})\) shows that the approximation improves when the events are spread over a large expected count. Physically, events spread over a larger expected count \(\lambda\) dilute each event’s share of the error, so the per-event total \(\sum_i p_i^2\) is discounted by \(\lambda^{-1}\); for \(\lambda>1\) the refined bound reduces to the average risk \(\bar p\) itself. The practical lesson for ML-NMR is sobering: with \(n=100\) patients at an average risk \(\bar p=0.3\), one has \(\sum_i p_i^2\approx n\bar p^2=9\) and even the refined bound \(\min(1,1/30)\cdot 9=0.3\) is large, so the Poisson surrogate is a poor approximation to a binary aggregate at the moderate risks typical of clinical events. Table 21.2 traces the verdict across event rates for \(n=100\) patients.

The Poisson approximation earns its place only when both \(\lambda\) and the individual \(p_i\) are small, the rare-event regime. 21.7 The adjusted binomial by moment matching

The second surrogate keeps the binomial family, with its bounded support \(\{0,1,\dots\}\) up to a sample size and its natural fit to event counts, but adjusts its two parameters so that its first two moments agree with those of the Poisson binomial. Unlike Le Cam’s Poisson, this surrogate carries no total-variation guarantee; its justification is exactly that it reproduces the mean and the variance, and nothing more is claimed. We make the construction and its properties precise, and we are scrupulous about what is and is not proved.

Theorem 21.4 (The two-parameter adjusted binomial) Let \(Y_\bullet\sim\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\) with \(\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i>0\) and \(\nu=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2\), so that \(\mathbb{E}[Y_\bullet]=\lambda\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_\bullet)=\lambda-\nu\) by Theorem 21.2. The unique pair \((N^{*},\pi^{*})\) with \(N^{*}>0\) and \(\pi^{*}\in(0,1)\) for which \(\mathrm{Bin}(N^{*},\pi^{*})\) has the same mean and variance, \[ N^{*}\pi^{*}=\lambda, \qquad N^{*}\pi^{*}(1-\pi^{*})=\lambda-\nu, \tag{21.17}\] is \[ \pi^{*}=\frac{\nu}{\lambda}=\frac{\sum_i p_i^2}{\sum_i p_i}, \qquad N^{*}=\frac{\lambda^2}{\nu}=\frac{\big(\sum_i p_i\big)^2}{\sum_i p_i^2}. \tag{21.18}\] Moreover:

  1. \(\pi^{*}\) is the \(p\)-weighted mean of the individual risks, \(\pi^{*}=\sum_i(p_i/\lambda)\,p_i\), and satisfies \(\pi^{*}\ge\bar p:=\lambda/n\), with equality if and only if all \(p_i\) are equal.

  2. \(N^{*}=\big(\sum_i p_i\big)^2/\sum_i p_i^2\le n\), with equality if and only if all \(p_i\) are equal; this is the effective sample size of the risk vector \(\mathbf p\), the same Cauchy-Schwarz quantity as the effective sample size of Chapter 18 (Theorem 18.6).

Proof. Solving the moment equations. From the first equation in Equation 21.17, \(N^{*}\pi^{*}=\lambda\). Expand the second, \(N^{*}\pi^{*}(1-\pi^{*})=N^{*}\pi^{*}-N^{*}(\pi^{*})^2=\lambda-N^{*}(\pi^{*})^2\), and set it equal to \(\lambda-\nu\); the \(\lambda\) terms cancel, leaving \(N^{*}(\pi^{*})^2=\nu\). Dividing this by \(N^{*}\pi^{*}=\lambda\) gives \(\pi^{*}=\nu/\lambda\), and then \(N^{*}=\lambda/\pi^{*}=\lambda^2/\nu\), which is Equation 21.18. These are well defined because \(\lambda>0\) and, unless every \(p_i=0\), \(\nu=\sum_i p_i^2>0\). Uniqueness is immediate: the two equations determine \(N^{*}(\pi^{*})^2=\nu\) and \(N^{*}\pi^{*}=\lambda\), whose ratio fixes \(\pi^{*}\) and hence \(N^{*}\). That \(\pi^{*}\in(0,1)\) holds because \(0<\nu\le\lambda\) (each \(p_i\le 1\) gives \(p_i^2\le p_i\), so \(\nu\le\lambda\), with the bound strict unless every \(p_i\in\{0,1\}\)), so \(\pi^{*}=\nu/\lambda\in(0,1]\), and \(\pi^{*}<1\) unless the risks are degenerate.

Part 1. Writing the weights \(\omega_i=p_i/\lambda\), which are nonnegative and sum to one, \(\pi^{*}=\nu/\lambda=\sum_i\omega_i p_i\) is the weighted mean of the \(p_i\) with weights proportional to the \(p_i\) themselves; larger risks receive larger weight, so the weighted mean exceeds the unweighted mean. Concretely, let \(\bar p=\lambda/n\) and let \(s_p^2=\tfrac1n\sum_i(p_i-\bar p)^2=\tfrac1n\nu-\bar p^2\) be the population variance of the risks. Then \[ \pi^{*}=\frac{\nu}{\lambda}=\frac{n(\bar p^2+s_p^2)}{n\bar p}=\bar p+\frac{s_p^2}{\bar p}\ \ge\ \bar p, \] with equality if and only if \(s_p^2=0\), that is, all \(p_i\) equal.

Part 2. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the vectors \(\mathbf 1=(1,\dots,1)\) and \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\), \[ \lambda^2=\Big(\sum_i 1\cdot p_i\Big)^2\le\Big(\sum_i 1^2\Big)\Big(\sum_i p_i^2\Big)=n\,\nu, \] so \(N^{*}=\lambda^2/\nu\le n\), with equality if and only if \(\mathbf p\) is proportional to \(\mathbf 1\), that is, all \(p_i\) equal. The expression \(\big(\sum_i p_i\big)^2/\sum_i p_i^2\) is the effective-sample-size functional of Chapter 18 (Theorem 18.6) evaluated with the \(p_i\) in the role of weights; the adjusted binomial’s “sample size” \(N^{*}\) is literally the effective number of independent equal-risk trials that the unequal-risk Poisson binomial is worth. \(\square\)

Remark. Reader translation. The adjusted binomial answers the question “which single equal-risk binomial has the same mean and spread as my unequal-risk count?” Its success probability \(\pi^{*}=\nu/\lambda\) is a risk-weighted average of the individual risks, so it sits above the plain average \(\bar p\) because higher-risk patients count more toward it, and its size \(N^{*}=\lambda^2/\nu\) is the effective number of equal-risk trials the arm is worth. Both parameters are chosen so the surrogate reproduces the count’s mean and variance exactly; nothing else is promised. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why a weighting quantity reappears here. It can seem surprising that \(N^{*}=(\sum_i p_i)^2/\sum_i p_i^2\) is the same functional as the effective sample size of matching-adjusted indirect comparison (Theorem 18.6), since one is a likelihood construction and the other a weighting diagnostic. The reason is structural: both are Cauchy-Schwarz answers to “how many equal-information units are these unequal ones worth?” In MAIC the inputs are the weights \(w_i\) and the answer is how many equally weighted patients carry the same information; here the inputs are the risks \(p_i\) and the answer is how many equal-risk Bernoulli trials carry the same mean and variance. Same inequality, different inputs. To keep the two apart, read \(N^{*}\) as the likelihood effective sample size, structurally identical to the weighting effective sample size of Chapter 18 but playing a different role, not the MAIC effective sample size of any particular analysis. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What is guaranteed, and what is not. The adjusted binomial \(\mathrm{Bin}(N^{*},\pi^{*})\) matches the Poisson binomial’s mean and variance exactly, by construction. It does not in general match the third or higher moments, and no total-variation bound of the Le Cam type (Theorem 21.3) is asserted or true for it: moment matching is a different and weaker promise than a bound on the distance between laws. The contrast with the Poisson approximation is instructive and the two should never be conflated. The Le Cam Poisson has a total-variation guarantee but the wrong variance (a Poisson has variance equal to its mean \(\lambda\), whereas the Poisson binomial has the smaller variance \(\lambda-\nu\)) and unbounded support; the adjusted binomial has the right first two moments and the correct bounded support \(\{0,\dots,N^{*}\}\) but no distance guarantee. They serve different regimes: the Poisson when \(\lambda\) and the \(p_i\) are small, the adjusted binomial when the sample size is moderate and the natural bound \(Y_\bullet\le n\) on the count is the feature that matters. In inference terms, choosing the wrong surrogate mis-scales the likelihood: the Le Cam Poisson, with variance \(\lambda>\lambda-\nu\), makes the observed count look less surprising than it is and so lets the arm speak too softly, whereas a correctly moment-matched law reports the right surprise and hence the right weight in the network. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Non-integer \(N^{*}\) and practical use. The effective sample size \(N^{*}=\lambda^2/\nu\) is generally not an integer, so \(\mathrm{Bin}(N^{*},\pi^{*})\) is used as a likelihood with a continuous size parameter, its mass function written through Gamma functions, \(P(y)=\frac{\Gamma(N^{*}+1)}{\Gamma(y+1)\,\Gamma(N^{*}-y+1)}(\pi^{*})^{y}(1-\pi^{*})^{N^{*}-y}\), which the Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler of Chapter 24 evaluates without difficulty.

A binomial with a non-integer size such as \(\mathrm{Bin}(3.7,0.45)\) looks strange at first sight; the Gamma-function form is exactly the smooth interpolation that makes sense of it, agreeing with the ordinary binomial coefficient whenever \(N^{*}\) happens to be a whole number. One caveat deserves flagging. The continuous mass is defined only for \(0\le y\le N^{*}\), and since \(N^{*}\le n\) an observed count can in principle exceed \(N^{*}\): in Example 21.5 the count \(y=5\) is possible under the true law yet falls outside the adjusted binomial’s support \(\{0,\dots,N^{*}\}=\{0,\dots,4\}\). In a real arm the reported total is a fixed number out of the real \(n\), and for the mild risk spreads usual in practice \(N^{*}\) sits close to \(n\), so \(y\le N^{*}\) holds comfortably; when it does not, the exact integrated binomial Equation 21.1 (integer \(n\), full support \(\{0,\dots,n\}\)) is the safe fallback, which is one reason implementations that anticipate occasional extreme counts prefer it.

This is the adjusted-binomial aggregate likelihood used for binary outcomes in Phillippo et al. (2020) and in the thesis Phillippo (2019) (its psoriasis case study), where the per-node event probabilities produced by the integration pipeline of Chapter 22 are summarized by their two moments \(\lambda\) and \(\nu\) and fed to Equation 21.18. Because \(N^{*}\le n\), the adjusted binomial automatically reports less information than \(n\) independent equal-risk trials would, the count-likelihood echo of the effective-sample-size deflation that weighting methods pay (Theorem 18.6). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This development corresponds to H.11 in the proof catalogue.

21.8 A fully worked aggregate binary likelihood

We carry a single AgD arm from covariate-specified individual risks to an aggregate likelihood, computing the latter three ways and laying the Le Cam Poisson alongside for contrast. Every number is exact and checkable by hand. The example is built so that the adjusted binomial has integer parameters, which keeps its mass function elementary, and so that the moment summaries \(\lambda\) and \(\nu\) come out in round numbers, which makes every law auditable by hand.

Example 21.5 (An aggregate event count by integration, exactly, and by approximation) An AgD arm reports the number of responders out of \(n=5\) patients. The study covariate distribution \(f_j\) is represented, through the integration of Chapter 22, by five equally weighted profiles, at which the individual-level model of Definition 20.1 gives the response probabilities \[ \mathbf p=(p_1,\dots,p_5)=(0.1,\ 0.3,\ 0.4,\ 0.5,\ 0.7), \tag{21.19}\] with \(p_i=\operatorname{expit}(\eta(x_i))\) for the logit link (Definition 7.2), where \(\operatorname{expit}(z)=e^{z}/(1+e^{z})\). The five profiles span a range of risks, from a low-risk profile at \(0.1\) to a high-risk profile at \(0.7\), with average risk \(0.4\).

Patients or profiles? The five values above are a numerical summary of the arm’s covariate distribution, the integration nodes of Chapter 22, not necessarily five individual patients. We nonetheless take the reported arm size to be \(n=5\) so that every count is checkable by hand; picture a stylized arm in which each profile stands for one patient. The conditional-versus-marginal question is precisely whether we hold these five profiles fixed (the conditional Poisson binomial of route two) or regard the arm as five exchangeable draws from the covariate distribution they summarize (the integrated binomial of route one).

Step 1: the summary moments. Compute \[ \lambda=\sum_i p_i=0.1+0.3+0.4+0.5+0.7=2.0, \qquad \nu=\sum_i p_i^2=0.01+0.09+0.16+0.25+0.49=1.00 . \] The aggregate response probability of Definition 20.2 is the average risk, \(\theta_\bullet=\lambda/n=2.0/5=0.4\).

Step 2: the integrated binomial (route one). Treating the five patients as an exchangeable sample from \(f_j\), Proposition 21.1 gives the aggregate count law \(\mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta_\bullet)= \mathrm{Bin}(5,0.4)\) exactly. Its mass function \(P(y)=\binom{5}{y}(0.4)^{y}(0.6)^{5-y}\) is \[ \big(P(0),\dots,P(5)\big)=(0.07776,\ 0.25920,\ 0.34560,\ 0.23040,\ 0.07680,\ 0.01024), \tag{21.20}\] with mean \(n\theta_\bullet=2.0\) and variance \(n\theta_\bullet(1-\theta_\bullet)=5(0.4)(0.6)=1.20\).

Step 3: the exact Poisson binomial (route two). Conditional on the five distinct profiles, the count is \(\mathrm{PoBin}(\mathbf p)\), whose mass function we obtain as the coefficients of the generating polynomial Equation 21.8, \[ G(z)=(0.9+0.1z)(0.7+0.3z)(0.6+0.4z)(0.5+0.5z)(0.3+0.7z). \tag{21.21}\] Multiplying the factors in turn, \[ \begin{aligned} (0.9+0.1z)(0.7+0.3z)&=0.63+0.34z+0.03z^2,\\ {}\times(0.6+0.4z)&=0.378+0.456z+0.154z^2+0.012z^3,\\ {}\times(0.5+0.5z)&=0.189+0.417z+0.305z^2+0.083z^3+0.006z^4,\\ {}\times(0.3+0.7z)&=0.0567+0.2574z+0.3834z^2+0.2384z^3+0.0599z^4+0.0042z^5, \end{aligned} \] so the exact aggregate count law is \[ \big(P(0),\dots,P(5)\big)=(0.0567,\ 0.2574,\ 0.3834,\ 0.2384,\ 0.0599,\ 0.0042). \tag{21.22}\] As a check the coefficients sum to \(1\); the mean is \(\sum_y y\,P(y)=2.0=\lambda\) and the variance is \(\sum_y y^2 P(y)-2.0^2=5.0-4.0=1.00=\lambda-\nu\), matching Theorem 21.2. The distribution is mildly right-skewed, with third central moment \(\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(1-2p_i)=0.12>0\), reflecting the spread of the underlying risks; this skew is one feature the two-moment surrogates of Steps 4 and 5 cannot reproduce.

Step 4: the adjusted binomial (route three). By Equation 21.18, \[ \pi^{*}=\frac{\nu}{\lambda}=\frac{1.0}{2.0}=0.5, \qquad N^{*}=\frac{\lambda^2}{\nu}=\frac{4.0}{1.0}=4 , \] an integer here by design, so the adjusted aggregate law is \(\mathrm{Bin}(4,0.5)\) with \(P(y)=\binom{4}{y}(0.5)^{4}=\binom{4}{y}/16\), \[ \big(P(0),\dots,P(4)\big)=(0.0625,\ 0.25,\ 0.375,\ 0.25,\ 0.0625), \qquad P(5)=0 . \tag{21.23}\] Its mean is \(N^{*}\pi^{*}=2.0\) and variance \(N^{*}\pi^{*}(1-\pi^{*})=4(0.5)(0.5)=1.00\), matching the exact Poisson binomial mean and variance by construction. Note \(N^{*}=4<5=n\), the Cauchy-Schwarz deflation of Theorem 21.4; the adjusted binomial cannot produce the count \(5\), whereas the exact Poisson binomial assigns it probability \(0.0042\).

Step 5: the Le Cam Poisson, for contrast. The matched Poisson is \(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)=\mathrm{Poi}(2)\), with \(P(y)=e^{-2}2^{y}/y!\), \[ \big(P(0),\dots,P(5)\big)=(0.1353,\ 0.2707,\ 0.2707,\ 0.1804,\ 0.0902,\ 0.0361),\ \dots \tag{21.24}\] its support unbounded. Its mean is \(2.0\) but its variance is \(\lambda=2.0\), far larger than the true \(1.00\): the Poisson cannot capture the underdispersion of a binary count.

Table 21.3 places the four laws side by side before we read off the reported count.

Step 6: compare the likelihoods. Suppose the arm reports \(y_\bullet=2\) responders, the modal count. The aggregate likelihood contribution under each model is the value at \(y=2\): \[ \underbrace{0.3834}_{\text{exact PoBin}},\quad \underbrace{0.375}_{\text{adjusted }\mathrm{Bin}(4,0.5)},\quad \underbrace{0.3456}_{\text{integrated }\mathrm{Bin}(5,0.4)},\quad \underbrace{0.2707}_{\text{Le Cam }\mathrm{Poi}(2)} . \] The adjusted binomial is within \(0.0084\) of the exact value, the integrated binomial within \(0.0378\), and the Le Cam Poisson off by \(0.1127\). The whole-distribution discrepancies tell the same story through the total-variation distance Equation 21.12: \[ \big\|\mathrm{PoBin}-\mathrm{Bin}(4,0.5)\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=0.0200, \qquad \big\|\mathrm{PoBin}-\mathrm{Poi}(2)\big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=0.1707 . \] The Le Cam bound Equation 21.14 for this arm is \(\sum_i p_i^2=\nu=1.00\), and the Barbour-Hall refinement is \(\min(1,\lambda^{-1})\nu=0.5\); both are correct (the actual distance \(0.1707\) is well below them) but far too loose to be useful, exactly the moderate-risk regime where the Poisson approximation is poor.

Step 7: reconcile the two binomials. The integrated binomial Equation 21.20 and the exact Poisson binomial Equation 21.22 share the mean \(2.0\) but differ in variance, \(1.20\) against \(1.00\). The difference is \[ n\theta_\bullet(1-\theta_\bullet)-(\lambda-\nu)=\nu-\frac{\lambda^2}{n}=1.00-\frac{4.0}{5}=0.20 =n\,s_p^2, \] the population variance of the five risks times the sample size, \(s_p^2=\tfrac1n\nu-\bar p^2=0.20-0.16=0.04\). This is the binary shadow of the mixed-Poisson overdispersion Equation 21.5: randomizing which profile a patient occupies, as the integrated binomial does, adds variance \(n\,s_p^2\) over the conditional law that fixes one patient at each profile. The adjusted binomial Equation 21.23 tracks the conditional Poisson binomial (right variance \(1.00\)), while the integrated binomial Equation 21.20 is the exact marginal law (variance \(1.20\)); both are legitimate answers to slightly different questions, and the worked numbers make the distinction concrete. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Which likelihood to report. For a genuine exchangeable sample of patients, the integrated binomial Equation 21.20 is exact and is the default ML-NMR choice (Proposition 21.1, Phillippo et al. (2020)). When one instead conditions on a fixed set of representative profiles, as the deterministic integration nodes of Chapter 22 are, the conditional Poisson binomial is the target and the adjusted binomial Equation 21.23 is its faithful two-moment surrogate. The Le Cam Poisson is never competitive at these risks; its value is theoretical, as the sharp total-variation statement of Theorem 21.3 and as the bridge to the Poisson limit of Chapter 4. The single discipline that matters in practice is to match the variance, which both the integrated and the adjusted binomial do for their respective targets and the Poisson does not. \(\blacktriangleleft\)
