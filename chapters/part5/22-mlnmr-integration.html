22  Multilevel Network Meta-Regression III: Numerical Integration Theory

22.1 Introduction

Here is the practical problem this chapter solves, stated for a health researcher before any mathematics. Picture an aggregate-data trial of a new plaque-psoriasis drug that publishes only summaries: the mean patient age, the mean baseline severity score, and the proportion of patients who are biomarker-positive. We want the average probability that a patient on this arm achieves a treatment response, so that the trial can be entered into a network and the drug compared indirectly against its competitors. Because the response probability is a nonlinear (logistic) function of a patient’s covariates, that average is an integral over the arm’s covariate distribution; and if we compute the integral badly, the modeled response rate is wrong, the odds ratio of this drug versus a comparator is wrong, and the reimbursement recommendation a health technology assessment (HTA) agency issues on the strength of that odds ratio is wrong. This chapter is the mathematics of computing that integral well. Its organizing idea, which we announce now and prove at the end (Section 22.10), is that the covariate points at which we evaluate the model are chosen once, deterministically, and reused unchanged at every step of the model fit. That single decision, invisible in the statement of the integral, is exactly what separates the method used in practice from a naive random simulation, and it is the reason the chapter builds toward deterministic quasi-Monte Carlo rather than stopping at ordinary Monte Carlo.

Chapter 20 reduced the entire aggregate-data contribution of multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) to a single integral. For an aggregate-data (AgD) study \(j\) reporting an arm \(k\) with covariate density \(f_j\), the aggregate-level mean outcome is the response-scale average of the individual conditional mean (Definition 20.2), \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{X}} g^{-1}\big(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}, \qquad \eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})=\mu_j+\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_1+\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}+\gamma_k, \tag{22.1}\] and the aggregation theorem (Theorem 20.1) showed that the AgD likelihood is built from this one quantity. The aggregation-bias theorem (Theorem 20.2) explained why the integral cannot be shortcut by plugging the covariate mean into \(g^{-1}\): for a nonlinear link the average of \(g^{-1}(\eta)\) is not \(g^{-1}\) of the average. Two links escaped: the identity link, where Equation 22.1 collapses to a function of the mean alone (Theorem 20.3), and the log link with jointly Gaussian covariates, where the moment generating function (Definition 4.7) supplies a closed form (Theorem 20.4). The link that matters most for health technology assessment, the logit, escaped neither: Equation 22.1 has no elementary closed form for any non-degenerate covariate distribution (Theorem 20.5). The discrete-outcome aggregate likelihoods of Chapter 21, the Poisson and Poisson-binomial constructions (Theorem 21.1, Theorem 21.2) and their approximations (Theorem 21.3, Theorem 21.4), all rest on the same integral through the success probabilities \(p_{ijk}=g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\). ML-NMR is therefore only as good as our ability to evaluate Equation 22.1 numerically. This chapter builds that ability from the ground up and proves the error guarantees that make it trustworthy.

Two features of the problem dictate the machinery. First, the dimension. The integral runs over \(\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\) where \(d\) is the number of covariates entered into the model, typically three to ten. Deterministic product quadrature, which is essentially perfect in one dimension, collapses under the curse of dimensionality: \(k\) nodes per axis means \(k^{d}\) nodes in total, and the cost of a fixed accuracy grows exponentially in \(d\) (Theorem 22.1, Corollary 22.1). Plain Monte Carlo (MC) integration escapes the curse with a dimension-free root-mean-square error of order \(N^{-1/2}\) (Theorem 22.2), but \(N^{-1/2}\) is slow, and, more damagingly, the re-randomization it requires injects noise into the likelihood and destroys the smooth Hamiltonian that gradient-based Markov chain Monte Carlo needs (Definition 8.9, Carpenter et al. (2017)). Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) keeps a single deterministic point set and replaces randomness by uniformity, achieving an error of order \((\log N)^{d}/N\), nearly \(1/N\), governed by the Koksma-Hlawka inequality (Theorem 22.3) and realized by low-discrepancy sequences such as Sobol’ points (Definition 22.3). This is the integration rule used throughout ML-NMR.

Second, the input. We do not know \(f_j\). An AgD study reports marginal summaries of each covariate, a mean and sometimes a standard deviation or a proportion, but almost never the joint distribution. The device that turns marginal information into a full joint law is the copula. Sklar’s theorem (Theorem 22.5) separates any joint distribution into its marginals and a copula carrying the dependence; the Gaussian copula (Definition 22.5) supplies a default dependence structure parameterized by a correlation matrix borrowed from individual patient data (IPD) or expert judgment; and the probability integral transform (Theorem 22.4) maps uniform inputs onto whatever marginal distribution each covariate is assumed to follow. Assembling these pieces gives the ML-NMR integration pipeline (Theorem 22.6): generate Sobol’ points in the unit hypercube, push them through a Gaussian copula and inverse-CDF margins to land at covariate vectors distributed as \(f_j\), and average \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk})\) over them. We close with a fully worked logit example whose every node, weight, and running estimate the reader can reproduce by hand, and a two-covariate trace that exhibits the copula step explicitly.

Throughout, \(g\) is the link function (Definition 7.2), \(\eta_{jk}\) the linear predictor of the individual model (Definition 20.1), and \(\Phi,\phi\) the standard normal distribution and density functions. We write \(\mathrm{expit}(z)=g^{-1}(z)=e^{z}/(1+e^{z})\) for the inverse logit, also called the logistic function; a health researcher meets it as the map that turns a log-odds back into a probability. The covariate density and distribution functions of study \(j\) are \(f_j\) and \(F_j\), with marginal distribution functions \(F_{j,1},\dots,F_{j,d}\) and covariate correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j\), all from Notation. This chapter corresponds to results H.12 to H.19 of the proof catalogue.

We fix one concrete scenario now and return to it at every transition, so that each abstract object acquires a definite job. It is the running thread of the chapter.

Example 22.1 (Running example: an aggregate psoriasis arm) An aggregate-data (AgD) trial arm of a psoriasis biologic reports three covariate summaries and nothing about their joint distribution: mean age \(45\) years (standard deviation \(12\)), mean baseline severity score \(18\) (standard deviation \(6\)), and proportion biomarker-positive \(0.30\). So \(d=3\) covariates enter the model: \(x_1=\text{age}\), \(x_2=\text{baseline severity}\), \(x_3=\text{biomarker status}\). From an individual-patient-data (IPD) study in the same network we have fitted the individual model, a logistic regression whose linear predictor \(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\) combines a study baseline, prognostic effects, and treatment-by-covariate interactions (the effect modifiers), and from that IPD study we also borrow a covariate correlation matrix, say age and severity correlated at \(0.3\). Our target is the arm’s average response probability \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{expit}\big(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}, \] the number that, contrasted with the same quantity on a comparator arm, becomes the odds ratio the HTA agency will act on. Everything the chapter builds is in service of computing this one integral: the margins (\(x_1,x_2\) modeled as normal from the reported means and standard deviations, \(x_3\) as a Bernoulli from the reported proportion) fix each covariate’s own distribution; the copula supplies the missing dependence (which ages tend to accompany which severities); the Sobol’ nodes are the covariate profiles at which we evaluate \(\mathrm{expit}(\eta_{jk})\); and the average over those profiles is \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\). In Section 22.11 we carry a stripped-down version of this arm, a single standardized severity covariate, all the way through the arithmetic to a final number a reader can check by hand. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

What this chapter will do, in order. The path from the integral to a trustworthy number has two halves, a numerical half and a dependence-modeling half, and they meet in the pipeline of Section 22.10.

  • Show why a covariate grid is hopeless beyond a few covariates (Section 22.3).
  • Escape the curse with random Monte Carlo, then see why its noise is fatal inside a Bayesian fit (Section 22.4).
  • Replace random points with evenly spread deterministic ones and bound the error by the Koksma-Hlawka inequality (Section 22.5), realized by Sobol’ nets (Section 22.6).
  • Build synthetic covariate profiles from an AgD study’s summaries using the probability integral transform (Section 22.7), Sklar’s theorem (Section 22.8), and the Gaussian copula (Section 22.9).
  • Assemble the pipeline and prove its error bound (Section 22.10), then compute a full logit example by hand (Section 22.11).

22.2 Reducing the aggregation integral to the unit cube

Every method in this chapter computes an integral against a probability density. It is convenient to fix, once, the two equivalent forms in which Equation 22.1 will appear. The first is the expectation form \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f_j}\big[g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{X}))\big], \tag{22.2}\] which is what MC estimates directly by sampling \(\mathbf{X}\). The second is the unit-cube form. Suppose we have a map \(T:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathcal{X}\) that transports the uniform distribution on the cube to \(f_j\), in the sense that \(\mathbf{X}=T(\mathbf{U})\) has density \(f_j\) whenever \(\mathbf{U}\) is uniform on \([0,1]^{d}\). In plain terms, \(T\) is the recipe that turns a list of uniform numbers between \(0\) and \(1\) into a plausible covariate profile for the aggregate study: feed it \((0.5,0.5,0.5)\) and it returns a patient of median age, median severity, and so on; feed it a whole grid of uniform inputs and it returns a whole cloud of synthetic patients whose distribution matches \(f_j\). Constructing this recipe from the marginal and correlation summaries an AgD study reports is the entire job of Section 22.8 to Section 22.10.

Then, writing \(\varphi:=g^{-1}\circ\eta_{jk}\) for the integrand on covariate space and \(\psi:=\varphi\circ T\) for its pullback to the cube (the same integrand, re-expressed as a function of the uniform inputs \(\mathbf{u}\) rather than of the covariates \(\mathbf{x}=T(\mathbf{u})\)), the change-of-variables identity gives \[ \theta_{\bullet jk} =\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(\mathbf{x})\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{U}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})}\big[\psi(\mathbf{U})\big] =\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\psi(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}. \tag{22.3}\]

All Equation 22.3 does is rename the variable of integration: the same average, first written over covariate space with weight \(f_j\), is rewritten over the unit cube with the flat (uniform) weight, at the price of pushing the covariate values through the recipe \(T\) inside the integrand. It is worth seeing a first number before the machinery. Take the running example (Example 22.1) stripped to a single standardized severity covariate with a standard normal margin, so \(T=\Phi^{-1}\) and, with a treatment arm whose linear predictor is \(\eta(x)=0.5+x\), the integrand on the cube is \(\psi(u)=\mathrm{expit}\big(0.5+\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)\). Three evenly spread inputs already give a rough answer:

Their average is \(0.6143\), already within about \(0.012\) of the true value \(\theta_\bullet=0.6020\) that Section 22.11 computes carefully. Everything between here and there is about choosing those input points so that the average converges fast and smoothly, and about building \(T\) for realistic multi-covariate arms.

The whole of the dependence-modeling apparatus, Sklar’s theorem, the Gaussian copula, and the inverse-CDF transform, exists to construct the transport map \(T\) from the marginal and correlation summaries that an AgD study reports; that construction is Section 22.8 to Section 22.10. The whole of the numerical apparatus, quadrature, MC, and QMC, exists to approximate the cube integral on the right of Equation 22.3 by a finite average over points \(\{\mathbf{u}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset[0,1]^{d}\), \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}\approx\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)} =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\psi(\mathbf{u}^{(n)}) =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g^{-1}\big(\eta_{jk}(T(\mathbf{u}^{(n)}))\big); \tag{22.4}\] that approximation, and its error, is Section 22.3 to Section 22.6. The single design decision that ties the two halves together, and that distinguishes ML-NMR from a naive simulation, is that the point set \(\{\mathbf{u}^{(n)}\}\) and the covariate nodes \(\{T(\mathbf{u}^{(n)})\}\) are fixed in advance and reused at every evaluation of the likelihood; only \(\eta_{jk}\), through the parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\), changes as the sampler explores the posterior. We return to the consequences of that decision in Section 22.10.

One notational caution before the numerical theory. The next three sections (Section 22.3 to Section 22.5) are general facts about integrating a function over an interval or a cube, so they call that function a generic \(f\), in keeping with the numerical-analysis literature. This generic integrand \(f\) is not the covariate density: our covariate density is \(f_j\), and the specific integrand those theorems get applied to is the ML-NMR pullback \(\psi\) of Equation 22.3. When in doubt, \(f\) inside a quadrature or discrepancy theorem means “whatever function is being integrated,” and for us that function is \(\psi\).

22.3 Gaussian quadrature and the curse of dimensionality

This section answers the first question a numerically minded reader asks: why not just lay down a grid of covariate values, evaluate the model on the grid, and average? In one dimension a well-chosen grid is not merely adequate, it is superb, and we prove exactly how superb. The catch is that the obvious way to build a grid in several dimensions multiplies its size beyond reach, and that failure is what forces the rest of the chapter. We begin with the gold standard for one-dimensional integration of a smooth function and then show why it does not survive the passage to several covariates. The rule is Gauss-Legendre quadrature: choose \(k\) nodes and \(k\) weights, \(2k\) numbers in all, and spend that budget so as to integrate exactly every polynomial of degree up to \(2k-1\). That maximal exactness is what gives Gauss quadrature its super-algebraic accuracy on smooth integrands.

We prove the one-dimensional error formula in full, even though the curse of dimensionality will make us abandon the method, because its error structure is the benchmark against which Monte Carlo and quasi-Monte Carlo are measured for the rest of the chapter: it is the accuracy we are trading away in exchange for freedom from the dimension. The proof uses two classical facts about the Legendre polynomials \(P_0,P_1,P_2,\dots\), the orthogonal polynomials on \([-1,1]\) (each \(P_k\) has degree \(k\), and \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_k(x)q(x)\,dx=0\) for every polynomial \(q\) of degree below \(k\); this orthogonality is the one property we use). The second tool is Hermite interpolation: given \(k\) nodes, the unique polynomial of degree at most \(2k-1\) that matches both \(f\) and its derivative \(f'\) at every node, whose remainder we quote in the proof. Both are standard (Stoer and Bulirsch, Section 3.6, cited in Section 22.12); a reader willing to take the error formula Equation 22.5 on faith can skip the proof and lose nothing needed later.

Theorem 22.1 (Gauss-Legendre quadrature error) Let \(f\in C^{2k}[-1,1]\), that is, \(f\) has \(2k\) continuous derivatives on \([-1,1]\). Let \(x_1,\dots,x_k\) be the \(k\) roots of the Legendre polynomial \(P_k\) (orthogonal on \([-1,1]\) with respect to Lebesgue measure), and let \(w_1,\dots,w_k>0\) be the unique weights for which the rule \[ Q_k[f]:=\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i\,f(x_i) \] is exact on polynomials of degree at most \(k-1\). Then:

  1. (Degree of exactness.) \(Q_k[p]=\int_{-1}^{1}p(x)\,dx\) for every polynomial \(p\) of degree at most \(2k-1\).

  2. (Error formula.) There exists \(\xi\in(-1,1)\) such that \[ \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)\,dx-Q_k[f] =\frac{2^{2k+1}\,(k!)^{4}}{(2k+1)\,[(2k)!]^{3}}\;f^{(2k)}(\xi). \tag{22.5}\]

  3. (Composite order.) If \([-1,1]\) is partitioned into panels of width \(h\) and the \(k\)-node rule is applied on each panel, the total error is \(O(h^{2k})\) as \(h\to 0\) with \(k\) fixed and \(f^{(2k)}\) bounded.

Proof. Part 1. The defining property of the Legendre polynomial \(P_k\) is orthogonality: \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_k(x)q(x)\,dx=0\) for every polynomial \(q\) with \(\deg q<k\) (a classical fact about orthogonal polynomials; see the references). Let \(p\) have \(\deg p\le 2k-1\). Polynomial division by \(P_k\) writes \(p=q\,P_k+r\) with \(\deg q\le k-1\) and \(\deg r\le k-1\). Integrating, \[ \int_{-1}^{1}p=\int_{-1}^{1}q\,P_k+\int_{-1}^{1}r=0+\int_{-1}^{1}r, \] the first integral vanishing by orthogonality since \(\deg q<k\). Evaluating the rule, and using \(P_k(x_i)=0\) at every node, \[ Q_k[p]=\sum_i w_i\big(q(x_i)P_k(x_i)+r(x_i)\big)=\sum_i w_i\,r(x_i)=Q_k[r]. \] Because \(\deg r\le k-1\), the rule integrates \(r\) exactly by its defining property, so \(Q_k[r]=\int_{-1}^{1}r\). Combining, \(Q_k[p]=\int_{-1}^{1}r=\int_{-1}^{1}p\).

Part 2. Let \(H\) be the Hermite interpolating polynomial of degree at most \(2k-1\) that matches \(f\) and \(f'\) at each node: \(H(x_i)=f(x_i)\) and \(H'(x_i)=f'(x_i)\) for \(i=1,\dots,k\). The Hermite remainder formula states that for each \(x\in[-1,1]\) there is \(\zeta_x\in(-1,1)\) with \[ f(x)-H(x)=\frac{f^{(2k)}(\zeta_x)}{(2k)!}\,\omega(x)^{2}, \qquad \omega(x):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-x_i). \] Since \(\deg H\le 2k-1\), Part 1 gives \(Q_k[H]=\int_{-1}^{1}H\), and since \(H(x_i)=f(x_i)\) we have \(Q_k[H]=Q_k[f]\). Therefore \[ \int_{-1}^{1}f-Q_k[f]=\int_{-1}^{1}(f-H) =\frac{1}{(2k)!}\int_{-1}^{1}f^{(2k)}(\zeta_x)\,\omega(x)^{2}\,dx. \] The kernel \(\omega(x)^{2}\ge 0\), so the integral mean value theorem yields a single \(\xi\in(-1,1)\) with \[ \int_{-1}^{1}f-Q_k[f]=\frac{f^{(2k)}(\xi)}{(2k)!}\int_{-1}^{1}\omega(x)^{2}\,dx. \] It remains to evaluate \(\int_{-1}^{1}\omega^{2}\). The nodes are the roots of \(P_k\), so \(\omega\) is the monic Legendre polynomial: \(\omega=P_k/c_k\), where \(c_k=\dfrac{(2k)!}{2^{k}(k!)^{2}}\) is the leading coefficient of the standard Legendre polynomial. With the normalization \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_k(x)^{2}\,dx=\dfrac{2}{2k+1}\), \[ \int_{-1}^{1}\omega(x)^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{c_k^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_k^{2} =\frac{2^{2k}(k!)^{4}}{[(2k)!]^{2}}\cdot\frac{2}{2k+1} =\frac{2^{2k+1}(k!)^{4}}{(2k+1)[(2k)!]^{2}}. \] Dividing by \((2k)!\) gives the constant in Equation 22.5.

Part 3. On a panel \([a,a+h]\) substitute \(x=a+\tfrac{h}{2}(1+t)\), so \(\int_a^{a+h}f=\tfrac{h}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\tilde f(t)\,dt\) with \(\tilde f(t)=f(a+\tfrac{h}{2}(1+t))\) and \(\tilde f^{(2k)}=(h/2)^{2k}f^{(2k)}\). Applying Part 2 to \(\tilde f\), the panel error is \[ \frac{h}{2}\cdot\frac{2^{2k+1}(k!)^{4}}{(2k+1)[(2k)!]^{3}}\Big(\tfrac{h}{2}\Big)^{2k}f^{(2k)}(\xi_a) =C_k\,h^{2k+1}f^{(2k)}(\xi_a), \] with \(C_k=\dfrac{(k!)^{4}}{(2k+1)[(2k)!]^{3}}\) a constant depending only on \(k\). Summing over the \(2/h\) panels and bounding \(|f^{(2k)}|\) by its supremum, \[ \Big|\int_{-1}^{1}f-\sum_{\text{panels}}Q_k\Big| \le \frac{2}{h}\cdot C_k\,h^{2k+1}\sup_{[-1,1]}|f^{(2k)}| =2\,C_k\,h^{2k}\sup_{[-1,1]}|f^{(2k)}|=O(h^{2k}).\qquad\square \]

Remark. What “order \(2k\)” buys, and what it cannot. The single-interval constant Equation 22.5 decays super-exponentially in \(k\): by Stirling’s approximation \(\dfrac{2^{2k+1}(k!)^{4}}{(2k+1)[(2k)!]^{3}}\sim\dfrac{\pi}{4^{2k}\,(2k)!}\) up to lower-order factors, so for an analytic integrand whose derivatives satisfy \(|f^{(2k)}|\le (2k)!\,R^{-2k}\) (radius of convergence \(R>1\)) the error falls like \(R^{-2k}\), geometric in the number of nodes. This is the spectral accuracy that makes a handful of Gauss nodes integrate a smooth one-dimensional function to machine precision. The composite reading of Part 3, error \(O(h^{2k})\) at fixed \(k\), is the form quoted in the catalogue; it is the relevant one when the integrand has limited smoothness and one refines the mesh rather than raising the node count. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The trouble is that ML-NMR integrates over several covariates at once, and the only elementary way to lift a one-dimensional rule to \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is to take a tensor product: place a grid of \(k^{d}\) nodes, one factor per axis. The accuracy per axis is inherited, but the node count is not.

Corollary 22.1 (The curse of dimensionality for product quadrature) Let \(f\in C^{2k}([-1,1]^{d})\) with all pure \(2k\)-th order partial derivatives bounded. The tensor-product Gauss-Legendre rule with \(k\) nodes per axis uses \(N=k^{d}\) function evaluations and has error \[ \Big|\int_{[-1,1]^{d}} f-\sum_{i_1=1}^{k}\cdots\sum_{i_d=1}^{k} w_{i_1}\cdots w_{i_d}\, f(x_{i_1},\dots,x_{i_d})\Big|=O\!\big(k^{-2k}\big)=O\!\big(N^{-2k/d}\big). \] For fixed per-axis budget \(k\), the exponent \(2k/d\) of \(N\) degrades with the dimension \(d\); equivalently, to hold the error at a tolerance \(\epsilon\) the cost grows like \(N=k^{d}\), exponentially in \(d\).

Proof. Write the cube integral as an iterated integral and apply Theorem 22.1 one axis at a time. Holding \(x_2,\dots,x_d\) fixed, the one-dimensional rule in \(x_1\) incurs error bounded by \(E_k\sup|\partial_1^{2k} f|\), where \(E_k=\frac{2^{2k+1}(k!)^4}{(2k+1)[(2k)!]^3}\) is the single-interval constant; integrating that bound over the remaining axes and then quadrating them in turn, the total error is at most \(E_k\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sup|\partial_m^{2k} f|\cdot 2^{d-1}\), a finite multiple of \(E_k=O(k^{-2k})\); the factor \(2^{d-1}\) is the product of the interval lengths accumulated as each already-quadrated axis contributes its width \(2\) to the bound. Writing the node count as \(N=k^{d}\), so that \(k=N^{1/d}\), the error \(O(k^{-2k})\) becomes \(O(N^{-2k/d})\). \(\square\)

The exponent \(2k/d\) tells the whole story, and it is worth reading carefully because it is easy to misread. With \(k\) held fixed, \(-2k/d\) is a constant, so refining a product grid does not improve the rate with \(N\); and raising \(k\) to sharpen the per-axis accuracy is self-defeating, because it also multiplies the node count \(N=k^{d}\). In one dimension a five-node rule (\(k=5\)) integrates a smooth function with error \(O(N^{-10})\), spectacular; in ten dimensions the same per-axis rule needs \(N=5^{10}\approx 10^{7}\) nodes just to place five points on each axis, and reaching error \(O(N^{-1})\) is all one can hope for. To make the threshold concrete, return to the running example (Example 22.1). Three covariates at five nodes each is \(5^{3}=125\) nodes, which sounds harmless. But this integral is not evaluated once. It is evaluated once per AgD arm; a plaque-psoriasis network may carry a dozen AgD arms; and the whole set of integrals is recomputed at every leapfrog step of every iteration of the Markov chain Monte Carlo fit, tens of thousands of times. At \(d=3\) the \(125\)-node grid is tolerable, but real ML-NMR analyses reach into more covariates: in a plaque-psoriasis network the effect modifiers may include age, baseline severity, disease duration, weight, and biomarker status; in a network of newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) treatments they may include age, disease stage, and cytogenetic risk. At \(d=8\), five nodes per axis is \(5^{8}\approx 3.9\times 10^{5}\) nodes, multiplied again by arms and by tens of thousands of posterior evaluations: product quadrature is out. ML-NMR routinely runs at \(d=3\) to \(10\), and the next two sections develop the two ways around the curse, both of which decouple the error rate from \(d\) in the exponent.

Remark. Why this matters for indirect comparison. Everything downstream of ML-NMR depends on \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\) being right. It is the modeled event rate on an AgD arm; the contrast of two such rates on the linear-predictor scale is the relative effect that the network synthesizes, and it is the number an HTA agency reads as the odds ratio of one drug versus another. A biased integral biases that odds ratio and can flip a cost-effectiveness verdict. Product quadrature would in principle deliver the integral to near-machine precision, but at the dimensions and repetition counts of a real network it is computationally impossible, which is exactly why the reliable-but-slow and reliable-but-fast alternatives of the following sections are needed rather than merely nice to have. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.4 Monte Carlo integration and its dimension-free rate

Monte Carlo integration replaces the deterministic grid by independent random draws. The plain-language idea is one every health researcher already trusts: to estimate an average over a population, draw a random sample and average over the sample. Here the “population” is the arm’s covariate distribution \(f_j\) and the quantity averaged is the response probability \(\mathrm{expit}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\), so we simulate many synthetic patients from \(f_j\), compute each one’s modeled response probability, and average. The larger the sample, the closer the sample average sits to the true integral; the price is that a random sample is noisy, and halving that noise takes four times the sample. Its error analysis is nothing more than the variance of a sample mean, but the conclusion is striking: the rate of convergence does not depend on the dimension at all.

Theorem 22.2 (Monte Carlo root-mean-square error) Let \(\mathbf{U}_1,\dots,\mathbf{U}_N\) be independent and identically distributed with common density \(f\) on a domain \(\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\), let \(h:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfy \(\sigma^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_f(h(\mathbf{U}))<\infty\), and let \(I=\mathbb{E}_f[h(\mathbf{U})]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}h\,f\). The Monte Carlo estimator \(\widehat I_N=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(\mathbf{U}_n)\) satisfies:

  1. (Unbiasedness.) \(\mathbb{E}[\widehat I_N]=I\).

  2. (Root-mean-square error.) \(\mathrm{Var}(\widehat I_N)=\sigma^{2}/N\), hence the root-mean-square error is \[ \mathrm{RMSE}(\widehat I_N)=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat I_N-I)^{2}\big]}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}=O(N^{-1/2}), \tag{22.6}\] a rate that does not depend on the dimension \(d\).

  3. (Asymptotic normality.) \(\sqrt N\,(\widehat I_N-I)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\).

Proof. Each summand \(h(\mathbf{U}_n)\) has, by construction, mean \(I\) and variance \(\sigma^{2}\).

Part 1. By linearity of expectation, \(\mathbb{E}[\widehat I_N]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[h(\mathbf{U}_n)]=\frac{1}{N}\cdot N I=I\).

Part 2. The draws are independent, so the covariances between distinct summands vanish and the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances: \[ \mathrm{Var}(\widehat I_N)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{Var}\big(h(\mathbf{U}_n)\big) =\frac{1}{N^{2}}\cdot N\sigma^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}. \] Because \(\widehat I_N\) is unbiased, its mean-square error equals its variance, and the root-mean-square error is its square root, \(\sigma/\sqrt N\). The variance \(\sigma^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\big(h(\mathbf{u})-I\big)^{2}f(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\) is a single number attached to the integrand and the sampling law; the dimension \(d\) enters only through that number, never through the exponent of \(N\).

Part 3. The variables \(h(\mathbf{U}_n)\) are independent and identically distributed with finite variance \(\sigma^{2}\), so the Lindeberg-Lévy central limit theorem (the independent-and-identically-distributed central limit theorem proved in Chapter 5) gives \(\sqrt N\,(\widehat I_N-I)=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(h(\mathbf{U}_n)-I\big)\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\). \(\square\)

Remark. A free variance bound for bounded links. For the logit link the integrand of Equation 22.3 is \(\psi=\mathrm{expit}\circ\eta_{jk}\circ T\in(0,1)\). A random variable supported in \([0,1]\) has variance at most \(1/4\) (Popoviciu’s inequality: the variance of a \([a,b]\)-valued variable is at most \((b-a)^2/4\), attained by the two-point law at the endpoints). Hence \(\sigma^{2}\le 1/4\) and, for the logit aggregate mean, the Monte Carlo root-mean-square error obeys the explicit, distribution-free bound \(\mathrm{RMSE}\le 1/(2\sqrt N)\) regardless of the covariate dimension. With \(N=10^{4}\) draws this is at most \(0.005\) on the probability scale. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why ML-NMR does not use plain Monte Carlo. Two defects rule it out as the integration rule inside a Bayesian fit. The first is the rate: \(N^{-1/2}\) is slow, and the integral in Equation 22.1 is re-evaluated once per study and arm at every leapfrog step of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Definition 8.9), so a slow rule multiplies the cost of the entire posterior computation. The second, and decisive, defect is stochasticity. If the points \(\mathbf{U}_n\) are redrawn at each likelihood evaluation, then \(\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) is a random function of the parameters, its gradient in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) is corrupted by Monte Carlo noise, and the Hamiltonian that Hamiltonian Monte Carlo integrates is no longer conserved; the sampler’s acceptance probability degrades and its stationarity guarantee (Theorem 8.9) is lost. Fixing a single point set removes the noise but forfeits the independence on which Theorem 22.2 rests. The resolution, deterministic low-discrepancy points, is the subject of the next section. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. In plain terms: why a jittery likelihood breaks the fit. The Bayesian fit that ML-NMR runs (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, Definition 8.9, Chapter 24) explores the space of parameter values by rolling a ball across the likelihood surface, using the slope (the gradient) of that surface to decide where to go next. This works only if the surface is a fixed, smooth landscape: the same parameters must always give the same likelihood, and nearby parameters must give nearby likelihoods. If we recompute the aggregation integral with a fresh random sample of synthetic patients at each step, the surface trembles underfoot, the same parameters give a slightly different height each time, the slope is corrupted by that trembling, and the ball wanders instead of settling into the posterior. The cure is to fix the synthetic patients once, so the surface stands still. That makes the likelihood reproducible and differentiable, at the cost of giving up the independence that the Monte Carlo error bound relied on, which is why we need a new, deterministic error theory. The three natural point-set choices line up as follows.

Point-set choice Error rate Reproducible? Smooth in the parameters? Verdict for ML-NMR
Fresh random points, redrawn each step \(O(N^{-1/2})\) no no (jitters) breaks the fit
Fixed pseudorandom points, drawn once \(O(N^{-1/2})\) yes yes usable but slow
Fixed Sobol’ (low-discrepancy) points \(O((\log N)^{d}/N)\) yes yes the method used

Fixing the points already buys reproducibility and smoothness; choosing them to be evenly spread rather than random buys, in addition, the far faster error rate. The next section proves that faster rate.

22.5 Quasi-Monte Carlo: the Koksma-Hlawka inequality

Monte Carlo gave a probabilistic error, a variance that shrinks on average but leaves the realized error on any one sample random. Having decided to fix the points once, we can no longer appeal to that probabilistic argument, and we instead want a deterministic ceiling: a bound that holds for certain on the specific point set we chose. Quasi-Monte Carlo keeps the equal-weight average of Equation 22.4 but replaces the random points by a deterministic set chosen to fill the cube as evenly as possible. The deterministic ceiling factors, beautifully, into two independent pieces: how evenly the points are spread, and how wildly the integrand moves. “Evenly spread” is made precise by discrepancy, a measure of how far the empirical distribution of the points departs from the uniform distribution; “how wildly the integrand moves” is made precise by its variation. The error is at most the product of the two: spread the points evenly (small discrepancy) and the average is accurate unless the integrand is very rough (large variation). This clean statement is the Koksma-Hlawka inequality. We first define its two ingredients, and after each definition pause to say, in plain terms, what it measures.

Definition 22.1 (Star discrepancy) For a point set \(P_N=\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_N\}\subset[0,1]^{d}\) and a corner box \([\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[0,u_i)\), the local discrepancy is the signed gap between the fraction of points in the box and its volume, \[ \Delta_N(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{N}\,\#\{n:\mathbf{u}_n\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u})\}-\prod_{i=1}^{d}u_i, \] and the star discrepancy is its worst case over all corner boxes, \[ D_N^{*}(P_N)=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^{d}}\big|\Delta_N(\mathbf{u})\big|. \tag{22.7}\] A point set is said to be of low discrepancy if \(D_N^{*}(P_N)=O\big((\log N)^{d}/N\big)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation: discrepancy is unevenness. Discrepancy asks a simple fairness question. Take any box anchored at the origin corner, say the lower-left quarter of the unit square; the fraction of your points that fall inside it should be about its area, one quarter. Local discrepancy is the gap between the actual fraction and the area for one such box, and star discrepancy is the worst gap over every anchored box at once. A point set with small star discrepancy is evenly spread at every scale, with no clumps and no gaps; a set with large star discrepancy has some region that is over- or under-populated. Independent random points clump by chance and have star discrepancy of order \(N^{-1/2}\); a low-discrepancy set drives it down to nearly \(1/N\), which is the whole source of the quasi-Monte Carlo advantage. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 22.2 (Hardy-Krause variation) For \(f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\) the total variation is \(V(f)=\sup\sum_{m}\big|f(t_{m})-f(t_{m-1})\big|\), the supremum over all partitions \(0=t_0<t_1<\dots<t_M=1\); \(f\) has bounded variation if \(V(f)<\infty\), and then for \(f\in C^{1}\), \(V(f)=\int_0^1|f'(t)|\,dt\). In \(d\) dimensions, the Vitali variation \(V^{(d)}(f)\) is the supremum over grid partitions of \(\sum|\Delta f|\), where \(\Delta f\) is the \(d\)-fold alternating difference of \(f\) over a grid box; for \(f\in C^{d}\), \(V^{(d)}(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\big|\partial_{1}\cdots\partial_{d}f\big|\,d\mathbf{u}\). The Hardy-Krause variation anchored at \(\mathbf{1}\) sums the Vitali variations of \(f\) restricted to every face of the cube, \[ V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)=\sum_{\emptyset\ne J\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}V^{(|J|)}\big(f|_{J}\big), \tag{22.8}\] where \(f|_{J}\) is the restriction of \(f\) to the face \(\{u_i=1:i\notin J\}\), a function of the coordinates indexed by \(J\). \(f\) has bounded Hardy-Krause variation if \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)<\infty\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation: variation is total roughness. This is the hardest definition in the chapter, so here is the picture. In one dimension, total variation \(V(f)\) is the total up-and-down travel of the graph: add up every rise and every fall, and for a smooth \(f\) this is \(\int_0^1|f'|\). A gently sloping function has small variation; a function that oscillates violently, or jumps, has large variation. The multivariable generalization adds one new idea, the alternating difference, which measures not just up-and-down travel but twisting. Over a rectangle \([a_1,b_1]\times[a_2,b_2]\) the two-dimensional alternating difference is the signed corner sum \[ f(b_1,b_2)-f(a_1,b_2)-f(b_1,a_2)+f(a_1,a_2), \] which is zero for any function that is additive in its two coordinates and grows only when the coordinates interact; the Vitali variation \(V^{(d)}\) totals the absolute alternating differences over all grid rectangles, capturing how much the function twists across the interior. The Hardy-Krause variation then totals the roughness over the whole cube and over every lower-dimensional face of it: for \(d=2\) it sums the ordinary variation of \(f\) along the top edge (\(J=\{1\}\)), the ordinary variation along the right edge (\(J=\{2\}\)), and the Vitali variation over the whole square (\(J=\{1,2\}\)). The faces are taken at the top corner, “anchored at \(\mathbf{1}\)”: the coordinates not in \(J\) are frozen at \(1\), a bookkeeping convention (one could equally anchor at \(\mathbf{0}\)) that ensures every face and the interior are counted once. The one thing to remember is the headline: \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\) is a single number, large when \(f\) is rough, jumpy, or strongly interacting, and small when \(f\) is smooth and mild. It is the \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}\) of Notation, and in the error bound it plays exactly the role its name suggests: a penalty for integrand roughness. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

In one dimension the face sum Equation 22.8 has the single term \(J=\{1\}\), so \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)=V(f)\) is ordinary total variation, and the corner boxes of Equation 22.7 are the intervals \([0,u)\). We prove the inequality in full in this one-dimensional case, where it is due to Koksma, and then state the multidimensional form due to Hlawka.

Theorem 22.3 (Koksma-Hlawka inequality) Let \(f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\) have bounded Hardy-Krause variation \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)<\infty\). For every point set \(P_N=\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_N\}\subset[0,1]^{d}\), \[ \bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(\mathbf{u}_n)-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\bigg| \;\le\; V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\,D_N^{*}(P_N). \tag{22.9}\] Consequently, for a low-discrepancy point set the quasi-Monte Carlo error is \(O\big(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\,(\log N)^{d}/N\big)\), nearly \(1/N\) and, for moderate \(d\), far better than the \(N^{-1/2}\) of plain Monte Carlo (Theorem 22.2).

Proof. The one-dimensional case (\(d=1\)), in full. Take \(f\in C^{1}[0,1]\); the general bounded-variation case is recovered at the end. Let \(P_N=\{u_1,\dots,u_N\}\subset(0,1)\), and let \(F_N(u)=\frac{1}{N}\#\{n:u_n\le u\}\) be the empirical distribution function of the points, a nondecreasing step function rising from \(0\) to \(1\). The local discrepancy is \(\Delta_N(u)=F_N(u)-u\), and by Equation 22.7, \(D_N^{*}=\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|\Delta_N(u)|\).

Apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to write each value as an integral of the derivative. For each point, \(f(u_n)=f(1)-\int_{u_n}^{1}f'(u)\,du=f(1)-\int_0^1\mathbf{1}\{u>u_n\}f'(u)\,du\). Averaging over \(n\) and interchanging the finite sum with the integral, \[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(u_n) =f(1)-\int_0^1\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{u>u_n\}\Big)f'(u)\,du =f(1)-\int_0^1 F_N(u)\,f'(u)\,du, \] where we used \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_n\mathbf{1}\{u>u_n\}=F_N(u)\) for all but the finitely many \(u\in P_N\), a set of Lebesgue measure zero that does not affect the integral. The exact integral admits the same treatment: substituting \(f(u)=f(1)-\int_u^1 f'(t)\,dt\) and applying Fubini’s theorem to swap the order of integration over the triangle \(\{0\le u\le t\le 1\}\), \[ \int_0^1 f(u)\,du=f(1)-\int_0^1\!\!\int_u^1 f'(t)\,dt\,du =f(1)-\int_0^1 f'(t)\Big(\int_0^t du\Big)dt=f(1)-\int_0^1 t\,f'(t)\,dt. \] Subtracting the two displays, the constants \(f(1)\) cancel and the derivative integrals combine: \[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(u_n)-\int_0^1 f(u)\,du =-\int_0^1\big(F_N(u)-u\big)f'(u)\,du=-\int_0^1\Delta_N(u)\,f'(u)\,du. \tag{22.10}\] This is the one-dimensional Hardy-Krause identity: the integration error is the integral of the local discrepancy against \(df\). Bounding the integral by the supremum of \(|\Delta_N|\) times the \(L^1\) norm of \(f'\), \[ \bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(u_n)-\int_0^1 f\bigg| \le\Big(\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|\Delta_N(u)|\Big)\int_0^1|f'(u)|\,du =D_N^{*}\,V(f), \] using \(V(f)=\int_0^1|f'|\) for \(f\in C^1\) (Definition 22.2). This is Equation 22.9 for \(d=1\).

For general \(f\) of bounded variation on \([0,1]\), replace the derivative integrals by Riemann-Stieltjes integrals against \(df\): integration by parts for the Riemann-Stieltjes integral, valid because the step function \(\Delta_N\) and the bounded-variation \(f\) may be taken to share no discontinuities (perturb the endpoints if necessary), gives \(\frac1N\sum_n f(u_n)-\int_0^1 f=\int_0^1 f\,d\Delta_N=-\int_0^1\Delta_N\,df\), and \(|\!\int_0^1\Delta_N\,df|\le\sup|\Delta_N|\int_0^1|df|=D_N^{*}V(f)\), where \(\int_0^1|df|=V(f)\) is the total variation. The bound Equation 22.9 follows in the same form.

The multidimensional case. The identity Equation 22.10 generalizes to \[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(\mathbf{u}_n)-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f =\sum_{\emptyset\ne J\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}(-1)^{|J|}\int_{[0,1]^{|J|}} \Delta_N\big(\mathbf{u}_J,\mathbf{1}\big)\,d_{\mathbf{u}_J} f|_{J}(\mathbf{u}_J), \] a sum of \(2^{d}-1\) face integrals of the local discrepancy against the mixed differentials of the face restrictions; bounding each by \(\sup|\Delta_N|=D_N^{*}\) times the corresponding Vitali variation and summing yields Equation 22.9 with \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\) from Equation 22.8. The multivariate Hardy-Krause identity and the discrepancy rate \(D_N^{*}=O((\log N)^d/N)\) for low-discrepancy sequences are classical; we cite Niederreiter (1992, Chapters 2 and 4) for the full development and do not reproduce the multidimensional integration-by-parts bookkeeping here. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters, and what the multidimensional identity says. The inequality Equation 22.9 is the theorem that licenses the entire QMC program of ML-NMR: it promises that if we spread the covariate nodes evenly (small \(D_N^{*}\)) and the response-probability integrand is not pathological (finite \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}\)), then the aggregate mean \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\) we compute is close to the truth by a margin we can name in advance, node by node, with no appeal to chance. The realized error on a specific Sobol’ net is genuinely bounded, not merely bounded on average. As for the multidimensional identity we did not prove in full: it says the integration error decomposes into \(2^{d}-1\) contributions, one for each nonempty subset of coordinates, and each contribution is the local discrepancy integrated against the roughness of \(f\) on the corresponding face of the cube. Geometrically, the whole-cube term (\(J=\{1,\dots,d\}\)) charges for how the points fail to fill the interior weighted by how much \(f\) twists across it, while the lower-dimensional terms charge for the same failure restricted to the edges and faces where some coordinates are pinned to the anchor. Summing all \(2^{d}-1\) charges and bounding each by \(D_N^{*}\) times a Vitali variation reassembles the Hardy-Krause variation and gives Equation 22.9. The reader who wants the bookkeeping will find it in Niederreiter; the reader who wants the moral has it already: error is unevenness times roughness. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Deterministic, not probabilistic. The contrast with Theorem 22.2 is sharp. Monte Carlo gives a probabilistic statement, a variance and a limiting normal law; the realized error on any one point set is random. Koksma-Hlawka gives a deterministic, worst-case ceiling on the error of a specific point set, factored cleanly into a property of the integrand, \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\), and a property of the points, \(D_N^{*}\). The factorization is what lets ML-NMR fix the points once and reason about the error of the resulting deterministic likelihood approximation (Definition 24.4 of Chapter 24 turns the \(N\)-versus-\(N/2\) comparison into a practical convergence check). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. When the integrand misbehaves. The bound Equation 22.9 is only as good as \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\) is finite, and there are two ways the ML-NMR pullback integrand \(\psi\) of Equation 22.3 can have infinite Hardy-Krause variation. The first is a discrete covariate margin. If covariate \(i\) is binary or ordinal, its inverse distribution function \(F_{j,i}^{-1}\) is a step function, so \(\psi\) jumps across the coordinate hyperplanes where the step occurs; a function with a jump transverse to an axis has infinite Vitali variation on the faces it crosses, and the smooth QMC rate Equation 22.9 need not hold. The second is an unbounded integrand, for instance a log link with \(\eta\) unbounded on the support, where \(g^{-1}=\exp\) grows without bound. For continuous margins and a bounded smooth link such as the logit, \(\psi\) is smooth and bounded and \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(\psi)\) is finite. For discrete margins, randomized QMC, in which the deterministic net is randomly scrambled (Owen, 1998), recovers near-optimal rates, but in a probabilistic rather than a deterministic Koksma-Hlawka sense. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example 22.2 (A discrete margin makes the integrand jump) Take the biomarker covariate of the running example (Example 22.1) alone: binary \(X\) with \(P(X=1)=0.3\), so its distribution function is the step \(F(x)=0.7\) on \([0,1)\) and \(F(x)=1\) on \([1,\infty)\), and its quantile function is \(F^{-1}(u)=0\) for \(u\le 0.7\) and \(F^{-1}(u)=1\) for \(u>0.7\). On an arm with linear predictor \(\eta(x)=-0.5+x\), the cube integrand \(\psi(u)=\mathrm{expit}(-0.5+F^{-1}(u))\) is flat, then jumps:

\(u\) \(F^{-1}(u)\) \(\psi(u)=\mathrm{expit}(-0.5+F^{-1}(u))\)
\(0.20\) \(0\) \(0.3775\)
\(0.50\) \(0\) \(0.3775\)
\(0.70\) \(0\) \(0.3775\)
\(0.75\) \(1\) \(0.6225\)
\(0.90\) \(1\) \(0.6225\)

The integrand cliff-jumps by \(0.6225-0.3775=0.2450\) across \(u=0.7\). In one dimension this single jump still has finite total variation (\(0.2450\)), so the Koksma-Hlawka bound survives and QMC works, though it loses its edge over evenly spaced grids near the cliff. The real damage is dimensional. When this binary covariate is correlated with a continuous covariate through the Gaussian copula (Section 22.9), the correlating step tilts the jump surface off the coordinate axis, and a jump across a tilted surface has infinite Hardy-Krause variation (Owen, 2005): the bound Equation 22.9 then reads \(|\text{error}|\le\infty\) and promises nothing. This is precisely the misbehavior flagged above, and the practical fix, randomized (scrambled) Sobol’ nets, recovers good average-case accuracy at the cost of the deterministic guarantee. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.6 Sobol’ sequences as digital nets

The Koksma-Hlawka inequality is only useful if we can exhibit point sets whose star discrepancy actually attains the low-discrepancy rate \(O((\log N)^{d}/N)\). The construction used by ML-NMR software is the Sobol’ sequence (Sobol’ (1967)). Its design principle is that the points should be perfectly equidistributed not merely overall but within every box of a family of dyadic boxes at once; the formalization of “perfect equidistribution in dyadic boxes” is the digital net.

Before the construction, the four properties a user actually relies on. A Sobol’ sequence is deterministic (the same points every time, so the likelihood is reproducible), evenly spread (low discrepancy, so the average converges fast), nested (the first \(N/2\) points are a subset of the first \(N\), so doubling the node count reuses all previous work and lets the \(N\)-versus-\(N/2\) convergence check of Section 22.11 compare like with like), and cheap to generate (each point is a handful of bitwise operations). Two further practical reasons pick Sobol’ over the other classical low-discrepancy families (Halton, Faure, Niederreiter): Joe and Kuo (2008) published well-tested direction numbers up to dimension \(21{,}200\), far beyond any ML-NMR need, and those tables ship inside the software (multinma, and the Stan and qrng libraries it builds on), so the covariate nodes are reproducible across machines. The construction below is optional reading; a user who trusts the software can take the four properties above and skip to Section 22.7.

The definition uses one piece of algebra worth naming in advance. \(\mathbb{F}_2=\{0,1\}\) is the field of two elements, where addition is exclusive-or (XOR: \(1\oplus 1=0\)) and multiplication is the ordinary product; a primitive polynomial over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) is, loosely, an irreducible polynomial whose powers cycle through all nonzero remainders, the algebraic device that guarantees the direction numbers it generates do not fall into a short repeating pattern and so keep the coordinate well spread. One caution for anyone implementing the recurrence below: the multiplications by \(2\) and by \(2^{\ell}\) are ordinary integer multiplications, and only the combining operation \(\oplus\) is over \(\mathbb{F}_2\); mixing the two up is the classic implementation bug.

Definition 22.3 (Digital \((t,m,s)\)-nets and the Sobol’ sequence) Fix a base \(b\ge 2\) and a dimension \(s\). An elementary interval (a \(b\)-adic box) in \([0,1)^{s}\) is a product \[ E=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\Big[\frac{a_i}{b^{d_i}},\frac{a_i+1}{b^{d_i}}\Big), \qquad d_i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0},\quad 0\le a_i<b^{d_i}, \] of volume \(b^{-(d_1+\cdots+d_s)}\). A \((t,m,s)\)-net in base \(b\) is a set of \(N=b^{m}\) points in \([0,1)^{s}\) such that every elementary interval of volume \(b^{t-m}\) (equivalently with \(d_1+\cdots+d_s=m-t\)) contains exactly \(b^{t}\) of the points. The parameter \(t\) measures the deviation from perfect balance: \(t=0\) is ideal, and smaller \(t\) is better.

The Sobol’ sequence is the case \(b=2\), built coordinate by coordinate from algebra over the field \(\mathbb{F}_2=\{0,1\}\). For dimension \(i\), fix a primitive polynomial over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), \[ p_i(x)=x^{e_i}+a_{i,1}x^{e_i-1}+\cdots+a_{i,e_i-1}x+1,\qquad a_{i,\ell}\in\{0,1\}, \] of degree \(e_i\), and a set of odd initial direction numbers \(v_{i,\ell}=m_{i,\ell}\,2^{-\ell}\) with \(m_{i,\ell}\) an odd integer below \(2^{\ell}\) for \(\ell=1,\dots,e_i\). Extend the direction numbers by the recurrence read off from the polynomial, \[ m_{i,\ell}=2\,a_{i,1}\,m_{i,\ell-1}\oplus 2^{2}a_{i,2}\,m_{i,\ell-2}\oplus\cdots \oplus 2^{e_i}m_{i,\ell-e_i}\oplus m_{i,\ell-e_i},\qquad \ell>e_i, \] where \(\oplus\) is bitwise exclusive-or. The \(i\)-th coordinate of the \(n\)-th Sobol’ point is the exclusive-or of the direction numbers selected by the binary digits of \(n=\sum_{\ell\ge 1}b_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}\), \[ u_{n,i}=\bigoplus_{\ell:\,b_\ell=1} v_{i,\ell}. \tag{22.11}\] With suitable direction numbers (Joe and Kuo, 2008), the Sobol’ points form a \((t,m,s)\)-net in base \(2\) for \(N=2^{m}\) and achieve the low-discrepancy rate \(D_N^{*}=O\big((\log N)^{s}/N\big)\) (Sobol’ (1967)). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The first coordinate is the van der Corput sequence. Taking the trivial direction numbers \(v_{1,\ell}=2^{-\ell}\) in Equation 22.11 gives, in dimension one, \(u_{n,1}=\sum_{\ell:b_\ell=1}2^{-\ell}\), which is exactly the radical-inverse (van der Corput) sequence in base \(2\): it reflects the binary digits of \(n\) about the radix point. For \(n=1,2,3,4,5,6,7\) this produces \[ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac18,\ \tfrac58,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac78, \] which as a set is precisely \(\{\tfrac18,\tfrac28,\tfrac38,\tfrac48,\tfrac58,\tfrac68,\tfrac78\}\), the seven interior eighths. One checks directly that this seven-point set has star discrepancy \(D_7^{*}=\tfrac18\), close to \(1/N\), whereas a typical set of seven independent uniform points has discrepancy of order \(1/\sqrt 7\approx 0.38\). The contrast is the QMC advantage in miniature, and we use exactly these points in the worked example of Section 22.11. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What scrambling is, in one paragraph. We invoke “randomized” or “scrambled” Sobol’ nets twice as the remedy for integrands of unbounded variation (discrete margins, Example 22.2) and for very high dimension. Scrambling, due to Owen (1998), applies a random but structure-preserving relabeling of the digits of every coordinate of every point: the dyadic-box balance that makes a Sobol’ net low-discrepancy is kept exactly, but the specific point positions are randomized. The payoff is twofold. First, one now has a family of statistically identical nets, so averaging the estimate over a few independent scrambles yields an honest, MC-style error bar for the integration error even though each net is deterministic once drawn. Second, for smooth integrands the scrambling improves the rate to nearly \(N^{-3/2}\), and for the rough integrands where plain QMC lost its guarantee it restores near-optimal accuracy in an average-case sense. The cost is that the deterministic, worst-case Koksma-Hlawka ceiling is traded for a probabilistic one; for the central inference of an HTA this is usually a good trade. A scrambled net is still fixed once per fit, so the smoothness in the parameters that Hamiltonian Monte Carlo needs is preserved. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.7 The probability integral transform

We now turn from the cube to the covariate space, and to the construction of the transport map \(T\) of Equation 22.3. Here is where this section is headed, stated before the theorems so the pieces have a purpose: to turn a uniform Sobol’ input into a covariate value we will invert that covariate’s marginal distribution function, and the theorem below guarantees that inverting the CDF of a uniform input reproduces exactly the intended distribution. Concretely, take age in the running example (Example 22.1), modeled as \(\mathcal{N}(45,12^{2})\). Its quantile function \(F^{-1}\) turns a uniform number into an age: \(F^{-1}(0.5)=45\) (the median), \(F^{-1}(0.975)=45+1.96\cdot 12\approx 68.5\), \(F^{-1}(0.025)\approx 21.5\). Feed in uniform inputs spread across \((0,1)\) and the ages that come out are distributed exactly as \(\mathcal{N}(45,12^{2})\). The same device handles the biomarker: with \(P(X=1)=0.3\) the quantile function returns \(0\) for inputs up to \(0.7\) and \(1\) above, so \(30\%\) of uniform inputs map to biomarker-positive, as intended. The atom of the construction is therefore the probability integral transform: the fact that any distribution can be generated from a uniform random number by applying the inverse distribution function, and conversely that a continuous distribution function maps its own variable back to uniform. We use the generalized inverse so that the statements hold for arbitrary, not only strictly increasing, distribution functions, which is what lets the biomarker’s step function participate.

Lemma 22.1 (The quantile coupling inequality) Let \(F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\) be a distribution function, that is nondecreasing and right-continuous with \(F(-\infty)=0\) and \(F(+\infty)=1\), and define its generalized inverse (the quantile function) \[ F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F(x)\ge u\},\qquad u\in(0,1). \] Then for all \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) and \(u\in(0,1)\), \[ F^{-1}(u)\le x\iff u\le F(x). \tag{22.12}\]

Proof. Write \(A_u=\{x:F(x)\ge u\}\), so \(F^{-1}(u)=\inf A_u\). Because \(F\) is nondecreasing, \(A_u\) is an upper set: if \(x\in A_u\) and \(x'\ge x\) then \(x'\in A_u\). Because \(F\) is right-continuous, \(A_u\) is closed on the left, hence contains its infimum: taking \(x_m\downarrow F^{-1}(u)\) with \(x_m\in A_u\), right-continuity gives \(F(F^{-1}(u))=\lim_m F(x_m)\ge u\), so \(F^{-1}(u)\in A_u\) and \(A_u=[F^{-1}(u),\infty)\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) If \(u\le F(x)\) then \(x\in A_u\), so \(x\ge\inf A_u=F^{-1}(u)\).

(\(\Rightarrow\)) If \(F^{-1}(u)\le x\) then, by monotonicity and \(F^{-1}(u)\in A_u\), \(F(x)\ge F(F^{-1}(u))\ge u\). \(\square\)

Theorem 22.4 (Inverse-CDF transformation) Let \(F\) be a distribution function and \(F^{-1}\) its generalized inverse.

  1. (Inverse transform.) If \(U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\), then \(F^{-1}(U)\sim F\).

  2. (Probability integral transform.) If, in addition, \(F\) is continuous and \(X\sim F\), then \(F(X)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\).

Proof. Part 1. For any \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), the coupling inequality Equation 22.12 gives the event identity \(\{F^{-1}(U)\le x\}=\{U\le F(x)\}\). Since \(U\) is uniform and \(F(x)\in[0,1]\), \[ P\big(F^{-1}(U)\le x\big)=P\big(U\le F(x)\big)=F(x). \] Thus \(F^{-1}(U)\) has distribution function \(F\), that is \(F^{-1}(U)\sim F\).

Part 2. Fix \(u\in(0,1)\) and set \(x_u=\sup\{x:F(x)\le u\}\). Continuity of \(F\) gives \(F(x_u)=u\): indeed \(F\to 0,1\) at \(\mp\infty\) and \(F\) is continuous, so by the intermediate value theorem the level \(u\) is attained, and \(x_u\) is the largest point of attainment. We claim the event identity \(\{F(X)\le u\}=\{X\le x_u\}\). If \(X\le x_u\) then \(F(X)\le F(x_u)=u\) by monotonicity. Conversely, if \(X>x_u\) then, \(x_u\) being the supremum of \(\{F\le u\}\), every \(t>x_u\) has \(F(t)>u\), so \(F(X)>u\); contrapositively \(F(X)\le u\) forces \(X\le x_u\). Therefore \[ P\big(F(X)\le u\big)=P\big(X\le x_u\big)=F(x_u)=u\qquad\text{for every }u\in(0,1), \] which is the distribution function of \(\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\). Hence \(F(X)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\). \(\square\)

Remark. Continuity is essential for the forward map. Part 1 needs no continuity: the inverse transform generates any distribution, discrete or continuous, from a uniform draw, and this is the workhorse of random-variate generation. Part 2 fails for discrete \(F\): if \(X\) is Bernoulli with success probability \(\pi\), then \(F(X)\) takes only the two values \(1-\pi\) (when \(X=0\)) and \(1\) (when \(X=1\)), so \(F(X)\) is not uniform. This asymmetry is exactly the source of the discrete-margin caveat in Theorem 22.3: a discrete covariate enters the pipeline through Part 1 only, its inverse distribution function is a step function, and the resulting jumps inflate the Hardy-Krause variation of the integrand. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.8 Sklar’s theorem

The inverse-CDF transform handles one covariate at a time. To build a joint covariate law from the marginal summaries that an AgD study reports, we need a principled way to glue marginals together with a specified dependence. Here is the concrete gap. The running example’s aggregate arm (Example 22.1) reports a mean age and a mean baseline severity, which fix each covariate’s own distribution, but says nothing about how they are paired within patients: do older patients tend to be more severe, or is there no association? Two arms with identical margins but different age-severity pairing have different covariate clouds, and, because the response probability bends nonlinearly with the linear predictor, different aggregate means. The margins alone do not determine the integral; we must supply the pairing.

One might ask why we do not simply assume the covariates are jointly normal and be done. The answer is that real covariates are not all normal: the biomarker is binary, and severity or duration may be skewed or bounded. A joint normal would force a normal margin on the biomarker, which is nonsense for a \(0/1\) variable. What we want is to keep a sensible margin for each covariate (normal age, normal severity, Bernoulli biomarker) and glue them with a chosen dependence, without the dependence disturbing the margins. Sklar’s theorem is exactly that principle: it asserts that every joint distribution factors into its marginals and a single object, the copula, that carries all of the dependence and none of the marginal information, so margin and dependence can be chosen separately and then combined.

Definition 22.4 (Copula) A function \(C:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]\) is a \(d\)-dimensional copula if it is the joint distribution function of a random vector \((U_1,\dots,U_d)\) each of whose components is uniform on \([0,1]\). Equivalently, \(C\) is grounded (\(C(\mathbf{u})=0\) if any \(u_i=0\)), has uniform one-dimensional margins (\(C(1,\dots,1,u_i,1,\dots,1)=u_i\)), and is \(d\)-increasing (the \(C\)-measure of every box in \([0,1]^d\) is nonnegative). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation: a copula is dependence with the units stripped off. Push each covariate through its own CDF and it becomes uniform on \([0,1]\) (the probability integral transform, Theorem 22.4), the same for every covariate regardless of its original scale. What survives that rescaling is only the pattern of association, and the copula \(C\) is precisely the joint distribution of those rescaled uniforms. So a copula answers “which quantiles tend to occur together”, for example “do high-age-percentile patients tend to be high-severity-percentile patients”, with the marginal scales of age and severity deliberately discarded. It is dependence in pure form, ready to be reattached to any margins we like. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Lemma 22.2 (Copulas are Lipschitz) Every \(d\)-copula \(C\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz in the \(\ell^{1}\) sense: \(|C(\mathbf{u})-C(\mathbf{v})|\le\sum_{i=1}^{d}|u_i-v_i|\) for all \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in[0,1]^{d}\). In particular \(C\) is continuous.

Proof. Let \((U_1,\dots,U_d)\) have joint distribution function \(C\) with uniform margins, so that \(C(\mathbf{u})=P(\bigcap_i\{U_i\le u_i\})\). For events \(A,B\), \(|P(A)-P(B)|\le P(A\triangle B)\), where \(A\triangle B\) is the symmetric difference. With \(A=\bigcap_i\{U_i\le u_i\}\) and \(B=\bigcap_i\{U_i\le v_i\}\), a point lies in \(A\triangle B\) only if for some coordinate \(i\) the value \(U_i\) falls between \(u_i\) and \(v_i\), so \(A\triangle B\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\{\min(u_i,v_i)<U_i\le\max(u_i,v_i)\}\). By the union bound and the uniform margins, \(P(A\triangle B)\le\sum_i P(\min(u_i,v_i)<U_i\le\max(u_i,v_i))=\sum_i|u_i-v_i|\). Hence \(|C(\mathbf{u})-C(\mathbf{v})|\le\sum_i|u_i-v_i|\), and continuity follows. \(\square\)

Theorem 22.5 (Sklar’s theorem) Let \(F\) be a joint distribution function on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) with one-dimensional margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\). Then there exists a copula \(C\) such that \[ F(x_1,\dots,x_d)=C\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}. \tag{22.13}\] If the margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\) are all continuous, then \(C\) is unique and is given on \([0,1]^{d}\) by \[ C(u_1,\dots,u_d)=F\big(F_1^{-1}(u_1),\dots,F_d^{-1}(u_d)\big). \tag{22.14}\]

Proof. We prove the existence direction in the continuous case; this is the case used by ML-NMR, where every covariate margin is taken continuous. Let \((X_1,\dots,X_d)\sim F\). Because each \(F_i\) is continuous, Theorem 22.4(2) gives \(U_i:=F_i(X_i)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\). Let \(C\) be the joint distribution function of \((U_1,\dots,U_d)\). Its one-dimensional margins are the laws of the \(U_i\), each uniform, so \(C\) is a copula by Definition 22.4.

It remains to verify the factorization Equation 22.13. From the proof of Theorem 22.4(2), for each \(i\) and each \(u_i\in(0,1)\) the event identity \(\{F_i(X_i)\le u_i\}=\{X_i\le x_{i,u_i}\}\) holds with \(x_{i,u_i}=\sup\{x:F_i(x)\le u_i\}\) and \(F_i(x_{i,u_i})=u_i\). Intersecting over the coordinates, \[ C(u_1,\dots,u_d)=P\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{d}\{F_i(X_i)\le u_i\}\Big) =P\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{d}\{X_i\le x_{i,u_i}\}\Big)=F(x_{1,u_1},\dots,x_{d,u_d}). \tag{22.15}\] Now evaluate Equation 22.15 at \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\) for a given \(\mathbf{x}\). Then \(x_{i,F_i(x_i)}=\sup\{t:F_i(t)\le F_i(x_i)\}\ge x_i\), and \(F_i\) is flat at level \(F_i(x_i)\) on the interval \([x_i,x_{i,F_i(x_i)}]\), so \(F_i(x_{i,F_i(x_i)})=F_i(x_i)\) and the coordinate-\(i\) enlargement \(\{x_i<X_i\le x_{i,F_i(x_i)}\}\) has probability \(F_i(x_{i,F_i(x_i)})-F_i(x_i)=0\). Enlarging each coordinate adds a null set, and a finite union of null sets is null, so the joint probability is unchanged: \(F(x_{1,F_1(x_1)},\dots,x_{d,F_d(x_d)})=F(x_1,\dots,x_d)\). Combined with Equation 22.15 at \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\) this gives \(C(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d))=F(\mathbf{x})\), which is Equation 22.13.

Uniqueness and the explicit form. When the margins are continuous, each \(F_i\) has range all of \([0,1]\), so as \(x_i\) ranges over \(\mathbb{R}\) the argument \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\) ranges over \((0,1)\); thus Equation 22.13 pins down \(C\) on a dense subset of \([0,1]^{d}\), and by Lemma 22.2 \(C\) is continuous, so it is determined on all of \([0,1]^{d}\), proving uniqueness. Finally, putting \(u_i\in(0,1)\) into Equation 22.15 and using \(x_{i,u_i}=F_i^{-1}(u_i)\) for continuous \(F_i\) (the largest and smallest points attaining level \(u_i\) differ only across a flat, on which \(X_i\) has zero probability) gives Equation 22.14. \(\square\)

Corollary 22.2 (Margins plus copula determine the distribution) Let \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{X}'\) be random vectors on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) with the same continuous one-dimensional margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\) and the same copula \(C\). Then \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{X}'\) have the same joint distribution. Equivalently, a continuous joint law is completely specified by naming its margins and its copula.

Proof. By Sklar’s theorem (Theorem 22.5), the joint distribution function of \(\mathbf{X}\) is \(F(\mathbf{x})=C(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d))\), and that of \(\mathbf{X}'\) is the same expression with the same \(C\) and the same \(F_i\); hence the two distribution functions are identical, and a distribution on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is determined by its distribution function. \(\square\)

Remark. Reader translation, and why we isolate it. This one-line corollary is the license for the whole ML-NMR construction: to specify a synthetic covariate distribution it is enough to choose each margin and choose a copula, and no further information is needed or possible. We pull it out as a named result because the integration pipeline (Theorem 22.6) leans on it at its crux, where it must conclude that a vector built to have the study’s margins and the study’s copula is distributed exactly as the study covariates \(f_j\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why Sklar is the right tool for aggregate data. Sklar’s theorem says exactly what an AgD study does and does not constrain. The reported marginal summaries fix the margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\), for instance a normal margin for a continuous biomarker calibrated to its reported mean and standard deviation, or a beta margin for a proportion. They say nothing about the copula \(C\). ML-NMR therefore supplies \(C\) from outside the AgD study, most often as a Gaussian copula whose correlation matrix is borrowed from the IPD study in the network or from external evidence (Section 22.9). The factorization Equation 22.13 guarantees that any choice of copula combined with the fixed margins yields a legitimate joint distribution with exactly those margins, so the marginal calibration is never disturbed by the dependence assumption. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.9 The Gaussian copula

The default copula for ML-NMR is the Gaussian copula: take a multivariate normal vector with a specified correlation matrix, and read off its dependence through the probability integral transform. It is the copula of a multivariate normal, stripped of its margins.

Definition 22.5 (Gaussian copula) Let \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\succeq 0\) be a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix with unit diagonal (a correlation matrix). The Gaussian copula with correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is \[ C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(u_1,\dots,u_d) =\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\big(\Phi^{-1}(u_1),\dots,\Phi^{-1}(u_d)\big), \tag{22.16}\] where \(\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\) is the joint distribution function of \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\) and \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the standard normal quantile function. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation: borrow the dependence of a normal, keep your own margins. A multivariate normal has a familiar dependence structure, one correlation number per pair of variables. The Gaussian copula is that dependence structure with the normal margins peeled away, ready to be glued onto whatever margins the covariates actually have. Operationally: to correlate the covariates, first pretend they are standard normals with correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\), generate correlated normal draws, then convert each back to its true margin. The correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is the one free input, and in ML-NMR it is borrowed from the IPD study in the network (or from external evidence) because an AgD study never reports it. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proposition 22.1 (The Gaussian copula is a copula, with an explicit sampler) The function Equation 22.16 is a copula. Moreover, if \(\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\) and \(U_i=\Phi(Z_i)\), then \((U_1,\dots,U_d)\) has distribution function \(C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\); and if \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}=LL^{\top}\) is a Cholesky factorization and \(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_d)\), then \(\mathbf{Z}=L\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\).

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\). The diagonal of \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is unity, so each \(Z_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), and since \(\Phi\) is continuous, Theorem 22.4(2) gives \(U_i=\Phi(Z_i)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\). The joint distribution function of \(\mathbf{U}=(U_1,\dots,U_d)\) is, using that \(\Phi\) is continuous and strictly increasing so \(\{\Phi(Z_i)\le u_i\}=\{Z_i\le\Phi^{-1}(u_i)\}\), \[ P\Big(\bigcap_{i}\{U_i\le u_i\}\Big)=P\Big(\bigcap_i\{Z_i\le\Phi^{-1}(u_i)\}\Big) =\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\big(\Phi^{-1}(u_1),\dots,\Phi^{-1}(u_d)\big)=C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\mathbf{u}). \] Thus \(C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\) is the joint distribution function of \(\mathbf{U}\), whose margins are uniform; it is a copula by Definition 22.4, and \(\mathbf{U}=\Phi(\mathbf{Z})\) samples it. For the last claim, \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is a correlation matrix hence positive semidefinite (the covariance-matrix positivity of Theorem 4.9), so it admits a Cholesky factor \(L\); the linear image \(\mathbf{Z}=L\boldsymbol{\zeta}\) of a standard normal vector is normal with mean \(\mathbf{0}\) and covariance \(L I_d L^{\top}=LL^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\). \(\square\)

Combining Proposition 22.1 with Sklar’s theorem and the inverse-CDF transform gives the construction of a joint covariate law from marginal summaries and a correlation matrix.

Remark. From a Gaussian copula to any margins. Given target margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\) and a correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\), sample \(\mathbf{X}\) with those margins and Gaussian-copula dependence by: drawing \(\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\); setting \(U_i=\Phi(Z_i)\) (uniforms with the Gaussian copula, Proposition 22.1); and setting \(X_i=F_i^{-1}(U_i)\) (the inverse transform, Theorem 22.4(1)). By Sklar’s theorem (Theorem 22.5) the resulting \(\mathbf{X}\) has exactly the margins \(F_i\) and copula \(C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\). The correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is the correlation of the latent normals, not in general of \(\mathbf{X}\) itself; the two coincide only when the margins are themselves normal, and otherwise are linked through the margins by a monotone transformation that preserves rank correlations. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Which correlation is which: a caution against pasting in a trial table. The single most common misuse of this machinery is to read a Pearson correlation off a trial’s covariate table and drop it straight into \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\). That is wrong unless the covariates are themselves normal, because \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is the correlation of the latent normals, not of the covariates. Four distinct quantities are in play, and keeping them apart is essential.

Quantity What it is Preserved by the Gaussian copula?
Latent correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) Pearson correlation of the underlying normal scores \(\mathbf{Z}\) this is the input you set
Covariate Pearson correlation linear correlation of the covariates \(\mathbf{X}\) themselves no: equals \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) only for normal margins, else attenuated
Spearman rank correlation correlation of the covariate ranks (quantiles) yes: unchanged by any monotone remargining
Tail dependence tendency to be jointly extreme in the tails always zero for a Gaussian copula

The safe practice is to specify \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) from the rank correlations of the IPD covariates (which survive the transform), or to accept a mild misspecification and use the reported Pearson values as an approximation, understanding that the realized covariate correlations after non-normal margins will be somewhat attenuated. Exercise 22.7 makes this attenuation quantitative. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Evaluating versus sampling the copula. The defining formula Equation 22.16 contains the multivariate normal distribution function \(\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\), which has no elementary closed form for \(d\ge 2\); in the bivariate case it reduces to Owen’s \(T\)-function and the tabulated bivariate normal integral (Owen (1956)), and for \(d\ge 3\) it is itself computed by numerical integration. Crucially, the ML-NMR pipeline never needs to evaluate \(\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\). It needs only to sample from the copula, and sampling proceeds through the Cholesky route of Proposition 22.1, which touches only the standard univariate \(\Phi\) and the factorization of \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\). This is why a \(d\)-variate dependence can be imposed at the cost of a single \(d\times d\) Cholesky factorization. The Gaussian copula also has zero tail dependence, so it understates the chance of two covariates being jointly extreme; for the central inference of HTA this is usually acceptable, and a \(t\)-copula or vine copula is the remedy when it is not. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What tail dependence is, and when to worry. Tail dependence asks: given that one covariate is extreme, how likely is another to be extreme too? Formally it is the limiting conditional probability \(\lim_{q\to 1}P\big(X_2>F_2^{-1}(q)\mid X_1>F_1^{-1}(q)\big)\), the chance of a joint upper extreme. The Gaussian copula sends this limit to \(0\) for every correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\ne\mathbf{1}\): no matter how strongly two covariates are correlated in the bulk, the Gaussian copula treats their simultaneous extremes as asymptotically independent. Clinically, this can understate reality when severe risk factors cluster: a patient in the worst decile of baseline severity may be far more likely than a Gaussian copula predicts to also be in the worst decile of, say, inflammatory burden, and if both are strong effect modifiers the aggregate response probability in that jointly severe corner is mis-weighted. When the decision hinges on such a corner, a \(t\)-copula (which has positive, symmetric tail dependence) or a vine copula (which lets each pair have its own tail behavior) is the principled replacement; for most central HTA estimates, where the bulk of the covariate mass drives the integral, the Gaussian copula’s convenience outweighs its blindness to the tails. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.10 The ML-NMR integration pipeline

We can now assemble the transport map \(T\) and prove the error bound for the aggregation integral. The pipeline reads as a chain of the transforms just developed: Sobol’ points into the cube, inverse normal to get independent normals, Cholesky to correlate them, normal distribution function back to uniforms with the Gaussian copula, and target inverse-CDFs to land on the covariate margins.

Before the theorem, the same pipeline as a recipe a practitioner can follow. Given the study’s marginal distributions \(F_{j,1},\dots,F_{j,d}\), its correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j\) with Cholesky factor \(L\), and a chosen number of nodes \(N\):

  1. Choose points. Take the first \(N\) Sobol’ points \(\mathbf{u}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{u}^{(N)}\) in the unit cube \([0,1]^{d}\) (evenly spread, deterministic, nested).
  2. To independent normals. Apply the inverse normal CDF coordinatewise, \(\mathbf{z}=\Phi^{-1}(\mathbf{u})\), turning each point into a vector of independent standard normal scores.
  3. Correlate. Multiply by the Cholesky factor, \(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=L\mathbf{z}\), giving normal scores with the target correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j\).
  4. Back to uniforms. Apply the normal CDF coordinatewise, \(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})\), giving uniforms carrying the Gaussian-copula dependence.
  5. To covariate values. Apply each covariate’s quantile function, \(x_i=F_{j,i}^{-1}(\tilde u_i)\), landing on a synthetic covariate profile with the right margins and the right dependence.
  6. Evaluate the model. Compute the individual response probability \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}^{(n)}))\) at each profile.
  7. Average. The mean of those \(N\) predictions is the estimate \(\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}\) of the aggregate mean.

Steps 1 to 5 build the transport map \(T\) and the fixed covariate nodes \(\mathbf{x}^{(n)}=T(\mathbf{u}^{(n)})\); they use only the study’s reported summaries and are done once, before the fit begins. Only steps 6 and 7 depend on the model parameters and are recomputed as the sampler explores the posterior. The theorem now states this precisely and bounds the error of step 7.

Theorem 22.6 (Integration over the unit hypercube) Let study \(j\) have covariate density \(f_j\) with continuous marginal distribution functions \(F_{j,1},\dots,F_{j,d}\) and Gaussian-copula correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j=L L^{\top}\). Define the transport map \(T:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathcal{X}\) coordinatewise by \[ \mathbf{z}=\Phi^{-1}(\mathbf{u}),\quad \tilde{\mathbf{z}}=L\mathbf{z},\quad \tilde u_i=\Phi(\tilde z_i),\quad x_i=F_{j,i}^{-1}(\tilde u_i),\qquad T(\mathbf{u})=(x_1,\dots,x_d), \tag{22.17}\] where \(\Phi^{-1}\) and \(\Phi\) act componentwise. Then:

  1. (Correctness.) If \(\mathbf{U}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})\), then \(\mathbf{X}=T(\mathbf{U})\sim f_j\); that is, \(T\) pushes the uniform law on the cube forward to the covariate law \(f_j\). Consequently the aggregation integral Equation 22.1 equals the cube integral of the pullback \(\psi=g^{-1}\circ\eta_{jk}\circ T\), \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\psi(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}. \tag{22.18}\]

  2. (Quasi-Monte Carlo estimate and error.) For a point set \(P_N=\{\mathbf{u}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset[0,1]^{d}\) the estimate \(\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\psi(\mathbf{u}^{(n)})\) obeys, whenever \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(\psi)<\infty\), \[ \big|\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}-\theta_{\bullet jk}\big|\le V_{\mathrm{HK}}(\psi)\,D_N^{*}(P_N). \tag{22.19}\] With \(P_N\) a Sobol’ net (Definition 22.3), \(D_N^{*}(P_N)=O((\log N)^{d}/N)\), so the integration error is \(O\big(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(\psi)(\log N)^{d}/N\big)\). For continuous margins and a bounded smooth link such as the logit, \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(\psi)<\infty\) and the rate holds.

Proof. Part 1. Track a uniform input through Equation 22.17. Let \(\mathbf{U}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})\) with independent coordinates. By Theorem 22.4(1) applied to \(F=\Phi\), each \(Z_i=\Phi^{-1}(U_i)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), and independence is preserved coordinatewise, so \(\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_d)\). The linear map gives \(\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}=L\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},LL^{\top})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j)\) (Proposition 22.1). Setting \(\tilde U_i=\Phi(\tilde Z_i)\) then yields, by the same proposition, a vector \(\tilde{\mathbf{U}}=(\tilde U_1,\dots,\tilde U_d)\) with uniform margins and joint law the Gaussian copula \(C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j}\). Finally \(X_i=F_{j,i}^{-1}(\tilde U_i)\) has, by Theorem 22.4(1), marginal distribution function \(F_{j,i}\), and by Sklar’s theorem (Theorem 22.5) the vector \(\mathbf{X}\) has copula \(C_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j}\); a distribution is determined by its margins and its copula (Corollary 22.2), so \(\mathbf{X}\sim f_j\). Therefore \(T\) pushes \(\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})\) forward to \(f_j\), and the change-of-variables identity for the pushforward measure, applied to \(\varphi=g^{-1}\circ\eta_{jk}\), gives \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(\mathbf{x})f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{U}}\big[\varphi(T(\mathbf{U}))\big] =\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\varphi(T(\mathbf{u}))\,d\mathbf{u}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\psi(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}, \] which is Equation 22.18.

Part 2. Apply the Koksma-Hlawka inequality (Theorem 22.3) to the integrand \(\psi\) on \([0,1]^{d}\) with the point set \(P_N\): the left-hand side of Equation 22.9 is exactly \(|\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}-\theta_{\bullet jk}|\) by Equation 22.18, giving Equation 22.19. The Sobol’ discrepancy rate is Definition 22.3. For continuous margins each \(F_{j,i}^{-1}\) is continuous and the composed map \(T\) is continuous; for the logit link \(g^{-1}=\mathrm{expit}\) is smooth and bounded into \((0,1)\) and \(\eta_{jk}\) is affine, so \(\psi=\mathrm{expit}\circ\eta_{jk}\circ T\) is smooth and bounded, hence of bounded Hardy-Krause variation, and Equation 22.19 attains the stated rate. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters: the capstone of the chapter. Theorem 22.6 is the whole chapter in one statement. It takes the only thing an AgD study gives us, marginal summaries and a borrowed correlation, and delivers the one thing ML-NMR needs, the aggregate response probability \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\), with a deterministic error that shrinks at nearly the \(1/N\) rate. Every earlier section is a component: the inverse-CDF transform is step 5, the Gaussian copula is steps 2 to 4, Sobol’ nets are step 1, and Koksma-Hlawka is the error bound Equation 22.19. The payoff for indirect comparison is direct: this \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\), contrasted across arms, is the relative effect the network estimates, so a trustworthy integral is what makes the odds ratio of one drug versus another, and the HTA recommendation that rests on it, trustworthy. The same map \(T\) evaluated at a target population’s margins (Section 22.10, scope remark below) is what lets ML-NMR report that odds ratio in a population matching neither trial. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The nodes are fixed; the integrand is smooth in the parameters. The covariate nodes \(\mathbf{x}^{(n)}=T(\mathbf{u}^{(n)})\) depend only on the study’s margins \(F_{j,i}\) and correlation \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_j\), not on the model parameters \(\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\mu_j,\boldsymbol{\beta}_1,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k},\gamma_k,\dots)\). They are computed once, before sampling begins. The aggregate mean is then the fixed finite average \[ \widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}(\boldsymbol{\xi}) =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g^{-1}\!\Big(\mu_j+\mathbf{x}^{(n)\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_1 +\mathbf{x}^{(n)\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}+\gamma_k\Big), \tag{22.20}\] a smooth, deterministic function of \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) with an exact gradient \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\widehat\theta_{\bullet jk}^{(N)}\) obtained by differentiating the sum term by term. This is precisely what Hamiltonian Monte Carlo requires (Definition 8.9, Carpenter et al. (2017)): a differentiable log-likelihood. Replacing the fixed Sobol’ net by fresh random draws at each step, as plain Monte Carlo would, would make Equation 22.20 a noisy function of \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) and break the gradient, which is the deeper reason QMC, not MC, is the integration rule of ML-NMR (Theorem 23.4 builds on this aggregate likelihood, and Definition 24.4 of Chapter 24 monitors the residual integration error against the Markov chain Monte Carlo error). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Scope of the pipeline. The construction integrates against any joint covariate distribution specified by continuous margins and a correlation matrix, and so computes the aggregation integral Equation 22.1 for any link, including the logit for which no closed form exists (Theorem 20.5). The same pipeline computes the target-population estimand integral (Theorem 23.1 of Chapter 23) by substituting the target population’s margins and correlation for the study’s, which is what lets ML-NMR report a contrast in a population represented by neither trial. It carries over verbatim to the unanchored extension of Chapter 26. The pipeline is reliable up to \(d\approx 20\) covariates with standard Sobol’ direction tables; beyond that, randomized (scrambled) Sobol’ nets or vine copulas are used. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Practical guidance: how to choose \(N\) and read the diagnostic. In an applied analysis the number of nodes \(N\) is not chosen from the Koksma-Hlawka constant, which is unknown, but by a doubling check. Compute the aggregate mean at \(N\) and at \(N/2\) nodes; because the Sobol’ net is nested, the \(N/2\) points are a subset of the \(N\) points and the gap \(|\widehat\theta^{(N)}-\widehat\theta^{(N/2)}|\) is a conservative proxy for the remaining integration error (if the estimate has stopped moving as points are added, it has converged). Double \(N\) until this gap falls well below the scale that matters, then stop. What scale matters? The integration error should be small relative to the posterior uncertainty in the same quantity: there is no value in pinning \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\) to five decimals when the posterior credible interval for the corresponding response rate is \(0.1\) wide, and conversely an error of \(0.005\) on the probability scale can matter if a reimbursement decision turns on a hair’s-breadth difference between two treatments near a cost-effectiveness threshold. The workable rule is: the integration error should be an order of magnitude smaller than both the posterior standard deviation of the estimand and the smallest decision-relevant difference in the target contrast. Chapter 24 formalizes this comparison, monitoring the integration error against the Markov chain Monte Carlo error so that neither source dominates (Definition 24.4). Defaults in multinma (typically \(N\) in the hundreds to low thousands per study) are calibrated to clear this bar for smooth links in moderate dimension; a discrete or skewed covariate, or a near-threshold decision, warrants raising \(N\) and rechecking the gap. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

22.11 A worked example: a logit aggregate mean by quasi-Monte Carlo

We carry the integration pipeline through a logit aggregate mean from start to finish, choosing the inputs so that every node and every running estimate can be reproduced with a calculator and a short table of normal quantiles. This is the stripped-down version of the running example (Example 22.1) promised in the introduction: read the single covariate \(X\) as a standardized baseline severity in the psoriasis arm, \(\mu\) as the arm’s treatment effect on the log-odds scale, and \(\theta_{\bullet}\) as the modeled response rate the network will contrast against a comparator. The integral is the binary-outcome aggregate probability Equation 22.1 that ML-NMR forms for an AgD arm, and we take a single covariate so that the copula step is trivial and the arithmetic is transparent; a two-covariate trace afterward exhibits the copula step explicitly.

Example 22.3 (A one-covariate logit aggregate mean) A study \(j\) has one continuous covariate with a standard normal margin, \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), and the arm in question has linear predictor \(\eta(x)=\mu+x\) on the logit scale, so the aggregate event probability is \[ \theta_{\bullet}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{expit}(\mu+x)\,\phi(x)\,dx =\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{expit}\big(\mu+\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)\,du, \] the second equality being the pullback Equation 22.18 with \(d=1\), where the pipeline Equation 22.17 reduces to \(x=F^{-1}(u)=\Phi^{-1}(u)\) (the Cholesky factor is \(L=1\) and the two normal transforms cancel). We use the seven-point Sobol’ (van der Corput) set \(\{\tfrac18,\tfrac28,\dots,\tfrac78\}\) of Section 22.6, whose nodes carry the standard normal quantiles \[ \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{k}{8}\Big):\quad -1.1503,\ -0.6745,\ -0.3186,\ 0,\ 0.3186,\ 0.6745,\ 1.1503\quad(k=1,\dots,7). \]

22.12 Notes and references

The numerical-integration program of this chapter is the computational core of multilevel network meta-regression as developed by Phillippo et al. (2020) and Phillippo (2019), whose Chapter 5 lays out the Sobol’-plus-copula pipeline of Theorem 22.6 and is implemented in the multinma package; the unanchored extension of Chapter 26 (Chandler and Ishak (2026)) reuses the same machinery. The general principle that the aggregate likelihood is an integral of the response-scale conditional mean, and therefore demands numerical integration whenever the link is nonlinear, is the subject of Chapter 20 (Theorem 20.1, Theorem 20.2, Theorem 20.5); the discrete-outcome aggregate likelihoods of Chapter 21 rest on the same integral through the success probabilities.

The classical numerical analysis is standard. Gauss-Legendre quadrature and the error formula Equation 22.5, with its derivation through Hermite interpolation and the Legendre normalization \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_k^2=2/(2k+1)\), are in Stoer and Bulirsch, Introduction to Numerical Analysis (Section 3.6); the orthogonality of Legendre polynomials and the Hermite remainder formula are the two classical facts the proof invokes. The dimension-free Monte Carlo rate Theorem 22.2 is the variance of a sample mean together with the central limit theorem of Chapter 5; the bounded-integrand variance ceiling is Popoviciu’s inequality. The Koksma-Hlawka inequality Theorem 22.3 is due to Koksma (1942/43) in one dimension and Hlawka (1961) in several; the one-dimensional identity Equation 22.10 and its proof by the fundamental theorem of calculus and Fubini are elementary and self-contained here, and the multidimensional Hardy-Krause identity, the definitions of Vitali and Hardy-Krause variation, and the low-discrepancy rate \(D_N^{*}=O((\log N)^{d}/N)\) are developed in Niederreiter, Random Number Generation and Quasi-Monte Carlo Methods (1992, Chapters 2 and 4). Sobol’ sequences and their digital-net structure are from Sobol’ (1967); the direction numbers used in modern software are those of Joe and Kuo (2008); randomized (scrambled) nets, which recover near-optimal rates for integrands of unbounded variation, are due to Owen (1998). The copula apparatus is Sklar’s theorem Theorem 22.5 (Sklar (1959)), with the textbook development in Nelsen, An Introduction to Copulas (2006); the Gaussian copula and its zero tail dependence are standard, and the bivariate normal probabilities needed to evaluate (though not to sample) the Gaussian copula are Owen’s tabulated integral (Owen (1956)). The probability integral transform Theorem 22.4 and the quantile coupling inequality Lemma 22.1 are elementary measure theory.

The forward connections are to the rest of Part V. The fixed-node likelihood Equation 22.20 is what makes the aggregate model differentiable in the parameters and hence amenable to the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo of Chapter 24 (Definition 8.9, Carpenter et al. (2017), Definition 24.1), where the integration error Equation 22.19 is monitored alongside the Markov chain Monte Carlo error (Definition 24.4, Definition 24.3). The same pipeline computes the target-population estimand integral of Chapter 23 (Theorem 23.1), which is the device by which ML-NMR transports a relative effect to a population represented by neither trial, and underwrites the identifiability and generalization results Theorem 23.2 and Theorem 23.4.

This chapter corresponds to results H.12 (Gauss-Legendre quadrature error), H.13 (Monte Carlo root-mean-square error), H.14 (Koksma-Hlawka), H.15 (Sobol’ nets), H.16 (Sklar’s theorem), H.17 (the Gaussian copula), H.18 (the inverse-CDF transform), and H.19 (the unit-hypercube integration pipeline) of the proof catalogue in proofs/H_ml_nmr.md, together with the integration-error material of proofs/I_computation.md (I.4), which Chapter 24 develops. The Coq development in ITC_Coq/theories/ formalizes the population-adjustment and causal core of the book but not the numerical-integration theory; the results of this chapter are pen-and-paper, and no machine-checked tags are claimed.
