25  General Likelihoods: Survival and Ordered Categorical Outcomes

25.1 Introduction

The four preceding chapters built multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) on a single integral. An individual in an aggregate-data (AgD) study is invisible: we never see the patient’s covariate vector \(\mathbf{x}\), only the study’s reported covariate distribution \(f_j\). To place an individual-level outcome model in the same network as that study, we average the individual model over \(f_j\). For a generalized linear model (GLM) the object that gets averaged is the conditional mean \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\), and the aggregate-level mean is \(\bar\theta_{jk}=\int g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}\) (Theorem 20.1). Chapter 20 showed this average is exact on the identity link (Theorem 20.3), is the moment generating function on the log link (Theorem 20.4), and has no closed form on the logit link (Theorem 20.5); Chapter 22 then computed the intractable cases by quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) on a Sobol’ net (Definition 22.3, Theorem 22.6) with a Gaussian copula (Definition 22.5, Theorem 22.5) to encode covariate dependence. Chapters 23 and 24 established the estimands and the Bayesian computation. All of this was carried out for outcomes that fit a one-parameter GLM: continuous, binary, and count.

Health-technology-assessment (HTA) practice is dominated by outcomes that do not fit a one-parameter GLM. The single most common primary endpoint in oncology submissions is time to an event, recorded as a possibly right-censored survival time; cardiovascular and respiratory submissions add composite time-to-event endpoints; dermatology, oncology, and psychiatry report ordered categorical severity scales (the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index response bands, the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, ordinal pain scales). A survival outcome is not summarized by a single mean: it is a whole curve, and its natural model lives on the hazard, an object indexed by time as well as by covariates. An ordered categorical outcome is a vector of cell probabilities tied together by a monotonicity constraint. Neither is a member of the exponential-dispersion family on which Chapters 20 to 23 were built. The question this chapter answers is whether ML-NMR is confined to that family, or whether the aggregation principle is deeper than the GLM that first carried it.

It is deeper. The aggregation in Theorem 20.1 never used the GLM structure of the integrand for anything except naming it a mean. What the method actually requires is one ingredient: an individual-level conditional likelihood, a function \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\boldsymbol\theta( \mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi))\) giving the probability (or density) of the outcome \(y\) for a patient with covariates \(\mathbf{x}\) under parameters \(\boldsymbol\xi\). Given that one object, the marginal likelihood of an AgD individual whose covariates are unobserved is the average of the conditional likelihood over the study’s covariate distribution, and the arm likelihood is the product of these marginal contributions over the (possibly reconstructed) individuals on the arm. This is the general-likelihood ML-NMR framework of Phillippo et al. (2025), and it makes ML-NMR the only population-adjustment method that extends verbatim to any outcome with a parametric individual model. By contrast, matching-adjusted indirect comparison (Chapter 18) balances reported moments and so needs the aggregate data in a moment form, and simulated treatment comparison (Chapter 19) needs an outcome regression on the IPD scale; neither absorbs a censored survival time or an ordinal band without bespoke surgery.

This chapter develops the framework with survival as the leading and hardest case. We define the survival function (Definition 25.1) and hazard (Definition 25.2) and prove the identity \(S=\exp(-\int h)\) that lets the hazard parameterize the entire distribution (Theorem 25.1). We then expose the feature that makes survival genuinely different from a GLM: under a proportional-hazards model the conditional log hazard ratio is a clean time-invariant linear function of the covariates (Theorem 25.2), yet the marginal hazard ratio one would estimate from a population is time-varying even when the conditional model is proportional (Theorem 25.3), a survival-scale instance of the non-collapsibility of Chapter 7 (Definition 7.7). The restricted mean survival time (Definition 25.3) repairs the collapsibility defect (Theorem 25.4) at the price of a transportability subtlety we flag for Chapter 26. We derive the right-censored individual likelihood (Definition 25.4) from first principles under independent censoring (Proposition 25.1), then build the two aggregation theorems that are the heart of the chapter: the marginal individual likelihood (Theorem 25.5) and the aggregate arm likelihood (Theorem 25.6), both resting on Theorem 20.1. The proportional-odds model (Definition 25.5) supplies the ordered-categorical case, and the general-likelihood theorem (Theorem 25.7) unifies everything: one integral, any outcome. A fully worked exponential-hazard example, in which every survival probability, every marginal hazard, the restricted mean, and the aggregate likelihood can be checked by hand, closes the development. This chapter corresponds to results J.1 to J.8 of the proof catalogue.

Throughout, \(T\ge 0\) denotes a time-to-event random variable, \(S\), \(h\), and \(H\) its survival, hazard, and cumulative-hazard functions, and \(g\) the link function (Definition 7.2) on whose scale the linear predictor \(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\) of Notation is built. A population \(\mathcal{P}\) is a distribution over the covariate space \(\mathcal{X}\) with density \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\); \(f_j\) is the covariate density of study \(j\); \(\boldsymbol\xi\) is the full parameter vector. We write \(L_i(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i)\) for an individual likelihood contribution and \(\bar L_{jk}(\boldsymbol\xi)\) for the contribution of arm \(k\) of an AgD study \(j\).

25.2 The core idea and a running example

Before any measure theory, here is the entire method in four sentences, together with one small trial to hold onto. Every abstract object in this chapter has a concrete counterpart in that trial, and every theorem below is an elaboration of one of these four steps. A reader who remembers only this section still has the essential idea.

Remark. The general-likelihood recipe, in four steps.

  1. One patient, covariates known. Write the likelihood of the outcome for a single patient as if their covariates \(\mathbf{x}\) were observed: choose a conditional individual likelihood \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\text{parameters at }\mathbf{x})\). For a binary outcome this is the Bernoulli mass; for survival it is the censored kernel \(h^{\delta}S\); for an ordinal scale it is a category probability.
  2. One patient, covariates unknown. In an aggregate-data (AgD) study the patient’s \(\mathbf{x}\) was never recorded, so average the conditional likelihood over the study’s reported covariate distribution \(f_j\). This single integral is the whole population adjustment.
  3. Whole arm. Multiply those averaged contributions across the patients (or reconstructed pseudo-patients) on the arm.
  4. Whole network. Share the treatment-effect parameters across studies, so every study estimates the same relative effects on one linear-predictor scale.

Steps 1 and 3 are ordinary likelihood bookkeeping; step 2 is the population adjustment that makes the AgD study comparable to the individual-level ones; step 4 is what makes it a network rather than a collection of separate models. Chapters 20 to 23 carried out these four steps for continuous, binary, and count outcomes. This chapter proves they go through unchanged for survival and ordered categorical outcomes, and states the theorem (Theorem 25.7) that makes “unchanged” precise. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example 25.1 (A running two-arm myeloma trial) Throughout the chapter we follow one small oncology example, a stylized version of the multiple-myeloma progression-free-survival network of Phillippo et al. (2025). A single binary baseline covariate \(X\in\{0,1\}\) marks a high-risk cytogenetic subgroup, present in half the comparator population, \(P(X=1)=q=\tfrac12\). The endpoint is time to progression \(T\), in years, modeled with a constant (exponential) conditional hazard. On the control arm \(C\), \[ h_C(s\mid x)=\lambda_x,\qquad \lambda_0=1,\quad \lambda_1=3\ \ \text{events per year}, \] so a high-risk patient has triple the event rate of a standard-risk patient. Treatment \(A\) acts by proportional hazards with a covariate-independent hazard ratio \(\psi=\tfrac12\): it halves every patient’s hazard, \(h_A(s\mid x)=\tfrac12\lambda_x\), with no effect modification. This conditional model is as clean as a generalized linear model. The lesson of the chapter is that its marginal summaries nonetheless misbehave (the marginal hazard ratio drifts with time, Theorem 25.3) while its aggregate-data likelihood requires a genuine integral (Theorem 25.7). We revisit this trial after every definition and complete the full computation in Example 25.3. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

25.3 The survival function and the hazard

A time-to-event outcome is described equivalently by its distribution function, its survival function, its density, or its hazard. The hazard is the modeling target because it is the scale on which the standard proportional-hazards and accelerated-failure-time models are linear, and on which the network’s relative effects are reported.

In plain terms, the plan of this section is: define these four equivalent descriptions of a time-to-event outcome, prove that the hazard alone determines all the others (so a model placed on the hazard is automatically a complete model of the outcome), and read off the censored likelihood the network will consume. Keep the running trial in view throughout: the control hazard there is the single number \(\lambda_x\), and we will watch every survival probability, density, and cumulative risk flow from it.

Definition 25.1 (Survival function) Let \(T\ge 0\) be a time-to-event random variable with cumulative distribution function \(F_T\). The survival function of \(T\) is \[ S(t):=P(T>t)=1-F_T(t),\qquad t\ge 0, \tag{25.1}\] and, given a covariate vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\), the conditional survival function is \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x}):=P(T>t\mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})\). The survival function is non-increasing and right-continuous, with \(\lim_{t\to-\infty}\) replaced by \(S(0^-)=1\); for a continuous \(T\) it is continuous with \(S(0)=1\), and for a proper distribution (one with \(P(T<\infty)=1\)) it satisfies \(\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)=0\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. \(S(t)\) is just the probability that a patient has not yet had the event by time \(t\). It starts at \(S(0)=1\), everyone event-free at enrollment, and decays toward \(0\). The three properties it inherits from the cumulative distribution function of Chapter 4, stated inline so that no back-reference is needed, are: \(S\) is non-increasing (the chance of surviving longer can only fall as the horizon grows), right-continuous, and runs from \(S(0)=1\) down to \(\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)=0\) for a proper distribution (everyone eventually has the event). We take these as given rather than reprove them. In the running trial \(S_C(t\mid x)=e^{-\lambda_x t}\), so the one-year survival is \(e^{-1}=0.37\) for a standard-risk control patient (\(\lambda_0=1\)) and \(e^{-3}=0.05\) for a high-risk one (\(\lambda_1=3\)): the high-risk stratum is depleted far faster. The companion object is the instantaneous event rate among survivors.

Definition 25.2 (Hazard function and cumulative hazard) Let \(T\) be a continuous time-to-event variable with density \(f\) and survival \(S\), and fix \(t\) with \(S(t)>0\). The hazard function is \[ h(t):=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow 0}\frac{P(t\le T<t+\Delta t\mid T\ge t)}{\Delta t} =\frac{f(t)}{S(t)}, \tag{25.2}\] The second equality \(h=f/S\), the working form used throughout, is derived in the proof immediately below. Conditionally on covariates \(h(t\mid\mathbf{x}):=f(t\mid\mathbf{x})/S(t\mid\mathbf{x})\). The cumulative hazard is \[ H(t):=\int_0^t h(s)\,ds,\qquad H(t\mid\mathbf{x}):=\int_0^t h(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,ds . \tag{25.3}\] The quantity \(h(t\mid\mathbf{x})\,\Delta t\) is, to first order in \(\Delta t\), the conditional probability of an event in the next short interval \([t,t+\Delta t)\) given survival to \(t\); the hazard is the local intensity of failure, a rate, not a probability, and it can exceed \(1\). A hazard of \(0.2\) per year does not mean a \(20\%\) chance over all follow-up; it means that among patients still event-free at time \(t\), a fraction of about \(0.2\,\Delta t\) have the event in the next interval of length \(\Delta t\) years, so roughly \(2\%\) in the next month. In the running trial the constant control hazard is \(\lambda_0=1\) per year for a standard-risk patient and \(\lambda_1=3\) per year for a high-risk one; the latter exceeds \(1\) and is still a perfectly valid rate. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proof. We verify the second equality in Equation 25.2, which is the working form. By the definition of conditional probability and continuity of \(T\), \[ \frac{P(t\le T<t+\Delta t\mid T\ge t)}{\Delta t} =\frac{P(t\le T<t+\Delta t)}{\Delta t\,P(T\ge t)} =\frac{F_T(t+\Delta t)-F_T(t)}{\Delta t}\cdot\frac{1}{S(t)} , \] using \(P(T\ge t)=P(T>t)=S(t)\) for continuous \(T\). As \(\Delta t\downarrow 0\) the first factor tends to \(F_T'(t)=f(t)\) at every point of differentiability of \(F_T\), so the limit is \(f(t)/S(t)\). \(\square\)

The hazard is not merely one summary among several; it determines the distribution completely. This is the identity on which every parametric and semiparametric survival model rests, and it is the reason a model specified on the hazard scale yields a coherent likelihood.

Before the formal statement, the idea in one line: the hazard accumulates over time into the cumulative hazard \(H(t)=\int_0^t h\), and survival is the exponential of the negative accumulated risk, \(S=e^{-H}\). A patient survives past \(t\) exactly to the extent that the risk piled up over \([0,t]\) is small. The theorem makes this precise and, crucially, invertible: knowing the hazard at every time is the same as knowing the whole distribution, so a model that specifies the hazard has specified everything.

Theorem 25.1 (The survival function is the exponential of minus the cumulative hazard) Let \(T\) be a continuous time-to-event variable whose survival function \(S\) is absolutely continuous with \(S(0)=1\), and let \(h=f/S\) and \(H(t)=\int_0^t h(s)\,ds\) be its hazard and cumulative hazard. Then for every \(t\ge 0\) with \(S(t)>0\), \[ S(t)=\exp\!\Big(-\!\int_0^t h(s)\,ds\Big)=e^{-H(t)}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad H(t)=-\log S(t). \tag{25.4}\] The same identity holds conditionally on covariates, \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\exp(-H(t\mid\mathbf{x}))\). Conversely, any measurable \(h\ge 0\) that is locally integrable with \(\int_0^\infty h=\infty\) defines, through Equation 25.4, a survival function of a proper continuous distribution with density \(f=h\,S\); thus the hazard and the distribution are in one-to-one correspondence.

Proof. Forward direction. Fix \(t_0\ge 0\) with \(S(t_0)>0\). Because \(S\) is non-increasing, \(S(s)\ge S(t_0)>0\) for all \(s\in[0,t_0]\), so \(S\) is bounded away from zero on \([0,t_0]\). Since \(S\) is absolutely continuous, so is \(\log S\) on \([0,t_0]\): the logarithm is continuously differentiable on the compact interval \([S(t_0),1]\), which contains the range of \(S\) over \([0,t_0]\), hence Lipschitz there, and a Lipschitz function composed with an absolutely continuous function is absolutely continuous. (Reminder for the reader meeting these words: a function is absolutely continuous when it is the integral of its own derivative, which is exactly the property that makes the fundamental theorem of calculus apply to it; it is Lipschitz on a set when the ratio of output change to input change stays bounded there. The logarithm has derivative \(1/u\), which is bounded on \([S(t_0),1]\) because \(u\) never drops below \(S(t_0)>0\), so it is Lipschitz there, and feeding an absolutely continuous input into a Lipschitz function preserves absolute continuity.) An absolutely continuous function is the integral of its almost-everywhere derivative (the fundamental theorem of calculus for the Lebesgue integral). At every point \(s\) where \(S\) is differentiable, differentiate the relation \(S=1-F_T\) term by term: since the density is the derivative of the cumulative distribution function, \(F_T'=f\) almost everywhere (Chapter 4), the constant \(1\) differentiates to \(0\), and \(S'(s)=-F_T'(s)=-f(s)\) almost everywhere. Hence \[ \frac{d}{ds}\log S(s)=\frac{S'(s)}{S(s)}=-\frac{f(s)}{S(s)}=-h(s) \qquad\text{for almost every }s\in[0,t_0]. \] Integrating from \(0\) to \(t_0\) and using \(\log S(0)=\log 1=0\), \[ \log S(t_0)=\log S(0)-\int_0^{t_0}h(s)\,ds=-H(t_0), \] and exponentiating gives \(S(t_0)=e^{-H(t_0)}\). As \(t_0\) was arbitrary subject to \(S(t_0)>0\), this is Equation 25.4. The conditional statement is identical with \(\mathbf{x}\) carried throughout, since for each fixed \(\mathbf{x}\) the function \(s\mapsto S(s\mid\mathbf{x})\) is an absolutely continuous survival function.

Converse. Let \(h\ge 0\) be locally integrable. Then \(H(t)=\int_0^t h\) is non-decreasing, absolutely continuous, with \(H(0)=0\), so \(S:=e^{-H}\) is non-increasing, absolutely continuous, \(S(0)=1\), and \(\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)=e^{-\lim H(t)}=e^{-\infty}=0\) under the stated divergence \(\int_0^\infty h=\infty\). Hence \(S\) is the survival function of a proper continuous distribution. Its density is \(f=-S'=H'\,e^{-H}=h\,e^{-H}=h\,S\) almost everywhere, and \(f/S=h\) recovers the prescribed hazard. The map \(h\mapsto S\) and its inverse \(S\mapsto h=-(\log S)'\) are mutually inverse, giving the one-to-one correspondence. \(\square\)

Remark. Why the hazard scale. Identity Equation 25.4 is what lets a model place its structure on the hazard and still deliver a likelihood. Specify \(h(t\mid\mathbf{x})\), integrate to \(H(t\mid\mathbf{x})\), exponentiate to \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x})\), and the density \(f=h\,S\) and the censored likelihood of Definition 25.4 follow. A constant hazard \(h(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{x})\) gives the exponential model \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x})=e^{-\lambda(\mathbf{x})t}\); a power-law hazard \(h(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{x})\nu t^{\nu-1}\) gives the Weibull model \(S=e^{-\lambda(\mathbf{x})t^{\nu}}\); a flexible spline on \(\log h_0\) gives the M-spline baseline used by the multinma survival models of Phillippo et al. (2025). In the running trial the control hazard is the constant \(\lambda_x\), so \(H_C(t\mid x)=\lambda_x t\) and \(S_C(t\mid x)=e^{-\lambda_x t}\): the entire time-to-progression distribution of each stratum is pinned down by the single number \(\lambda_x\), which is exactly what a one-parameter exponential model asserts. All share the relation Equation 25.4. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This pair of definitions and the identity correspond to J.1, J.2, and J.3 in the proof catalogue.

25.4 The conditional and marginal hazard ratio

The relative effect in a survival network is reported on the log-hazard scale, so the link is \(g=\log\) applied to the hazard. Why that scale? Because the Cox proportional-hazards model is the lingua franca of oncology and cardiovascular submissions: trials report hazard ratios, regulators and reimbursement agencies expect them, and the log-hazard is the scale on which those ratios are additive and, as we are about to see, the scale on which the network’s shared effect is a single transportable number. The proportional-hazards (PH) model writes the conditional hazard as a baseline function modulated by the exponential of a linear predictor.

Remark. The proportional-hazards ML-NMR model. For an individual with covariates \(\mathbf{x}\) in study \(j\) on treatment arm \(k\), the PH model is \[ h_{jk}(s\mid\mathbf{x})=h_{0j}(s)\,\exp\!\big(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\big), \qquad \eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1+\gamma_k+\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}, \tag{25.5}\] with reference-treatment constraints \(\gamma_1=0\) and \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,1}=\mathbf{0}\). Here \(h_{0j}(s)>0\) is the study-\(j\) baseline hazard, \(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1\) the prognostic effect, \(\gamma_k\) the treatment main effect, and \(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\) the treatment-by-covariate interaction (effect modification). On the log-hazard scale the model is linear, \(\log h_{jk}(s\mid\mathbf{x})=\log h_{0j}(s)+\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\); the scalar study intercept \(\mu_j\) of the GLM notation is here promoted to the baseline log-hazard function \(\log h_{0j}(s)\), the one feature that distinguishes survival from a fixed-mean GLM. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Each study keeps its own baseline; the network shares only the effects. The subscript \(j\) on \(h_{0j}(s)\) is load-bearing. Every study is allowed its own baseline hazard function, exactly as every study in a logistic or normal meta-analysis is allowed its own intercept \(\mu_j\). What the network borrows across studies is not the baseline risk, which reflects each trial’s population, accrual, and follow-up, but the relative quantities: the treatment main effects \(\gamma_k\) and the effect-modifier interactions \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\). This is the survival analogue of the fixed-study-intercept, shared-treatment-effect structure used throughout Parts III and V: a nuisance baseline per study, common effects across the network. It is why a study contributing only a control arm still informs the network (through its baseline and any prognostic terms) without pinning down a treatment effect on its own. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 25.2 (Conditional log hazard ratio under proportional hazards) Under the PH model Equation 25.5, the conditional hazard ratio of treatment \(b\) versus treatment \(a\) at covariate value \(\mathbf{x}\) is, for every time \(s\), \[ \frac{h_{b}(s\mid\mathbf{x})}{h_{a}(s\mid\mathbf{x})} =\exp\!\big(\eta_{b}(\mathbf{x})-\eta_{a}(\mathbf{x})\big), \qquad \log\frac{h_{b}(s\mid\mathbf{x})}{h_{a}(s\mid\mathbf{x})} =(\gamma_b-\gamma_a)+\mathbf{x}^\top(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,b}-\boldsymbol\beta_{2,a}). \tag{25.6}\] The conditional log hazard ratio is therefore (i) time-invariant, free of \(s\), and (ii) affine in \(\mathbf{x}\), with slope the difference of interaction vectors. It does not depend on the prognostic term \(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1\) or on the baseline hazard \(h_{0j}\).

Proof. Both arms share the same baseline hazard \(h_{0j}(s)\) and prognostic term within a study, so by Equation 25.5, \[ \frac{h_b(s\mid\mathbf{x})}{h_a(s\mid\mathbf{x})} =\frac{h_{0j}(s)\exp(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1+\gamma_b+\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{2,b})} {h_{0j}(s)\exp(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1+\gamma_a+\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{2,a})} . \] The positive factor \(h_{0j}(s)\) cancels, eliminating all dependence on \(s\), and the common prognostic factor \(\exp(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1)\) cancels. What remains is \(\exp\big((\gamma_b-\gamma_a)+\mathbf{x}^\top(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,b}-\boldsymbol\beta_{2,a})\big)\). Taking logarithms gives Equation 25.6. Both stated properties are immediate from this expression: there is no \(s\), and the dependence on \(\mathbf{x}\) is linear. \(\square\)

Why this matters. Because the conditional log hazard ratio is free of time and free of the baseline, one comparison in the network is described by a single contrast \(\gamma_b-\gamma_a\) (plus interaction terms if there is effect modification), not by a whole time-varying curve. That is precisely what lets a survival network report one transportable number per treatment comparison, the same kind of estimand the Bucher and network-meta-analysis machinery of Part III reported on the log-odds and log-rate scales. Take the property away and there is no single “the effect of \(b\) versus \(a\)” to pool.

The conditional log hazard ratio is thus exactly the kind of object the network models: a contrast of linear predictors, with effect modification carried by \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\), identifiable from any IPD arm with enough events by partial likelihood (Cox) or by full parametric likelihood. The marginal hazard ratio, the quantity a single Cox fit to a population would report, is a different and far less docile animal. We first record the representation that drives everything.

Remark. Conditional versus marginal, in plain language. A conditional hazard ratio compares two treatments for patients with the same covariate value \(\mathbf{x}\): it is what a covariate-adjusted Cox model estimates, and under proportional hazards it is the single number of Theorem 25.2. A marginal hazard ratio compares the two treatments at the level of whole populations, with covariates averaged out: it is what an unadjusted Cox model, fitted to a whole trial arm, reports. One might expect these to coincide, as they would for a straight-line model. They do not. The reason is survivor selection: at any time \(s>0\) the patients still at risk are not a random sample of the enrolled population but the ones who have not yet had the event, and treatment changes who survives. The marginal hazard ratio at time \(s\) therefore compares two differently selected risk sets, and it drifts with \(s\) even when the conditional ratio is a fixed constant. The next lemma makes “averaged over the survivors” exact; the theorem after it shows the drift cannot be avoided. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Lemma 25.1 (Survivor-weighted representation of the marginal hazard) Let \(\mathcal{P}\) have covariate density \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\), let the conditional hazard \(s\mapsto h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\) be continuous for each \(\mathbf{x}\), and suppose that for every finite horizon \(\Sigma\) the envelope \(\sup_{s\le\Sigma}h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\) is \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\)-integrable in \(\mathbf{x}\). Define the population (marginal) survival under treatment \(t\) as the covariate average \(S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f_{\mathcal{P}}}[S_t(s\mid\mathbf{X})]\) and the marginal hazard \(h_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s):=-\partial_s\log S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)\). Then \[ h_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\frac{\int h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}}{\int S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}} =\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_t(\cdot\mid s)}\!\big[h_t(s\mid\mathbf{X})\big], \tag{25.7}\] where \[ \tilde f_t(\mathbf{x}\mid s):=\frac{f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})} {S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)} \tag{25.8}\] is the survivor covariate density, the distribution of covariates among those still at risk at time \(s\) under treatment \(t\).

Proof. By Theorem 25.1, \(\partial_s S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})=-h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\, S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\). The integrability envelope and \(S_t\le 1\) give a dominating function \(\sup_{s\le\Sigma}h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\) for the integrand \(|\partial_s S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})|\) uniformly on \([0,\Sigma]\), so by the dominated-convergence justification for differentiating under the integral sign (Theorem 4.2), \[ \partial_s S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\int\partial_s S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}=-\int h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}. \] Dividing by \(S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\int S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}\) and negating yields the first equality in Equation 25.7. The integrand of the numerator is \(h_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\) times the density Equation 25.8, so the ratio is the expectation of \(h_t(s\mid\mathbf{X})\) under \(\tilde f_t(\cdot\mid s)\). \(\square\)

The representation says the marginal hazard at time \(s\) is the conditional hazard averaged not over the original population but over the survivors, whose covariate distribution \(\tilde f_t(\cdot\mid s)\) drifts with \(s\). This drift is the engine of non-collapsibility.

Remark. A numeric snapshot of survivor drift (running trial). Take the control arm of the running trial at \(s=0.5\) years. The survivor covariate density Equation 25.8 reweights each stratum by its survival, so the high-risk fraction among survivors is \[ \tilde f_C(x{=}1\mid 0.5)=\frac{\tfrac12\,e^{-\lambda_1(0.5)}}{\tfrac12\,e^{-\lambda_0(0.5)}+\tfrac12\, e^{-\lambda_1(0.5)}}=\frac{e^{-1.5}}{e^{-0.5}+e^{-1.5}}=\frac{0.223}{0.607+0.223}=0.269, \] down from \(0.5\) at enrollment, because high-risk patients have progressed faster. The marginal hazard is then the survivor-weighted average of the two conditional hazards, \[ h_C^{\mathcal{P}}(0.5)=0.731\,\lambda_0+0.269\,\lambda_1=0.731(1)+0.269(3)=1.538, \] strictly below the enrollment-population average \(\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac12(3)=2\). The population’s apparent hazard fell not because any individual’s hazard changed, each stratum keeps its constant \(\lambda_x\), but because the sickest patients have been selected out of the risk set. Repeating this in both arms and at several times gives the drift of the covariate mix, the “moving picture” of survivor selection:

time \(s\) (years) high-risk fraction, control \(\tilde f_C(1\mid s)\) high-risk fraction, treated \(\tilde f_A(1\mid s)\)
\(0\) \(0.500\) \(0.500\)
\(0.5\) \(0.269\) \(0.378\)
\(2\) \(0.018\) \(0.119\)

At every time past \(0\) the treated arm still holds relatively more high-risk patients than the control arm, because treatment \(A\) slowed their progression: the two arms are comparing increasingly different covariate mixes. This is the entire mechanism, and the next theorem shows it forces the marginal hazard ratio to move. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The theorem below turns this picture into an inequality. Its practical consequence is blunt: a single marginal hazard ratio read off a Cox fit is an artifact of the follow-up window and the censoring pattern, not a parameter that transports across trials, populations, or follow-up lengths.

Theorem 25.3 (The marginal hazard ratio is time-varying under conditional proportional hazards) Work in a population \(\mathcal{P}\) with a covariate that acts as a frailty (here meaning a multiplicative prognostic factor \(\rho(\mathbf{x})\) that scales the baseline hazard; we use the word loosely, not in the sense of an unobserved random-effect frailty distribution): there is a measurable \(\rho:\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)\), non-degenerate under \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\), and a baseline \(h_{a0}(s)>0\) such that the control hazard is \(h_a(s\mid\mathbf{x})=h_{a0}(s)\,\rho(\mathbf{x})\), and let treatment \(b\) satisfy conditional proportional hazards with covariate-independent ratio \(h_b(s\mid\mathbf{x})/h_a(s\mid\mathbf{x})=\psi\), a constant with \(0<\psi<1\) (a beneficial treatment, no effect modification on the log-hazard scale). Then the marginal hazard ratio \[ \mathrm{mHR}(s):=\frac{h_b^{\mathcal{P}}(s)}{h_a^{\mathcal{P}}(s)} =\psi\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_b(\cdot\mid s)}[\rho(\mathbf{X})]} {\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_a(\cdot\mid s)}[\rho(\mathbf{X})]}}_{=:R(s)\ \ge\ 1} \tag{25.9}\] satisfies \(\mathrm{mHR}(0)=\psi\) and \(\mathrm{mHR}(s)>\psi\) for every \(s>0\). Consequently \(\mathrm{mHR}(\cdot)\) is not constant: although the conditional hazard ratio is the single number \(\psi\), the marginal hazard ratio varies with time and is attenuated toward the null value \(1\). This is non-collapsibility of the hazard ratio (Definition 7.7) on the survival scale.

Proof. The factorization. Since \(h_b=\psi\,h_a\) pointwise, Lemma 25.1 gives \(h_b^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_b(\cdot\mid s)}[h_b(s\mid\mathbf{X})]= \psi\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_b(\cdot\mid s)}[h_a(s\mid\mathbf{X})]\) and \(h_a^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_a(\cdot\mid s)}[h_a(s\mid\mathbf{X})]\). Under the frailty structure \(h_a(s\mid\mathbf{x})=h_{a0}(s)\rho(\mathbf{x})\), the common factor \(h_{a0}(s)\) pulls out of both expectations and cancels in the ratio, leaving Equation 25.9 with \(R(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_b(\cdot\mid s)}[\rho]/\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_a(\cdot\mid s)}[\rho]\).

Value at \(s=0\). From Equation 25.8, \(\tilde f_t(\mathbf{x}\mid 0)=f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x}) S_t(0\mid\mathbf{x})/S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(0)=f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\) for both \(t\in\{a,b\}\), because \(S_t(0\mid\mathbf{x})=1\). Hence the two survivor distributions coincide with \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\) at \(s=0\), \(R(0)=1\), and \(\mathrm{mHR}(0)=\psi\).

Strict inequality for \(s>0\). By Theorem 25.1 and the frailty form, \(S_a(s\mid\mathbf{x})=\exp(-\rho(\mathbf{x})\,A(s))\) with \(A(s):=\int_0^s h_{a0}(u)\,du>0\), and \(S_b(s\mid\mathbf{x})=S_a(s\mid\mathbf{x})^{\psi}=\exp(-\psi\rho(\mathbf{x})A(s))\). Therefore the two survivor densities Equation 25.8 are exponential tilts of one another in \(\rho\): \[ \tilde f_a(\mathbf{x}\mid s)\propto f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{-A(s)\rho(\mathbf{x})}, \qquad \tilde f_b(\mathbf{x}\mid s)\propto f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{-\psi A(s)\rho(\mathbf{x})} , \] so \(\tilde f_b\propto\tilde f_a\cdot e^{(1-\psi)A(s)\rho(\mathbf{x})}\) with \((1-\psi)A(s)>0\). (An exponential tilt of a distribution is a reweighting of its density by a factor \(e^{c\,\rho(\mathbf{x})}\) followed by renormalizing; the parameter \(c\) is the natural parameter, and the family so generated is a one-parameter exponential family with \(\rho\) as its sufficient statistic. So the treated survivor density is an exponential tilt of the control survivor density.) Introduce the one-parameter family of tilts \(d\mu_c(\mathbf{x})\propto f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\, e^{-A(s)\rho(\mathbf{x})}\,e^{c\,\rho(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}\) for \(c\in[0,(1-\psi)A(s)]\), so that \(\mu_0=\tilde f_a(\cdot\mid s)\) and \(\mu_{(1-\psi)A(s)}=\tilde f_b(\cdot\mid s)\). Let \(m(c):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_c}[\rho(\mathbf{X})]\). Differentiating the exponential family in its natural parameter \(c\) gives \[ m'(c)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_c}[\rho^2]-\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_c}[\rho]\big)^2 =\operatorname{Var}_{\mu_c}(\rho(\mathbf{X}))\ \ge\ 0, \] the last equality being the standard exponential-family identity that the derivative of the mean of the sufficient statistic with respect to the natural parameter equals its variance (the same score-variance calculation met for the score function in Chapter 5). The variance is strictly positive because \(\rho\) is non-degenerate under \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\) and the tilt keeps the support. Hence \(m\) is strictly increasing, so \(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_b(\cdot\mid s)}[\rho]=m((1-\psi)A(s))>m(0)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde f_a(\cdot\mid s)}[\rho]\), that is \(R(s)>1\) and \(\mathrm{mHR}(s)=\psi R(s)>\psi\). Because \(\psi<1\), moving above \(\psi\) moves the marginal hazard ratio strictly closer to the null \(1\). Since \(\mathrm{mHR}(0)=\psi\neq\mathrm{mHR}(s)\) for any \(s>0\), \(\mathrm{mHR}(\cdot)\) is not constant. \(\square\)

Remark. The mechanism, in words. Treatment \(b\) lowers everyone’s hazard by the factor \(\psi\), so high-frailty patients survive longer under \(b\) than under \(a\). At any later time the treated risk set therefore still contains relatively many high-frailty (high-\(\rho\)) patients, while the control risk set has been depleted of them; the surviving treated population has a higher average baseline hazard than the surviving control population. The marginal hazard ratio compares these two differently-selected risk sets and so understates the true protective effect: it is pulled toward \(1\), by more as time passes and selection accumulates. The conditional model never changed; the marginalization moved. This is precisely the survival-scale form of the odds-ratio non-collapsibility of Definition 7.7, and it is why a single reported hazard ratio is an artifact of the follow-up window and the censoring distribution, not a transportable parameter. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

25.5 Restricted mean survival time, a collapsible alternative

The non-collapsibility and time-variation of the marginal hazard ratio motivate a marginal summary that behaves. The restricted mean survival time is the area under the survival curve up to a fixed horizon; it is collapsible over covariates by a one-line Fubini argument.

Definition 25.3 (Restricted mean survival time) For a horizon \(\tau>0\), the restricted mean survival time (RMST) of treatment \(t\) in population \(\mathcal{P}\) is \[ \operatorname{RMST}_t^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau):=\int_0^{\tau}S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)\,ds, \tag{25.10}\] the expected event-free time over \([0,\tau]\). The conditional RMST is \(\operatorname{RMST}_t(\tau\mid\mathbf{x}):=\int_0^{\tau}S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,ds\), and the RMST contrast of \(b\) versus \(a\) is \(\Delta\!\operatorname{RMST}_{ba}^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau):=\operatorname{RMST}_b^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)- \operatorname{RMST}_a^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 25.4 (RMST and the RMST contrast are collapsible over covariates) With \(S_t^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f_{\mathcal{P}}}[S_t(s\mid\mathbf{X})]\), \[ \operatorname{RMST}_t^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau) =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f_{\mathcal{P}}}\!\big[\operatorname{RMST}_t(\tau\mid\mathbf{X})\big], \qquad \Delta\!\operatorname{RMST}_{ba}^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau) =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f_{\mathcal{P}}}\!\big[\operatorname{RMST}_b(\tau\mid\mathbf{X}) -\operatorname{RMST}_a(\tau\mid\mathbf{X})\big]. \tag{25.11}\] The marginal RMST is the population average of the conditional RMSTs: marginal equals average conditional, which is collapsibility.

Proof. The integrand \(S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\ge 0\) is jointly measurable in \((s,\mathbf{x})\) and nonnegative, so by Tonelli’s theorem (Theorem 4.8) the order of integration over \(s\in[0,\tau]\) (Lebesgue measure) and over \(\mathbf{x}\) (the measure \(f_{\mathcal{P}}\)) may be exchanged: \[ \operatorname{RMST}_t^{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)=\int_0^{\tau}\!\!\Big(\int S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\, f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\Big)ds =\int\!\Big(\int_0^{\tau}S_t(s\mid\mathbf{x})\,ds\Big)f_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\big[\operatorname{RMST}_t(\tau\mid\mathbf{X})\big]. \] The contrast statement follows by linearity of expectation applied to the difference of the two arms. \(\square\)

Remark. Collapsible but not automatically transportable. Collapsibility (marginal equals average conditional) is a statement within one population \(\mathcal{P}\); transportability is a statement across populations. The RMST contrast is collapsible by Theorem 25.4, yet if the conditional model is fitted on the log-hazard scale (PH, \(g=\log\)) while the reported effect is the RMST (a time-scale quantity), the composition \(\operatorname{RMST}_t(\tau\mid\mathbf{x})=\int_0^{\tau} \exp\!\big(-\!\int_0^s e^{\eta_t(\mathbf{x})}h_{0}(u)\,du\big)ds\) is a nonlinear functional of \(\eta_t(\mathbf{x})\). The shared-effect-modifier assumption (Definition 17.6) holds on the log-hazard scale, not on the RMST scale, so the RMST contrast computed in the comparator population does not generally equal the RMST contrast in a different target population; transporting it requires the target covariate distribution, exactly as in the scale-misalignment analysis taken up for the unanchored case in Chapter 26 (Theorem 26.4). The clean estimand to transport remains the conditional log-hazard model; RMST is a collapsible read-out in a stated population. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Collapsible does not mean transportable: a two-population snapshot. It is tempting to hear “collapsible” and conclude “safe to compare across populations.” It is not. Collapsibility says the marginal RMST contrast in a population equals the average of the conditional contrasts in that same population; it says nothing about a different population with a different covariate mix. In the running trial the conditional RMST contrast at horizon \(\tau=2\) is \(0.399\) years for a standard-risk patient (\(x=0\)) and \(0.301\) years for a high-risk patient (\(x=1\)) (both computed in Example 25.3). A comparator population with high-risk prevalence \(q=\tfrac12\) therefore has marginal contrast \(\tfrac12(0.399)+\tfrac12(0.301)=0.350\) years, whereas a target population with \(q^{*}=\tfrac14\) has \(\tfrac34(0.399)+\tfrac14(0.301)=0.375\) years. Same conditional model, same treatment, different marginal RMST contrast, purely because the covariate mix differs. The object that is shared across the two populations is the conditional log-hazard model; the RMST contrast must be recomputed in each target population, which is why transporting it needs the target covariate distribution. Exercise Exercise 25.8 works the general formula. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The practical decision rule for a survival submission follows.

25.6 The right-censored individual likelihood

Survival data are incomplete: for many patients we observe not the event time but a censoring time, the last moment at which they were known to be event-free. The likelihood must use the density for observed events and the survival probability for censored observations. We define the contribution and then derive it from the joint model of event and censoring under the standard independence assumption.

Definition 25.4 (Right-censored individual likelihood) Let an individual with covariates \(\mathbf{x}\) have a (possibly right-censored) observation \((t,\delta)\), where \(t\ge 0\) is the observed time and \(\delta\in\{0,1\}\) is the event indicator: \(\delta=1\) if \(t\) is an event time and \(\delta=0\) if \(t\) is a right-censoring time. Under a model with conditional hazard \(h(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\) and survival \(S(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol\xi)\), the right-censored individual likelihood contribution is \[ L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;t,\delta,\mathbf{x}) =h(t\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)^{\delta}\;S(t\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi) =\begin{cases} f(t\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)=h(t\mid\mathbf{x})S(t\mid\mathbf{x}), & \delta=1,\\[2pt] S(t\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi), & \delta=0. \end{cases} \tag{25.12}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation, and a timeline. Picture one patient’s follow-up as a horizontal line beginning at enrollment. Two things can end the line. If the event (progression) is observed at time \(t\), we know exactly when it happened, and the contribution is the density \(f(t\mid\mathbf{x})=h(t\mid\mathbf{x}) S(t\mid\mathbf{x})\): the chance of surviving event-free up to \(t\), times the instantaneous rate of failing right at \(t\). If instead the patient is last seen event-free at time \(t\), because the trial reached its data cutoff or the patient left for reasons unrelated to prognosis, the line is right-censored: we know only that \(T>t\), and the contribution is the survival probability \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x})\). An event pins down a point on the line; a censoring pins down only a lower bound. The single formula \(h^{\delta}S\) encodes both by switching the hazard factor on (\(\delta=1\)) or off (\(\delta=0\)). In the running trial we will use exactly two such patients on the comparator control arm: an event at \(t_1=0.5\) years and a censoring at \(t_2=2\) years (Example 25.3, Step 4).

Proposition 25.1 (Derivation of the censored likelihood under independent censoring) Let the event time \(T\) have conditional density \(f(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\), survival \(S(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\), and hazard \(h=f/S\), and let the censoring time \(C\) be conditionally independent of \(T\) given \(\mathbf{x}\), with survival \(G(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\) and density \(g(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\) that do not depend on the event-model parameter \(\boldsymbol\xi\) (non-informative, independent censoring). We observe \(\tilde T=\min(T,C)\) and \(\delta=\mathbf{1}\{T\le C\}\). Then the joint density of the observed pair \((\tilde t,\delta)\) factorizes as \[ p(\tilde t,\delta\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi) =\big[f(\tilde t\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\,G(\tilde t\mid\mathbf{x})\big]^{\delta} \big[S(\tilde t\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\,g(\tilde t\mid\mathbf{x})\big]^{1-\delta}, \tag{25.13}\] and the factor depending on \(\boldsymbol\xi\) is proportional to Equation 25.12. Hence the maximum-likelihood and Bayesian inferences for \(\boldsymbol\xi\) may use \(h^{\delta}S\) as the individual contribution, discarding the censoring factors \(G^{\delta}g^{1-\delta}\) as constants in \(\boldsymbol\xi\).

Proof. Condition on \(\mathbf{x}\) throughout and suppress it; independence of \(T\) and \(C\) makes their joint density \(f(t)g(c)\). Consider the two observable events.

An event (\(\delta=1\)) observed at \(\tilde t=t\). In words, we see an event at \(t\) exactly when the true event time equals \(t\) and the censoring time has not yet arrived, that is \(C\ge t\); those are the censoring values compatible with observing the event. This is the set \(\{T=t,\ C\ge t\}\), and its density in \(t\) is obtained by integrating the joint density over all such censoring values \(c\ge t\) that leave \(T\) uncensored: \[ \int_{t}^{\infty}f(t)\,g(c)\,dc=f(t)\int_t^{\infty}g(c)\,dc=f(t)\,G(t), \] using \(\int_t^\infty g(c)\,dc=P(C>t)=G(t)\).

A censoring (\(\delta=0\)) observed at \(\tilde t=c\). Symmetrically, we see a censoring at \(c\) exactly when the censoring time equals \(c\) and the true event has not yet happened, that is \(T>c\). This is the set \(\{C=c,\ T>c\}\), with density in \(c\) \[ \int_{c}^{\infty}f(t)\,g(c)\,dt=g(c)\int_c^{\infty}f(t)\,dt=g(c)\,S(c). \] Writing both cases with the indicator \(\delta\) gives Equation 25.13. Substituting \(f=h\,S\) in the \(\delta=1\) branch, \[ p(\tilde t,\delta\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi) =\big[h(\tilde t)S(\tilde t)\big]^{\delta}S(\tilde t)^{1-\delta}\cdot G(\tilde t)^{\delta}g(\tilde t)^{1-\delta} =\underbrace{h(\tilde t)^{\delta}S(\tilde t)}_{\text{depends on }\boldsymbol\xi}\cdot \underbrace{G(\tilde t)^{\delta}g(\tilde t)^{1-\delta}}_{\text{free of }\boldsymbol\xi}, \] where the exponents of \(S\) combine as \(\delta+(1-\delta)=1\). The second factor does not involve \(\boldsymbol\xi\), so as a function of \(\boldsymbol\xi\) the contribution is proportional to \(h(\tilde t\mid\mathbf{x})^{\delta}S(\tilde t\mid\mathbf{x})\), which is Equation 25.12. \(\square\)

Remark. Scope of the assumption. The factorization needs only that censoring is independent of the event time given covariates and that its mechanism carries no information about \(\boldsymbol\xi\). In health-research terms, three common patterns clarify the boundary. Administrative censoring at a common data cutoff, when the trial database locks on a fixed calendar date, satisfies the assumption, because the cutoff date is unrelated to any individual patient’s prognosis. Loss to follow-up satisfies it only when the reason a patient drops out is unrelated to their event risk after conditioning on covariates, for example a patient who moves house. Informative dropout, where patients leave the study precisely because they are deteriorating, violates it: then a censoring carries hidden bad news about \(T\), the censoring survival \(G\) is no longer free of \(\boldsymbol\xi\), and the clean split fails. Interval censoring and left truncation likewise break the split and require the corresponding modified contributions; see the survival-analysis texts cited in Section 25.11. For the network the important point is that Equation 25.12 is a conditional individual likelihood of exactly the form the aggregation machinery of the next section consumes. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This corresponds to J.4 in the proof catalogue.

25.7 Aggregation for general likelihoods

We now perform the step that makes survival (or any other) outcomes part of an ML-NMR network. In an AgD study the individual covariate vectors are unobserved, so the conditional likelihood Equation 25.12 cannot be evaluated at a known \(\mathbf{x}\). Instead we average it over the study’s covariate distribution. This is the general-likelihood analogue of the mean-aggregation theorem Theorem 20.1 of Chapter 20, and it is what Phillippo et al. (2025) call the integrated likelihood.

One question must be settled before the theorems: if the study is aggregate, where do the individual observations \(y_{1jk},\dots,y_{n_{jk}jk}\) in the theorems below come from? Two situations arise. When the outcome has a sufficient summary, a binary study reporting an event count, no individual rows are needed and the arm likelihood collapses to a single aggregate contribution (Chapters 20 and 21). Survival has no such single summary: the reported object is a Kaplan-Meier curve. There the individual rows are pseudo-IPD, patient-level times and event indicators reconstructed from the published curve, sketched in the bridge after Theorem 25.6 and developed in Chapter 26. The point to hold throughout this section is a split that beginners often miss: even after the event times are reconstructed, the covariates are still unobserved. We know when each pseudo-patient progressed or was censored, but not their covariate vector \(\mathbf{x}\); that is exactly why the covariate integral over \(f_j\) remains, applied to every pseudo-patient.

Theorem 25.5 (Marginal individual likelihood) Consider an individual \(i\) in an AgD study \(j\), with outcome \(y_i\) observed but covariate vector \(\mathbf{X}_i\) unobserved and distributed according to the study covariate density \(f_j\). Let \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i,\mathbf{x})\) be the conditional individual likelihood, that is the conditional density or probability mass \(p(y_i\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\xi)\) of the outcome given covariates (for survival, Equation 25.12; for binary, the Bernoulli mass; and so on). Then the marginal individual likelihood, the likelihood contribution that uses only the observed \(y_i\), is \[ L_i(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i) =\int_{\mathcal{X}}L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i,\mathbf{x})\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f_j}\!\big[\,p(y_i\mid\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol\xi)\,\big]. \tag{25.14}\] For discrete covariates the integral is a sum over \(\mathcal{X}\).

Intuition. The aggregate study never recorded \(\mathbf{x}\), so we cannot condition on it. The only honest likelihood for the observed outcome \(y_i\) is the one that integrates \(\mathbf{x}\) out, weighting each possible covariate value by how common it is in that study, namely \(f_j\). That weighted average is Equation 25.14. The proof is nothing more than the law of total probability made explicit, but the modeling content is real: it is step 2 of the recipe of Section 25.2, and it is what makes an AgD arm comparable to an IPD arm in the same network.

Proof. Under parameter \(\boldsymbol\xi\) the joint law of \((Y_i,\mathbf{X}_i)\) in study \(j\) has density \(p(y_i,\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\xi)=p(y_i\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\xi)\,f_j(\mathbf{x})= L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i,\mathbf{x})\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\), the conditional outcome model times the covariate density. The likelihood of the observed datum is the marginal density of \(Y_i\), obtained by integrating out the unobserved \(\mathbf{X}_i\) (the law of total probability, equivalently marginalization of a joint density, Chapter 4): \[ L_i(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i)=p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}p(y_i,\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\, d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i,\mathbf{x})\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}, \] which is Equation 25.14. The integral is finite because \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}\ge 0\) and, for a probability-mass outcome, \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}\le 1\), so the integral is at most \(\int f_j=1\); for a density outcome it is the marginal density of \(Y_i\), finite for almost every \(y_i\). \(\square\)

Remark. Relation to mean aggregation and to numerical integration. Theorem Theorem 20.1 aggregates the conditional mean \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\); theorem Theorem 25.5 aggregates the conditional likelihood \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}\). The two agree exactly when the likelihood kernel is affine in the mean, as for the Bernoulli mass \(p^{y}(1-p)^{1-y}\), which equals \(p\) at \(y=1\) and \(1-p\) at \(y=0\): then \(\int p(\mathbf{x})^{y}(1-p(\mathbf{x}))^{1-y}f_j\,d\mathbf{x}=\bar p^{\,y}(1-\bar p)^{1-y}\) with \(\bar p=\int p\,f_j\), so integrating the likelihood reproduces the aggregate-mean model. Contrast the count case as an explicit non-example: the Poisson mass \(\theta^{y}e^{-\theta}/y!\) is not affine in its mean \(\theta\) (it carries both a \(\theta^{y}\) factor and an \(e^{-\theta}\) factor), so in general \(\int \theta(\mathbf{x})^{y}e^{-\theta(\mathbf{x})}/y!\,f_j\,d\mathbf{x}\ne\bar\theta^{y}e^{-\bar\theta}/y!\); averaging the likelihood is genuinely different from evaluating it at the average mean. The survival kernel \(h^{\delta}S\) and the proportional-odds mass \(p_k\) fail affinity in the same way. For kernels that are not affine in a single mean, survival (\(h^{\delta}S\)) and the proportional-odds mass of Definition 25.5 among them, the integral Equation 25.14 genuinely exceeds mean aggregation, and like the logit case (Theorem 20.5) it has no closed form. It is then evaluated by the Sobol’ QMC rule of Chapter 22 (Definition 22.3, Theorem 22.6): with integration nodes \(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}\) drawn from \(f_j\) through the inverse-CDF map (Theorem 22.4) and a Gaussian copula (Definition 22.5, Theorem 22.5) for covariate dependence, \[ L_i(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{r=1}^{N} L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}\!\big(\boldsymbol\xi;y_i,\mathbf{x}^{(r)}\big), \] the same average that drives every AgD contribution in the network. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The arm likelihood is the product of these marginal contributions. We make the conditional-independence structure explicit so that the product is justified rather than assumed.

Theorem 25.6 (Aggregate arm likelihood) Let arm \(k\) of AgD study \(j\) contribute \(n_{jk}\) individual observations \(y_{1jk},\dots,y_{n_{jk}jk}\) whose unobserved covariate vectors are independent draws from \(f_j\), and let the outcomes be conditionally independent across individuals given their covariates and \(\boldsymbol\xi\). Then the marginalized outcomes \(Y_{1jk},\dots,Y_{n_{jk}jk}\) are independent, each with the marginal individual likelihood Equation 25.14, and the aggregate arm likelihood factorizes as \[ \bar L_{jk}(\boldsymbol\xi;\mathbf{y}_{jk}) =\prod_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}L_i(\boldsymbol\xi;y_{ijk}) =\prod_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;y_{ijk},\mathbf{x})\, f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}. \tag{25.15}\]

Proof. Write \(\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1jk},\dots,Y_{n_{jk}jk})\) and \(\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots, \mathbf{X}_{n_{jk}})\). Conditional independence of outcomes given covariates and \(\boldsymbol\xi\) gives the joint conditional density \(p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol\xi)=\prod_{i}p(y_{ijk}\mid\mathbf{X}_i, \boldsymbol\xi)\), and independence of the covariate draws gives the joint covariate density \(\prod_i f_j(\mathbf{x}_i)\). Marginalizing the covariates from the joint law and using Tonelli’s theorem (Theorem 4.8) on the nonnegative integrand to factor the multiple integral into a product of single integrals, \[ \bar L_{jk}(\boldsymbol\xi) =\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n_{jk}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}p(y_{ijk}\mid\mathbf{x}_i,\boldsymbol\xi)\, f_j(\mathbf{x}_i)\,d\mathbf{x}_1\cdots d\mathbf{x}_{n_{jk}} =\prod_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}p(y_{ijk}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\xi)\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x} =\prod_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}L_i(\boldsymbol\xi;y_{ijk}), \] which is Equation 25.15. The factorization shows the \(Y_{ijk}\) are independent with the stated marginals. \(\square\)

Remark. Sufficient summaries and pseudo-IPD. When the conditional kernel is affine in a mean and the AgD report a sufficient summary, Equation 25.15 collapses to a low-dimensional aggregate model: for Bernoulli outcomes summarized by an event count \(r_{jk}=\sum_i y_{ijk}\), the product is \(\bar p_{jk}^{\,r_{jk}}(1-\bar p_{jk})^{n_{jk}-r_{jk}}\), the binomial likelihood with aggregate success probability \(\bar p_{jk}=\int\operatorname{expit}(\eta_{jk})f_j\), recovering the binary ML-NMR of Chapters 20 and 21 and, with no covariates to integrate, the standard network meta-analysis likelihood (Theorem 23.4). For survival the reported summary is a Kaplan-Meier curve, which is not a single sufficient statistic; the individual observations entering Equation 25.15 are then pseudo-IPD \((t_{ijk},\delta_{ijk})\) reconstructed from the published curve and the numbers at risk, the reconstruction whose validity is established for the unanchored setting in Chapter 26 (Theorem 26.5). In outline the reconstruction, the algorithm of Guyot and colleagues, digitizes the published Kaplan-Meier curve into a fine grid of (time, survival-probability) points, then uses the reported numbers at risk in each interval to solve, step by step, for how many events and how many censorings must have occurred to produce the observed drops in the curve; its output is a table of pseudo-observations \((t_{ijk},\delta_{ijk})\) whose own Kaplan-Meier estimate reproduces the published one. What is recovered is the pair (time, event indicator) for each pseudo-patient; what remains unobserved is the covariate vector, which is again why each pseudo-patient still contributes the integrated likelihood Equation 25.14 over \(f_j\) rather than a conditional likelihood at a known \(\mathbf{x}\). This digitize-then-reconstruct step is the one part of a real submission that most distinguishes practice from the clean theory, so we flag it here rather than leaving it entirely to Chapter 26. Once reconstructed, each pseudo-individual contributes the marginal censored likelihood Equation 25.12 integrated over \(f_j\), exactly as in Equation 25.15. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

These two theorems correspond to J.5 (Theorem 25.5) and J.6 (Theorem 25.6) in the proof catalogue.

25.8 Ordered categorical outcomes

The second outcome type that escapes the one-parameter GLM is the ordered categorical scale, and it is not a niche case. HTA submissions routinely report ordered severity bands: the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status \(0,1,2,3,4\), psoriasis response bands (PASI 50, 75, 90), and ordinal pain or function scales. The categories have a meaningful order, better to worse, but no numeric spacing, so a mean is not the natural summary; the natural object is the whole vector of category probabilities, tied together by the order. The standard model is the proportional-odds (cumulative-logit) model, which places a single linear predictor against an increasing ladder of cutpoints. We develop it, give it the same concrete two-point treatment the survival case received, and show it drops into the aggregation machinery of Section 25.7 without a single change.

Definition 25.5 (Proportional-odds model) Let \(Y\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}\) be an ordered categorical outcome (the categories carry a meaningful order, for example increasing disease severity). With cutpoints \(\alpha_1\le\alpha_2\le\cdots\le\alpha_{K-1}\) and a linear predictor \(\eta(\mathbf{x})\), the proportional-odds model specifies the cumulative probabilities by \[ \operatorname{logit}P(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})=\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x}), \qquad k=1,\dots,K-1, \tag{25.16}\] equivalently \(P(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x}))\). With the conventions \(\alpha_0=-\infty\) and \(\alpha_K=+\infty\) (so \(\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_0-\eta)=0\) and \(\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_K-\eta)=1\)), the category probabilities are the consecutive differences \[ p_k(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi):=P(Y=k\mid\mathbf{x}) =\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x}))-\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_{k-1}-\eta(\mathbf{x})), \qquad k=1,\dots,K. \tag{25.17}\] For ML-NMR (and the unanchored ML-UMR of Chapter 26) the linear predictor is the network linear predictor \(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1+\gamma_k+\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\), with study-specific cutpoints \(\alpha_{k}^{(j)}\) permitted. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. Imagine a hidden continuous “severity” axis. The cutpoints \(\alpha_1\le\cdots\le\alpha_{K-1}\) are fixed thresholds that slice this axis into the \(K\) ordered categories, and the linear predictor \(\eta(\mathbf{x})\) shifts a patient’s position along it. (Recall \(\operatorname{expit}(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), the inverse logit, increasing from \(0\) to \(1\).) The minus sign in \(\operatorname{logit}P(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x})=\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x})\) is a direction convention: a larger \(\eta(\mathbf{x})\) lowers every cumulative probability \(P(Y\le k)\), which pushes probability mass toward the higher (worse) categories. So with this convention a positive treatment or covariate effect on \(\eta\) means “more severe.” The name proportional odds refers to the property proved next: the effect of covariates on the cumulative odds is the same at every cutpoint \(k\), a single shared slope rather than one slope per threshold, which is what makes the model parsimonious enough to enter a network with a single \(\eta_{jk}\).

Lemma 25.2 (The proportional-odds category probabilities are a valid distribution; proportional odds) Fix \(\mathbf{x}\) and write \(p_k=p_k(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\) as in Equation 25.17. If the cutpoints are ordered, \(\alpha_1\le\cdots\le\alpha_{K-1}\), then \(p_k\ge 0\) for all \(k\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k=1\). Moreover the model has the proportional-odds property: for any two covariate values \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}'\) and any cutpoint \(k\in\{1,\dots,K-1\}\), the cumulative log odds ratio \[ \log\frac{\operatorname{odds}(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x})}{\operatorname{odds}(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x}')} =\eta(\mathbf{x}')-\eta(\mathbf{x}) \] does not depend on \(k\).

Proof. Nonnegativity. The function \(\operatorname{expit}\) is strictly increasing. For \(1\le k\le K\) the ordering \(\alpha_{k-1}\le\alpha_k\) (including the conventions \(\alpha_0=-\infty\le\alpha_1\) and \(\alpha_{K-1}\le+\infty=\alpha_K\)) gives \(\alpha_{k-1}-\eta(\mathbf{x})\le\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x})\), hence \(\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_{k-1}-\eta)\le\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_k-\eta)\), so \(p_k=\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_k-\eta)-\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_{k-1}-\eta)\ge 0\).

Sum to one. The sum telescopes: \[ \sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big[\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_k-\eta)-\operatorname{expit} (\alpha_{k-1}-\eta)\Big]=\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_K-\eta)-\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_0-\eta)=1-0=1. \]

Proportional odds. From Equation 25.16, \(\operatorname{logit}P(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x})= \alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x})\), and the logit of a probability is the log odds, so the log odds of \(Y\le k\) is \(\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x})\). Subtracting the same expression at \(\mathbf{x}'\) cancels the cutpoint \(\alpha_k\): \[ \log\operatorname{odds}(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x})-\log\operatorname{odds}(Y\le k\mid\mathbf{x}') =\big(\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x})\big)-\big(\alpha_k-\eta(\mathbf{x}')\big)=\eta(\mathbf{x}')-\eta(\mathbf{x}), \] independent of \(k\). \(\square\)

The conditional individual likelihood for an ordered categorical observation \(y_i=k\) is simply the category probability \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;k,\mathbf{x})=p_k(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\) of Equation 25.17, which is a probability mass in \([0,1]\), so Theorem 25.5 and Theorem 25.6 apply verbatim: the marginal individual likelihood of an AgD ordinal observation is \(\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{k}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\), evaluated by QMC. Because \(p_k\) is not affine in a single mean, this integral, like the survival integral, does not reduce to mean aggregation.

Example 25.2 (A three-category performance-status aggregation) Collapse ECOG performance status into three ordered bands \(Y\in\{1,2,3\}\) (good, intermediate, poor), and use the same binary high-risk covariate \(X\in\{0,1\}\) as the running survival trial, with prevalence \(q=P(X=1)=\tfrac12\) in the comparator AgD population. Take cutpoints \(\alpha_1=-1,\ \alpha_2=1\) and a linear predictor \(\eta(x)=\eta_0+\beta x\) with \(\eta_0=0,\ \beta=1\), so \(\eta(0)=0\) and \(\eta(1)=1\); the high-risk covariate raises severity.

Conditional category probabilities. With \(p_k(x)=\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_k-\eta(x))-\operatorname{expit}(\alpha_{k-1}-\eta(x))\) and \(\operatorname{expit}(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), \[ \begin{aligned} x=0:\quad &P(Y\le1)=\operatorname{expit}(-1)=0.269,\quad P(Y\le2)=\operatorname{expit}(1)=0.731,\\ &(p_1,p_2,p_3)=(0.269,\ 0.462,\ 0.269);\\[2pt] x=1:\quad &P(Y\le1)=\operatorname{expit}(-2)=0.119,\quad P(Y\le2)=\operatorname{expit}(0)=0.500,\\ &(p_1,p_2,p_3)=(0.119,\ 0.381,\ 0.500). \end{aligned} \] The high-risk covariate (\(x=1\)) moves mass out of the good band (\(p_1:0.269\to0.119\)) and into the poor band (\(p_3:0.269\to0.500\)), exactly the “larger \(\eta\) means more severe” direction of the sign convention. Proportional odds is visible directly: the \(x{=}1\) versus \(x{=}0\) difference in cumulative log odds is \(\eta(0)-\eta(1)=-1\) at both cutpoints (at \(k=1\), \((-2)-(-1)=-1\); at \(k=2\), \(0-1=-1\)), one shared slope.

Marginal (aggregate) category probabilities. Averaging over \(X\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\tfrac12)\), a two-point sum with weights \(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\), gives the integrated category probabilities of an AgD patient: \[ \bar p_1=\tfrac12(0.269)+\tfrac12(0.119)=0.194,\quad \bar p_2=\tfrac12(0.462)+\tfrac12(0.381)=0.421,\quad \bar p_3=\tfrac12(0.269)+\tfrac12(0.500)=0.384, \] which sum to \(1\). The marginal individual likelihood of a single AgD observation recorded in the poor band is \(L_i=\bar p_3=0.384\); for the intermediate band it is \(\bar p_2=0.421\). These are the ordinal instances of Theorem 25.5: the two integration nodes are the covariate values \(\{0,1\}\) with QMC weights \(\{\tfrac12,\tfrac12\}\), exactly as in Step 4 of the survival example, and a continuous covariate would replace the two-point sum with an \(N\)-node Sobol’ average. The aggregate arm likelihood is the product of such \(\bar p_{y_i}\) over the arm’s individuals, by Theorem 25.6. Because \(p_k\) is not affine in a single mean, this integrated likelihood does not collapse to a category probability evaluated at some aggregate mean; the full two-point (in general \(N\)-point) average is retained, precisely as for survival. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. When proportional odds fails. The single slope \(\eta(\mathbf{x})\) shared across cutpoints is an assumption; it can be assessed and relaxed. Sensitivity models include the adjacent-category logit, the continuation-ratio model, and the unconstrained multinomial logit; each supplies a different conditional category-probability kernel \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}\), and each is aggregated by the identical integral Equation 25.14. The generality of the framework is precisely that the choice of ordinal model is a choice of integrand, not a change of machinery. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This corresponds to J.7 in the proof catalogue.

25.9 The general-likelihood ML-NMR framework

We can now state the unifying theorem. Everything above was a special case of a single construction: pick a conditional individual likelihood, average it over the covariate distribution to aggregate, multiply over individuals.

Before the statement, a map of its symbols, since several parameter objects appear at once. Each abstract object corresponds to a concrete item in a submission, illustrated on the running trial.

Whether the general integral Equation 25.18 simplifies depends entirely on one property of the kernel, affinity in the mean, summarized in Table 25.1.

The theorem then says: choose the one function \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\), and the entire aggregation, integration, and inference pipeline of Chapters 20 to 24 runs unchanged. That is the structural reason ML-NMR, unlike MAIC (Chapter 18) and STC (Chapter 19), never needs to be rebuilt for a new outcome type.

Theorem 25.7 (General-likelihood ML-NMR) Let \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\) be any individual-level conditional likelihood (a density or probability mass in \(y\) for each value of the conditional parameter \(\boldsymbol\theta\)), and let the network model determine the conditional parameter of an individual with covariates \(\mathbf{x}\) in study \(j\) on arm \(k\) through \(\boldsymbol\theta=\boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\), built from the linear predictor \(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_1+\gamma_k+\mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\) of Notation. Then:

  1. (IPD arm.) For an IPD arm with observed covariates, the arm log-likelihood is the sum of conditional contributions, \(\sum_{i}\log\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\big(y_{ijk}\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}_{ijk};\boldsymbol\xi)\big)\).

  2. (AgD arm.) For an AgD arm with \(n_{jk}\) (possibly reconstructed) observations and unobserved covariates distributed as \(f_j\), the arm likelihood is the integrated likelihood \[ \bar L_{jk}(\boldsymbol\xi)=\prod_{i=1}^{n_{jk}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}} \big(y_{ijk}\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\big)\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}, \tag{25.18}\] each factor a well-defined likelihood contribution by Theorem 25.5, the product justified by Theorem 25.6, and the integral computed by the Sobol’ QMC rule of Chapter 22.

  3. (GLM reduction.) If \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\theta)\) is a one-parameter GLM density with mean \(\theta=g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\) and the kernel is affine in \(\theta\) for each fixed \(y\), then Equation 25.18 reduces to the mean-aggregation model of Theorem 20.1 with aggregate mean \(\bar\theta_{jk}=\int g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\). The affine-in-\(\theta\) hypothesis is essential and restrictive: it holds for the Bernoulli mass and the identity-link Gaussian mean, but not for the Poisson mass \(\theta^{y}e^{-\theta}/y!\), the survival kernel \(h^{\delta}S\), or the proportional-odds mass \(p_k\), all of which are nonlinear in \(\theta\) and retain the full integral (Table 25.1). So “reduces to a GLM” really means “reduces in the Bernoulli or identity-link case”; the other families genuinely need Equation 25.18.

  4. (NMA reduction.) If there are no covariates (or \(f_j\) is degenerate at a single \(\mathbf{x}_j\)), Equation 25.18 reduces to the standard network meta-analysis likelihood (Theorem 23.4).

In particular Equation 25.18 accommodates survival outcomes with \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}=h^{\delta}S\) (Definition 25.4) and ordered categorical outcomes with \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}=p_k\) (Definition 25.5), as well as continuous, binary, count, and negative binomial outcomes, by changing only the integrand \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\).

Proof. Part 1 is the definition of a likelihood from conditionally independent observations: with covariates observed, individual \(i\) contributes \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y_{ijk}\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}_{ijk}; \boldsymbol\xi))\), and conditional independence makes the arm likelihood the product, hence the arm log-likelihood the sum.

Part 2 is Theorem 25.6 with the conditional kernel \(L_{i\mid\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\xi;y_{ijk},\mathbf{x})=\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y_{ijk}\mid \boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi))\). Each factor is well defined and finite by Theorem 25.5: for a probability-mass kernel \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\in[0,1]\) the integral is at most \(1\), and for a density kernel it is the marginal density of \(Y_{ijk}\), finite for almost every value. The QMC evaluation is the Sobol’ rule of Theorem 22.6 applied to the nonnegative integrand \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y_{ijk}\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol\xi))\), with the covariate marginals mapped by Theorem 22.4 and the dependence supplied by the Gaussian copula (Definition 22.5, Theorem 22.5).

Part 3. Suppose \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\theta)\) is, for each fixed \(y\), an affine function of the mean \(\theta\), say \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\theta)=A(y)+B(y)\,\theta\). With \(\theta(\mathbf{x})= g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\) and \(\bar\theta_{jk}=\int g^{-1}(\eta_{jk})f_j\), \[ \int_{\mathcal{X}}\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\big(y\mid g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\big)f_j(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}=A(y)\!\int\! f_j+B(y)\!\int\! g^{-1}(\eta_{jk})f_j=A(y)+B(y)\,\bar\theta_{jk} =\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\bar\theta_{jk}), \] so the integrated likelihood equals the kernel evaluated at the aggregate mean \(\bar\theta_{jk}\), which is the mean-aggregation model of Theorem 20.1. (The Bernoulli kernel \(\theta^{y}(1-\theta)^{1-y}\) with \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is affine in \(\theta\), equal to \(\theta\) when \(y=1\) and \(1-\theta\) when \(y=0\), so binary ML-NMR is the canonical instance.)

Part 4. If there are no covariates, \(\boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)= \boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\boldsymbol\xi)\) is constant in \(\mathbf{x}\), the integral over the (degenerate) \(f_j\) is the integrand itself, and Equation 25.18 becomes \(\prod_i\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y_{ijk}\mid \boldsymbol\theta_{jk}(\boldsymbol\xi))\), the ordinary arm likelihood of a network meta-analysis on the chosen scale; the network reduces to the no-regression case of Theorem 23.4.

The survival and ordinal instances are immediate: substitute \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(t,\delta\mid\mathbf{x})= h(t\mid\mathbf{x})^{\delta}S(t\mid\mathbf{x})\) from Definition 25.4, or \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(k\mid\mathbf{x})=p_k(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\xi)\) from Definition 25.5, into Equation 25.18. Neither kernel is affine in a single mean, so neither reduces under Part 3; the full integral is retained and computed by QMC. \(\square\)

Remark. Cost, and why it is the price of generality. The simple picture first: for each aggregate patient we compute the outcome likelihood at \(N\) representative covariate values and average them, and we redo this every time the sampler proposes new parameters. Everything after this sentence is detail. The integrated likelihood Equation 25.18 is evaluated at \(N\) Sobol’ nodes for each AgD observation at every Hamiltonian Monte Carlo leapfrog step (Chapter 24). For survival there is a second integral hidden inside each node: computing \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\exp(-\int_0^t h)\) at a flexible baseline hazard requires integrating the hazard over time. The cost is therefore \(N\) covariate nodes times a time quadrature times the number of observations times the number of gradient evaluations; this is the heaviest computation in the book, but it is linear in each factor and entirely parallelizable, and it is what buys an outcome-agnostic method. The diagnostics of Chapter 24, \(\widehat R\) (Theorem 24.2), effective sample size (Definition 24.3), and the integration-error monitoring of Definition 24.4, govern both the Markov chain and the QMC truncation; model comparison across competing likelihoods (a Weibull against an M-spline baseline, proportional odds against multinomial) uses the information criteria of Theorem 24.3 and the Pareto-smoothed importance-sampling leave-one-out of Definition 24.6. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. One family at a time, or a joint network? A natural question is whether a single network may combine survival, ordinal, binary, and count outcomes. Two readings, both supported by the framework. The common case in this book is one outcome family per network: every study reports the same endpoint, one kernel \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\) is used throughout, and the machinery is reused across outcome families only in the sense that different networks plug in different kernels. The framework also permits, in principle, a single joint model whose studies report different outcome families, provided they share the treatment-effect parameters \(\gamma_k\) and \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\) on one common linear-predictor scale: then study \(j\) contributes its own kernel \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}^{(j)}\) but the same \(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\), and the shared estimand is the relative effect on that scale. What makes such a combination meaningful is precisely a shared linear-predictor-scale effect; without it there is nothing to pool. Unless stated otherwise, the networks in this book use one family at a time. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Generality as the distinguishing feature. This theorem is the structural reason ML-NMR is more flexible than its competitors. Matching-adjusted indirect comparison (Chapter 18) requires the aggregate data in a moment form to solve its balancing equations, and simulated treatment comparison (Chapter 19) requires an outcome regression of a specific form; both need rework for each outcome type, and neither ingests a censored survival time or an ordinal band without it. General-likelihood ML-NMR changes one function, \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}\), and reuses the entire aggregation, integration, and inference pipeline. The same generality carries to the unanchored extension ML-UMR (Chapter 26), where the time-to-event and proportional-odds kernels reappear inside the unanchored likelihood (Theorem 26.4). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This capstone corresponds to J.8 in the proof catalogue.

25.10 A fully worked survival example

We carry a complete survival aggregation from a conditional hazard model to an aggregate-data likelihood, choosing the numbers so that every survival probability, marginal hazard, restricted mean, and likelihood factor is exact and checkable by hand. The example uses a single binary covariate, which turns every covariate integral into a two-point sum (the degenerate QMC rule for a Bernoulli marginal) and makes the non-collapsibility of the hazard ratio and the collapsibility of the RMST visible in closed form. It is a stylized analogue of the multiple-myeloma progression-free-survival network of Phillippo et al. (2025), whose multinma analysis integrates four covariates (age modeled as gamma, three binary) at \(64\) Sobol’ nodes with a Gaussian copula; here we keep one binary covariate so the arithmetic is transparent.

Example 25.3 (Exponential-hazard aggregation with a binary prognostic covariate) This is the full computation promised for the running trial of Example 25.1; it takes that trial from the conditional hazard model to the aggregate-data likelihood. A target population \(\mathcal{P}\) (the comparator AgD population) has a single binary prognostic covariate \(X\in\{0,1\}\) with \(P(X=1)=q=\tfrac12\); \(X\) marks a high-risk subgroup. The outcome is time to progression, modeled with a constant (exponential) conditional hazard. The control treatment \(C\) has \[ h_C(s\mid x)=\lambda_x,\qquad \lambda_0=1,\quad\lambda_1=3\quad(\text{events per year}), \] so the high-risk group has triple the control hazard. Treatment \(A\) acts by proportional hazards with a covariate-independent conditional hazard ratio \(\psi=\tfrac12\) (a beneficial treatment, conditional log HR \(=\log\tfrac12=-0.693\), no effect modification), \[ h_A(s\mid x)=\psi\,\lambda_x=\tfrac12\lambda_x . \] By Theorem 25.1 the conditional survival functions are \(S_C(s\mid x)=e^{-\lambda_x s}\) and \(S_A(s\mid x)=e^{-\psi\lambda_x s}=e^{-\lambda_x s/2}\).

Step 1: marginal survival. Averaging over \(X\) (a two-point sum, weights \(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\)), \[ S_C^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\tfrac12 e^{-s}+\tfrac12 e^{-3s},\qquad S_A^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\tfrac12 e^{-s/2}+\tfrac12 e^{-3s/2}. \]

Step 2: the marginal hazard ratio is time-varying (Theorem 25.3). Using Lemma 25.1, \(h_C^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=(e^{-s}+3e^{-3s})/(e^{-s}+e^{-3s})\) and \(h_A^{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\tfrac12(e^{-s/2}+3e^{-3s/2})/(e^{-s/2}+e^{-3s/2})\). The conditional hazard ratio is the constant \(\psi=0.5\) at every time and covariate; the marginal hazard ratio \(\mathrm{mHR}(s)=h_A^{\mathcal{P}}(s)/h_C^{\mathcal{P}}(s)\) is not:

\(s\) (years) \(h_C^{\mathcal{P}}(s)\) \(h_A^{\mathcal{P}}(s)\) \(\mathrm{mHR}(s)\)
\(0\) \(2.0000\) \(1.0000\) \(0.5000\)
\(0.5\) \(1.5379\) \(0.8776\) \(0.5706\)
\(1\) \(1.2384\) \(0.7689\) \(0.6209\)
\(2\) \(1.0360\) \(0.6192\) \(0.5977\)
\(4\) \(1.0007\) \(0.5180\) \(0.5176\)
\(\infty\) \(1.0000\) \(0.5000\) \(0.5000\)

At \(s=0\) no selection has occurred and the marginal hazard ratio equals the conditional value \(0.5\); for every \(s>0\) it exceeds \(0.5\), attenuated toward the null \(1\), peaking near \(0.62\) around one year before the slow (\(x=0\)) stratum comes to dominate both risk sets and the ratio returns toward \(0.5\). A single Cox fit reporting “\(\mathrm{HR}=0.60\)” would be reporting an artifact of the one-year follow-up window, not the conditional effect \(0.5\). (Check the \(s=0\) entry by hand: \(h_C^{\mathcal{P}}(0)=(1+3)/2=2\) is the average baseline hazard, \(h_A^{\mathcal{P}}(0)=\tfrac12\cdot 2=1\), ratio \(0.5\).)

Step 3: RMST is collapsible (Theorem 25.4). Take horizon \(\tau=2\) years. The conditional RMSTs are \(\operatorname{RMST}_t(\tau\mid x)=\int_0^{\tau}e^{-\lambda_x^{(t)}s}\,ds=(1-e^{-\lambda_x^{(t)} \tau})/\lambda_x^{(t)}\) with \(\lambda_x^{(C)}=\lambda_x\) and \(\lambda_x^{(A)}=\tfrac12\lambda_x\): \[ \operatorname{RMST}_C(2\mid 0)=1-e^{-2}=0.8647,\quad \operatorname{RMST}_C(2\mid 1)=\tfrac{1-e^{-6}}{3}=0.3325, \] \[ \operatorname{RMST}_A(2\mid 0)=\tfrac{1-e^{-1}}{0.5}=1.2642,\quad \operatorname{RMST}_A(2\mid 1)=\tfrac{1-e^{-3}}{1.5}=0.6335 . \] The marginal RMSTs computed two ways agree exactly. Averaging the conditional values: \(\operatorname{RMST}_C^{\mathcal{P}}(2)=\tfrac12(0.8647+0.3325)=0.5986\) and \(\operatorname{RMST}_A^{\mathcal{P}}(2)=\tfrac12(1.2642+0.6335)=0.9489\). Integrating the marginal survival of Step 1: \(\int_0^{2}(\tfrac12 e^{-s}+\tfrac12 e^{-3s})\,ds=\tfrac12(1-e^{-2})+\tfrac16(1-e^{-6})= 0.5986\), the same. The collapsible RMST contrast is \(\Delta\!\operatorname{RMST}_{AC}^{\mathcal{P}}(2)=0.9489-0.5986=0.3503\) years, equal to the average of the conditional contrasts \(\tfrac12(0.3995+0.3010)=0.3503\). Unlike the hazard ratio, this marginal contrast is the average of conditional contrasts.

Step 4: the marginal individual likelihood (Theorem 25.5). The comparator study reports its covariate distribution (\(X\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\tfrac12)\)) and supplies, on the control arm, reconstructed pseudo-IPD; take two pseudo-observations, an event at \(t_1=0.5\) (\(\delta_1=1\)) and a right-censoring at \(t_2=2\) (\(\delta_2=0\)). With the conditional censored kernel \(L_{i\mid x}=h_C(t\mid x)^{\delta}S_C(t\mid x)=\lambda_x^{\delta}e^{-\lambda_x t}\), the marginal contributions are the two-point sums \[ L_1(\boldsymbol\xi)=\tfrac12\big[\lambda_0 e^{-\lambda_0 t_1}\big]+\tfrac12\big[\lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1 t_1}\big] =\tfrac12 e^{-0.5}+\tfrac12\,(3e^{-1.5})=0.3033+0.3347=0.6380, \] \[ L_2(\boldsymbol\xi)=\tfrac12\big[e^{-\lambda_0 t_2}\big]+\tfrac12\big[e^{-\lambda_1 t_2}\big] =\tfrac12 e^{-2}+\tfrac12 e^{-6}=0.06767+0.00124=0.06891 . \] (\(L_1\) is a density, with units of inverse time; \(L_2\) is a probability.) Here the two integration nodes are the covariate values \(\{0,1\}\) with QMC weights \(\{\tfrac12,\tfrac12\}\); a continuous covariate would replace this exact two-point rule with an \(N\)-node Sobol’ average, as in the \(64\)-node multinma analysis.

Step 5: the aggregate arm likelihood (Theorem 25.6). The product over the two pseudo-observations is \[ \bar L_{C}(\boldsymbol\xi)=L_1(\boldsymbol\xi)\,L_2(\boldsymbol\xi)=0.6380\times 0.06891=0.04396 . \] This is the contribution of the comparator control arm to the network likelihood at the parameter values \((\lambda_0,\lambda_1)=(1,3)\); as a function of the unknown \(\boldsymbol\xi\) it is \(\bar L_C(\boldsymbol\xi)=\big[\tfrac12\lambda_0 e^{-\lambda_0/2}+\tfrac12\lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1/2}\big] \cdot\big[\tfrac12 e^{-2\lambda_0}+\tfrac12 e^{-2\lambda_1}\big]\) (each bracket is \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) rewritten as functions of the free rates, using the observed times \(t_1=\tfrac12\) and \(t_2=2\): the first factor carries \(e^{-\lambda_x t_1}=e^{-\lambda_x/2}\), the second \(e^{-\lambda_x t_2}=e^{-2\lambda_x}\)), ready for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. The treatment arm contributes the analogous product with \(\lambda_x\) replaced by \(\psi\lambda_x\), tying the arms together through the shared \(\psi\) exactly as the anchored network requires. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What the example shows. The conditional model is as clean as a GLM: a constant hazard ratio \(\psi=0.5\), linear on the log-hazard scale, identifiable from any arm. Yet the marginal hazard ratio is time-varying (Step 2), so it is not a transportable parameter; the RMST contrast is collapsible (Step 3), so it is a faithful read-out in a stated population but, by the scale-misalignment remark after Theorem 25.4, still requires the target covariate distribution to transport. The aggregation itself (Steps 4 and 5) is exactly the integrated likelihood Equation 25.18 with \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}= h^{\delta}S\), computed as a finite sum because the covariate is binary. Replace \(h^{\delta}S\) by the proportional-odds mass \(p_k\) and the same five steps deliver the ordinal aggregate likelihood; this is the generality of Theorem 25.7 made concrete. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

25.11 Notes and references

The general-likelihood ML-NMR framework of this chapter is the contribution of Phillippo et al. (2025), which extends the original binary and count ML-NMR of Phillippo et al. (2020) to survival, ordered categorical, and other outcome types through the integrated likelihood Equation 25.18, and is implemented in the multinma package. The survival development, the proportional-hazards and accelerated-failure-time individual models, the flexible M-spline baseline hazard, and the reconstruction of pseudo-IPD from published Kaplan-Meier curves, follows Phillippo et al. (2025) and Chapter 7 of Phillippo (2019). The survival fundamentals of Section 25.3, the survival function, the hazard, the identity \(S=\exp(-\int h)\), and the right-censored likelihood under independent censoring, are classical; the standard reference is Klein and Moeschberger (see the proposed bibliography entry in the work log), and the independent-censoring factorization Proposition 25.1 is the textbook construction. The Kaplan-Meier reconstruction algorithm is due to Guyot and colleagues (proposed entry in the log); its role in the unanchored network is developed in Chapter 26 (Theorem 26.5).

The non-collapsibility of the marginal hazard ratio (Theorem 25.3) is a much-discussed feature of survival analysis: even a correctly specified conditional proportional-hazards model induces a marginal hazard ratio that varies with time and is attenuated toward the null, because the at-risk set is selected by survival. The survivor-weighted representation Equation 25.7 and the exponential-tilt covariance argument given here make the attenuation precise and quantitative; the phenomenon is the survival-scale instance of the general non-collapsibility of Definition 7.7 (Chapter 7) and of the odds-ratio non-collapsibility of Chapter 11, and it is the reason the restricted mean survival time (Definition 25.3), collapsible by the Fubini argument of Theorem 25.4, is increasingly preferred as a marginal summary. The transportability subtlety, that RMST collapsibility does not imply RMST transportability when the conditional model is on the log-hazard scale, is the scale-misalignment finding analyzed for the unanchored case by Chandler and Ishak (2026) and revisited in Chapter 26 (Theorem 26.4); the clean transportable object remains the conditional log-hazard model under the shared-effect-modifier assumption (Definition 17.6) of Chapter 17.

The proportional-odds model (Definition 25.5) is the standard cumulative-logit model for ordered categorical data; the validity lemma Lemma 25.2 and the proportional-odds interpretation are elementary and appear in any categorical-data text, with McCullagh and Nelder (1989) the classical GLM reference. The aggregation theorems Theorem 25.5 and Theorem 25.6 are the general-likelihood forms of the mean-aggregation theorem Theorem 20.1 of Chapter 20, and they inherit the numerical-integration apparatus of Chapter 22, the Sobol’ net (Definition 22.3, Theorem 22.6, Sobol’ (1967)), the inverse-CDF transform (Theorem 22.4, Owen (1956)), and the Gaussian copula (Definition 22.5, Theorem 22.5, Sklar (1959)). The Bayesian computation, including the diagnostics and model comparison referenced in Section 25.9, is the Stan/Hamiltonian Monte Carlo apparatus of Chapter 24 (Carpenter et al. (2017)), and the network reductions to standard NMA (Theorem 23.4) connect to Dias et al. (2018).

There is no machine-checked counterpart for this chapter. The companion Coq development (~/Documents/GitHub/ITC_Coq/theories/) does not formalize survival, hazard, restricted mean survival time, or ordered categorical outcomes: there is no Survival.v, and the only mention of hazards and non-collapsibility is a prose comment in STC.v. The reasons, recorded in the source manuscript, are that survival’s content requires Lebesgue integration over time beyond the analysis machinery used elsewhere in the development, and that the most interesting statements, the time-variation of the marginal hazard ratio and its non-collapsibility, are analytic results more naturally stated in prose than as Coq lemmas. The results of this chapter are therefore pen-and-paper, and the proofs above are self-contained. This chapter corresponds to results J.1 (survival function), J.2 (hazard), J.3 (the identity \(S=\exp(-\int h)\)), J.4 (the right-censored likelihood, with J.4a the conditional hazard ratio, J.4b the time-varying marginal hazard ratio, and J.4c the collapsible RMST), J.5 (the marginal individual likelihood), J.6 (the aggregate arm likelihood), J.7 (the proportional-odds model), and J.8 (the general-likelihood framework) of the proof catalogue in proofs/J_general_likelihoods.md.

25.12 Exercises

The exercises progress from warm-up and interpretation to computation and proof. A star (\(\star\)) marks an exercise whose full worked solution appears in Appendix F. Start with the first two if the formal development felt fast: they use only the running trial and the definitions.

Exercise 25.1 (Warm-up: hazard is a rate, not a probability.) In the running trial (Example 25.1) the control hazard is the constant \(\lambda_0=1\) per year for standard-risk and \(\lambda_1=3\) per year for high-risk patients. (a) Using \(S(t\mid x)=e^{-\lambda_x t}\), compute the probability a standard-risk control patient is progression-free at \(t=1\) and at \(t=2\) years. (b) Do the same for a high-risk patient. (c) A colleague says “a hazard of \(3\) per year means a \(300\%\) chance of progressing in a year, which is impossible.” In one or two sentences, explain what is wrong with this reading and what the number \(3\) actually measures.

Exercise 25.2 (Interpretation: conditional versus marginal hazard ratio.) The running trial has conditional hazard ratio \(\psi=0.5\) at every time and covariate value, yet the marginal hazard ratio in Example 25.3 rises to about \(0.62\) near one year before falling back. (a) State in one sentence which of the two, conditional or marginal, is the transportable estimand the network reports, and why. (b) Using the risk-set-drift table in Section 25.4, explain in plain language why the treated arm retains relatively more high-risk patients at later times. (c) A submission reports a single “HR \(=0.60\)” from an unadjusted Cox fit with two years of follow-up. Explain why this number should not be read as the per-patient effect of treatment.

Exercise 25.3 (Computation) Weibull from the hazard. Let the conditional hazard be the power law \(h(s\mid\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{x})\,\nu\,s^{\nu-1}\) with shape \(\nu>0\) and scale \(\lambda(\mathbf{x})>0\). Using Theorem 25.1, compute the cumulative hazard \(H(s\mid\mathbf{x})\), the survival function \(S(s\mid\mathbf{x})\), and the density \(f(s\mid\mathbf{x})\), and verify that \(f=h\,S\). Show that with \(\nu=1\) the model is exponential. Then write the right-censored individual likelihood Equation 25.12 for an observation \((t,\delta)\) and confirm it reduces to a density when \(\delta=1\) and to a survival probability when \(\delta=0\).

Exercise 25.4 (Computation) Additivity of the cumulative hazard under PH. Under the PH model Equation 25.5 with a constant baseline hazard \(h_{0j}(s)=\kappa_j\), show that the conditional cumulative hazard is \(H_{jk}(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\kappa_j t\,\exp(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\) and hence \(S_{jk}(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\exp(-\kappa_j t\,e^{\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})})\). Deduce that on the log cumulative-hazard scale, \(\log H_{jk}(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\log(\kappa_j t)+\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\), so the covariate effect is additive and time-invariant. Explain why this makes the conditional log hazard ratio Equation 25.6 time-invariant but does not make the marginal hazard ratio time-invariant.

Exercise 25.5 (Proof) Binary ML-NMR as a special case. Take \(\pi_{\mathrm{Ind}}(y\mid\theta)=\theta^{y}(1-\theta)^{1-y}\) with \(y\in\{0,1\}\) and \(\theta=\operatorname{expit}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}))\). Using Theorem 25.7 part 3, show that the integrated marginal likelihood Equation 25.14 equals \(\bar p_{jk}^{\,y}(1-\bar p_{jk})^{1-y}\) with \(\bar p_{jk}=\int \operatorname{expit}(\eta_{jk})f_j\), and that the aggregate arm likelihood for an event count \(r_{jk}\) out of \(n_{jk}\) is the binomial kernel \(\bar p_{jk}^{\,r_{jk}}(1-\bar p_{jk})^{n_{jk}-r_{jk}}\). Contrast this with the survival kernel \(h^{\delta}S\) and explain precisely why the latter does not collapse to a function of a single aggregate mean.

Exercise 25.6 (Computation) A three-category proportional-odds likelihood. Let \(K=3\) with cutpoints \(\alpha_1\le\alpha_2\) and linear predictor \(\eta\). Write the three category probabilities \(p_1,p_2,p_3\) from Equation 25.17 explicitly in terms of \(\operatorname{expit}\), verify directly that they are nonnegative and sum to one (Lemma 25.2), and compute the marginal individual likelihood of an AgD observation \(y=2\) when \(X\in\{0,1\}\) with \(P(X=1)=q\) and \(\eta(x)=\gamma+\beta x\), as the two-point sum \(q\,p_2(1)+(1-q)\,p_2(0)\). Numerically evaluate it for \(\alpha_1=-1,\alpha_2=1,\gamma=0.5,\beta=1,q=0.4\).

Exercise 25.7 (\(\star\), Challenge) Quantifying the attenuation. In the frailty setting of Theorem 25.3 with a binary frailty \(\rho(X)\in\{\rho_0,\rho_1\}\), \(\rho_0<\rho_1\), \(P(X=1)=q\), constant baseline \(h_{a0}(s)=1\), and constant conditional hazard ratio \(\psi\in(0,1)\), derive a closed-form expression for the correction factor \(R(s)\) in Equation 25.9 and hence for \(\mathrm{mHR}(s)\). Prove that \(R(s)\ge 1\) for all \(s\) with equality only at \(s=0\) and as \(s\to\infty\), that \(\mathrm{mHR}(s)>\psi\) on \((0,\infty)\), and that \(\lim_{s\to 0^+}\mathrm{mHR}(s)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathrm{mHR}(s)=\psi\). Identify the time of maximal attenuation by setting \(\tfrac{d}{ds}R(s)=0\) and interpret it as the moment of maximal survivor selection. (This generalizes the table in Example 25.3, recovered at \(\rho_0=1,\rho_1=3,q=\tfrac12, \psi=\tfrac12\).)

Hint. With a binary frailty the survivor covariate density is a two-point distribution, so write \(R(s)\) as a ratio of two tilted two-point means and use the exponential-tilt identity from the proof of Theorem 25.3. The maximizer of \(R(s)\) solves a transcendental equation; identifying it qualitatively, as the time of maximal spread between the two survivor distributions, is enough for the interpretation.

Exercise 25.8 (\(\star\), Proof) Collapsible but not transportable. Consider two populations \(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{P}^{*}\) with the same binary covariate \(X\) but different prevalences \(q\ne q^{*}\), and the exponential PH model of Example 25.3 (conditional hazards \(\lambda_x\) for control and \(\psi\lambda_x\) for treatment, shared across populations: the shared-effect-modifier assumption holds on the log-hazard scale). Using Theorem 25.4, show that the RMST contrast \(\Delta\!\operatorname{RMST}_{AC}(\tau)\) differs between \(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{P}^{*}\), and give an explicit formula for the difference in terms of \(q-q^{*}\). Conclude that RMST collapsibility (a within-population identity) does not imply RMST transportability (a cross-population identity), and explain how this differs from the conditional log-hazard model, which is shared across the two populations by assumption. Relate your conclusion to the scale-misalignment remark following Theorem 25.4 and to Chapter 26 (Theorem 26.4).

Hint. By Theorem 25.4 each population’s RMST contrast is a weighted average of the two conditional contrasts with weights \((1-q,q)\); subtract the two averages, the conditional contrasts cancel, and the difference is a single multiple of \(q-q^{*}\). The two-population snapshot in Section 25.5 is the numeric special case.
