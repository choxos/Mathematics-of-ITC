24  Bayesian Computation and Diagnostics

24.1 Introduction

The three preceding chapters built the multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) model and showed how its aggregate-data likelihood is an integral of an individual-level integrand against a covariate distribution (the aggregation theorem, Theorem 20.1), how that integral is computed by quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) on the unit hypercube (Theorem 22.6), and how the resulting model is identified and estimated (Theorem 23.1, Theorem 23.4). Nothing in that development told us how to actually fit the model, nor how to know whether the fit can be trusted. That is the business of this chapter.

ML-NMR and its unanchored extension are estimated by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), specifically by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and its adaptive form the No-U-Turn Sampler, the engine of the Stan probabilistic programming language (Carpenter et al. (2017)) on which the reference software is built. We proved in Chapter 8 that HMC (Definition 8.9) leaves the target posterior invariant, by way of the Metropolis-Hastings detailed-balance argument (Theorem 8.9). Invariance is a statement about the limiting behavior of an idealized chain. A real analysis runs a finite number of finite chains, and faces two distinct and additive sources of error that a responsible report must certify are small.

The first is the Monte Carlo error of the sampler itself: the chains may not have reached the stationary distribution (lack of convergence), and even at stationarity their draws are autocorrelated, so a run of length \(N\) carries less information than \(N\) independent draws. The hierarchical structure of ML-NMR makes this acute. Random effects on study baselines and on treatment contrasts (Definition 8.3, Definition 8.1) introduce a variance parameter \(\tau\) whose joint posterior with the effects it governs has a pathological “funnel” geometry that defeats a naive sampler. Section Section 24.4 shows how to remove the funnel by reparameterization, and proves precisely why it works in terms of the leapfrog geometry of Definition 8.9. Sections Section 24.5 and Section 24.6 give the two workhorse convergence diagnostics, the Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction statistic \(\hat R\) and the effective sample size, and derive both from first principles.

The second source is unique to ML-NMR among the methods in this book: a numerical-integration error nested inside the likelihood. Every evaluation of the aggregate likelihood approximates an integral by a finite QMC sum, so the model the sampler actually targets is a perturbation of the intended one. Section 2 turns the Koksma-Hlawka bound of Chapter 22 (Theorem 22.3) into an operational diagnostic and proves the doubling rule that certifies the integration grid is fine enough.

The chapter closes with model comparison. Sections Section 24.8 and 1 develop the deviance information criterion (DIC), the widely applicable information criterion (WAIC), and leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV), prove the relationships among them as estimates of the same expected predictive density, and give the Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) algorithm that makes LOO-CV computable without refitting the model. A fully worked diagnostic example, every number checkable by hand, ties the pieces together in Section 24.11. The chapter corresponds to results I.1 to I.6 of the proof catalogue.

Throughout, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) denotes the full parameter vector, \(\mathbf y\) the observed data, \(\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) the prior, \(p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol{\xi})\) the likelihood, and \(\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf y)\propto p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) the posterior, as in Notation and Chapter 8. We write a generic scalar quantity of interest as \(\theta=h(\boldsymbol{\xi})\), for instance a treatment contrast \(d_{ab}\) or a marginal probability \(\theta_{\bullet jk}\).

Remark. A note on two effective sample sizes. The symbol \(\mathrm{ESS}\) of Notation is the effective sample size of a weighted sample, \(\mathrm{ESS}=(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2\), which governed the variance inflation of MAIC in Chapter 18. The effective sample size of an autocorrelated MCMC chain, developed in Section 24.6, is a different quantity with a different formula, and to keep them apart we write it \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\). Both answer the same kind of question, “how many independent draws is this sample worth,” but one loses efficiency to unequal weights and the other to serial correlation. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

24.2 The sampler in one page: HMC, NUTS, warmup, and divergences

Before the diagnostics, a page on the machine they diagnose, for the reader who has fit a model in Stan but never looked under the hood. None of this is proved here; it recaps Chapter 8 and fixes the vocabulary the practical remarks below depend on.

What a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler does. We cannot write the posterior \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\) down in closed form for an ML-NMR model, but we can draw a long sequence of parameter vectors \(\boldsymbol\xi^{(1)},\boldsymbol\xi^{(2)},\dots\) whose histogram reproduces it. Each draw is proposed from the current one and then accepted or rejected; the accepted draws are our sample, and any posterior summary, a mean or a credible interval for the contrast \(d_{AC}\), is just an average over them. That is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).

Why Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. A random-walk proposal (as in random-walk Metropolis or Gibbs sampling) staggers blindly, and in the tens of correlated dimensions of an ML-NMR fit almost every proposed step is rejected, so the sampler barely moves. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) instead treats the negative log posterior \(U(\boldsymbol\xi)=-\log\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\) as a landscape and rolls a frictionless puck across it, using the gradient \(\nabla U\) to steer. A single HMC move can travel a long way across the posterior while rarely being rejected, which is why HMC, not random-walk Metropolis, is the sampler that makes ML-NMR feasible: random-walk methods die in high dimensions, and HMC uses gradient information to take long, directed steps.

How the puck moves: the leapfrog integrator. The puck’s path obeys Newtonian mechanics, which the computer solves in small time steps of size \(\epsilon\), the step size, using the leapfrog integrator of Definition 8.9. Each leapfrog step gives the puck’s momentum a half-kick from the gradient, drifts its position, then completes the kick; we write \(r\) for that momentum. The single number \(\epsilon\) controls how large each step is, and getting it wrong is the source of the trouble in Section 24.4.

What the No-U-Turn Sampler adds. Plain HMC needs the user to choose how many leapfrog steps to take per move. The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) chooses automatically: it keeps stepping until the trajectory starts to double back on itself (a “U-turn”), so no trajectory-length tuning is required. NUTS is the algorithm Stan actually runs.

Warmup versus sampling. A run has two phases. During warmup the sampler is both moving from its arbitrary starting point toward the region where the posterior mass actually lives (the typical set, which for a high-dimensional posterior is a thin shell away from the mode, so finding the mode and sampling the posterior are different tasks) and tuning two knobs: the step size \(\epsilon\) and a mass matrix, a linear rescaling of the parameters that makes the landscape rounder. Warmup draws are tuned-away and discarded; only the post-warmup draws, taken with the knobs frozen, are kept. Everything called “\(N\) draws” below means post-warmup draws.

Divergent transitions. When the landscape has a region of very sharp curvature, a fixed step size \(\epsilon\) is too coarse to follow it: the leapfrog puck flies off, the simulated energy jumps by more than a set threshold, and Stan records a divergent transition. A divergence is not a rounding nuisance; it flags a part of the posterior the sampler could not enter, so that region is under-sampled and any summary that depends on it, often the heterogeneity \(\tau\) and the effects tied to it, can be biased. Even a handful of divergences after warmup must be explained, not ignored. The rest of Section 24.4 is, at bottom, the story of where these divergences come from and how a change of coordinates removes them.

The vocabulary in one place:

Term One-line meaning
MCMC draw a dependent sequence whose histogram is the posterior
HMC gradient-guided proposals that move far with few rejections
NUTS HMC that picks its own trajectory length; the Stan default

24.3 A running example: a two-study evidence network

Every diagnostic below is illustrated on one small network, carried from section to section so that the abstractions attach to something concrete. It is the smallest network that still has all the features that make ML-NMR computation delicate.

The data. Two randomized trials inform a plaque psoriasis indirect comparison with a binary outcome, achieving a \(75\%\) reduction in the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (a “PASI 75 response”).

  • Study \(AB\) compares treatment \(A\) (control) with treatment \(B\) and reports individual patient data (IPD): for each of its patients we have the treatment arm, the outcome, and two baseline covariates, age and disease severity, each centered and scaled.
  • Study \(BC\) compares \(B\) with \(C\) and reports only aggregate data (AgD): per arm, the number of responders out of the arm total, plus the arm’s mean age and mean severity.

The network is the chain from \(A\) to \(B\) to \(C\): \(B\) is the common comparator, so the \(A\)-versus-\(C\) contrast \(d_{AC}\) is estimated only indirectly, by combining the within-study contrasts. This is exactly the ITC problem of Parts III and IV, now fit as one ML-NMR model.

The model. On the log-odds (logit) scale the linear predictor for a patient with covariates \(\mathbf x\) on arm \(k\) in study \(j\) is, as in Notation, \[ \eta_{jk}(\mathbf x)=\mu_j+\boldsymbol\beta_1^\top\mathbf x+\gamma_k+\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}^\top\mathbf x, \] with the convention \(\gamma_k=0\) and \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}=\mathbf 0\) on the reference arm, study baselines \(\mu_{AB},\mu_{BC}\), prognostic effects \(\boldsymbol\beta_1\), a treatment main effect \(\gamma_k\), and age-and-severity effect modifiers \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2,k}\). Because \(B\) appears in both studies, its relative effect is treated as exchangeable across studies with a random-effects heterogeneity scale \(\tau\) (the NMA layer of Definition 13.4).

Why this network stresses every diagnostic. It has exactly one study per contrast. That single fact drives the whole chapter:

  • \(\tau\) is estimated from a single pair of contrasts, so it is weakly identified; its posterior reaches toward \(0\), which is the funnel of Section 24.4, so the fit needs the non-centered parameterization.
  • The aggregate study \(BC\) contributes to the likelihood only through an integral of the individual-level response over its covariate distribution, a two-dimensional integral over age and severity that must be done by QMC (2).
  • With so few studies, one of them can dominate: the \(\widehat k\) diagnostic of PSIS-LOO
    1. will tell us whether \(d_{AC}\) rests on a single study.
  • And we will want to compare models, for instance with and without the age effect modifier \(\boldsymbol\beta_{2}\), which is the job of WAIC and LOO (Section 24.8).

Keep this network in mind: at each transition we return to it and read the diagnostic in its terms.

24.4 Centered and non-centered parameterizations

The random-effects layer of an ML-NMR model is, at its core, the normal hierarchy of Chapter 8: a vector of study- or contrast-level effects drawn from a common population distribution whose scale \(\tau\) is itself estimated. We isolate that layer and study how the algebraic form in which it is written to the sampler changes the geometry HMC must traverse, even though it cannot change the posterior distribution. This is the content of catalogue result I.1.

Here is the problem in one picture, before any algebra. In our running network \(\tau\) is the scale of the random treatment contrast, estimated from a single pair of studies, so the posterior lets \(\tau\) wander close to \(0\). Plot the draws of a study effect \(\theta_j\) against \(\log\tau\). The cloud is shaped like a trumpet bell, or a funnel: wide at the top, where \(\tau\) is large and the effects \(\theta_j\) range freely, and pinched to a needle at the bottom, where \(\tau\) is small and every \(\theta_j\) is forced close to the common mean \(\mu\). A sampler exploring this shape must take large steps in the wide mouth and minute steps in the narrow neck. A single step size cannot do both: tuned for the mouth it flies out of the neck and diverges; tuned for the neck it crawls hopelessly in the mouth. That mismatch is the whole pathology, and the fix is to change coordinates so the funnel becomes a cylinder. The two coordinate systems are the centered and non-centered parameterizations.

Definition 24.1 (Centered and non-centered parameterization) Consider the normal hierarchy for effects \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_J\) with population mean \(\mu\) and scale \(\tau>0\), \[ \theta_j \mid \mu,\tau \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\tau^2), \qquad j=1,\dots,J, \tag{24.1}\] embedded in a model whose remaining likelihood depends on the \(\theta_j\) through data terms.

  • The centered parameterization (CP) takes the unknowns to be \((\mu,\tau,\theta_1,\dots,\theta_J)\) themselves, so that Equation 24.1 enters the prior directly.
  • The non-centered parameterization (NCP) takes the unknowns to be \((\mu,\tau,\tilde\theta_1,\dots, \tilde\theta_J)\) with standardized effects \[ \tilde\theta_j \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\ \text{ a priori}, \qquad \theta_j := \mu + \tau\,\tilde\theta_j, \tag{24.2}\] and the data terms are written as functions of the deterministic transform \(\mu+\tau\tilde\theta_j\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Both parameterizations describe the identical statistical model and give the identical answer; they differ only in what the sampler is asked to move around in. The centered form (CP) samples the effects \(\theta_j\) directly. The non-centered form (NCP) samples standardized shocks \(\tilde\theta_j\) of fixed unit scale and reconstructs \(\theta_j=\mu+\tau\tilde\theta_j\) afterward, so the width of the effect is carried by \(\tau\) rather than baked into the coordinate. The point of the section is that this cosmetic-looking choice decides whether HMC sees a funnel or a cylinder. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The two are the same model: Equation 24.2 is an invertible change of variables that carries the standard normal prior on \(\tilde\theta_j\) to the \(\mathcal N(\mu,\tau^2)\) prior on \(\theta_j\). We record this precisely, because the equivalence is what licenses choosing between them on purely computational grounds.

Lemma 24.1 (The two parameterizations define one posterior) Fix \((\mu,\tau)\) with \(\tau>0\). The map \(T_{\mu,\tau}:\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}\mapsto\boldsymbol\theta\) defined by \(\theta_j=\mu+\tau\tilde\theta_j\) is a diffeomorphism of \(\mathbb R^J\) with constant Jacobian determinant \(\tau^{J}\). The CP joint density in \((\mu,\tau,\boldsymbol\theta)\) and the NCP joint density in \((\mu,\tau,\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta})\) are related by this change of variables, and the two analyses return identical posteriors for every quantity \(h(\mu,\tau,\boldsymbol\theta)\).

Proof. For fixed \((\mu,\tau)\) the map is affine with linear part \(\tau I_J\), hence a bijection of \(\mathbb R^J\) with Jacobian matrix \(\tau I_J\) and determinant \(\tau^J\neq 0\); it is its own smooth inverse \(\tilde\theta_j=(\theta_j-\mu)/\tau\), so it is a diffeomorphism. Writing \(\varphi\) for the standard normal density, the NCP prior on \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}\) is \(\prod_j\varphi(\tilde\theta_j)\). Pushing it forward through \(T_{\mu,\tau}\) by the change-of-variables formula gives, at \(\boldsymbol\theta= T_{\mu,\tau}(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta})\), \[ \prod_{j}\varphi(\tilde\theta_j)\cdot\left|\det \tfrac{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}}{\partial \boldsymbol\theta}\right| =\prod_{j}\varphi\!\Big(\tfrac{\theta_j-\mu}{\tau}\Big)\cdot\tau^{-J} =\prod_{j}\mathcal N(\theta_j;\mu,\tau^2), \] which is exactly the CP prior. Because the likelihood is written in both as a function of the same numbers \(\theta_j=\mu+\tau\tilde\theta_j\), and the priors on \((\mu,\tau)\) are shared, the joint densities agree under the transform. A diffeomorphic reparameterization leaves all integrals, hence all posterior expectations and quantiles of any \(h\), invariant. \(\square\)

Remark. What the proof is saying, in words. A diffeomorphism is just a change of variables that is smooth and smoothly invertible, so no information is lost or created by switching between \(\boldsymbol\theta\) and \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}\). The change-of-variables formula is the multivariable version of the \(u\)-substitution from calculus: when you rewrite a density in new coordinates you must multiply by the Jacobian factor (here the constant \(\tau^{-J}\)) to keep total probability equal to one. Because that bookkeeping is exact, every probability, mean, and credible interval is identical in the two coordinates. This is the license we need: we may pick the parameterization purely for how well the sampler moves, with no effect on the reported treatment contrast. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

If the posteriors are identical, why does the choice matter? Because HMC does not sample the posterior directly; it simulates Hamiltonian trajectories whose stability and step size are dictated by the local curvature of the negative log density, and curvature is not invariant under reparameterization. We make this quantitative. Recall from Definition 8.9 that HMC sets \(U(\boldsymbol\xi)=-\log\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\) (the “potential energy”) and advances the state by the leapfrog integrator with a single global step size \(\epsilon\). The behavior of the leapfrog integrator on a quadratic potential is the whole story, and it is governed by one number.

To keep the section self-contained, recall the leapfrog integrator explicitly. It advances a position \(x\) and its momentum \(r\) by a step of size \(\epsilon\) in three substeps: a half-kick of the momentum by the gradient, \(r^{+}=r-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}U'(x)\); a full drift of the position, \(x'=x+\epsilon r^{+}\); and a second half-kick, \(r'=r^{+}-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}U'(x')\). The momentum \(r\) is the auxiliary “velocity” HMC gives the puck, and \(\epsilon\) is the same step size the sampler tunes in warmup. The next lemma analyzes this map on the simplest possible landscape, a parabola, because near any smooth mode the landscape is a parabola to second order; the one inequality it produces, the largest step the integrator can survive, is what governs both the funnel pathology and its non-centered cure. That is why a page on a harmonic oscillator earns its place: it is the single fact the entire section turns on.

Lemma 24.2 (Linear stability of the leapfrog integrator) Let \(U(x)=\tfrac12\omega^2 x^2\) be a one-dimensional harmonic potential with frequency \(\omega>0\) and unit mass. One leapfrog step of size \(\epsilon\) (as in Definition 8.9) acts on the state \((x,r)\) as the linear map \((x',r')^\top=A(x,r)^\top\) with \[ A=\begin{pmatrix} 1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2\omega^2 & \epsilon \\[2pt] -\epsilon\omega^2\big(1-\tfrac14\epsilon^2\omega^2\big) & 1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2\omega^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A=1 . \tag{24.3}\] The iterates \(A^n\) remain bounded (the integrator is stable) if and only if \(\epsilon\omega\le 2\); for \(\epsilon\omega>2\) they grow geometrically, and the simulated trajectory diverges.

Proof. With \(U'(x)=\omega^2 x\), the three leapfrog substeps of Definition 8.9 read \(r^{+}=r-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\omega^2 x\), then \(x'=x+\epsilon r^{+}=(1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2\omega^2)x+ \epsilon r\), then \(r'=r^{+}-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\omega^2 x'\). Substituting \(x'\) into the last line, \[ r'=r-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\omega^2 x-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\omega^2\big[(1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2\omega^2)x+\epsilon r\big] =\big(1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2\omega^2\big)r-\epsilon\omega^2\big(1-\tfrac14\epsilon^2\omega^2\big)x, \] which is Equation 24.3. Its determinant is \((1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2\omega^2)^2+\epsilon\cdot\epsilon\omega^2(1-\tfrac14\epsilon^2\omega^2) =1-\epsilon^2\omega^2+\tfrac14\epsilon^4\omega^4+\epsilon^2\omega^2-\tfrac14\epsilon^4\omega^4=1\), confirming the volume preservation of Definition 8.9. Writing \(s=\epsilon\omega\), the trace is \(2-s^2\), so the eigenvalues of \(A\) solve \(\lambda^2-(2-s^2)\lambda+1=0\) with discriminant \((2-s^2)^2-4=s^2(s^2-4)\). If \(s<2\) the discriminant is negative, the eigenvalues are complex conjugates, and since their product is \(\det A=1\) they have modulus \(1\); the powers \(A^n\) are then conjugate to a rotation and stay bounded. If \(s>2\) the discriminant is positive, the eigenvalues are real with product \(1\), so one exceeds \(1\) in modulus and \(A^n\) grows like that eigenvalue to the \(n\); the trajectory diverges. At \(s=2\) the matrix has a defective double eigenvalue \(-1\) and \(\|A^n\|\) grows linearly. Hence stability holds exactly for \(\epsilon\omega\le 2\). \(\square\)

The frequency \(\omega\) in Lemma 24.2 is \(\sqrt{U''(x)}\), the square root of the local curvature of the potential. The lemma therefore says: the largest usable leapfrog step at a point is \(\epsilon\lesssim 2/\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}\), where \(\lambda_{\max}\) is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian \(\nabla^2 U\) there. A single global \(\epsilon\) can be efficient only if this curvature is roughly constant across the region the chain must visit. The next lemma computes the curvature in the two parameterizations and exposes the funnel.

Lemma 24.3 (Funnel curvature of the centered hierarchy) Consider Equation 24.1 with a single weakly-informed effect \(\theta_j\) whose data term contributes curvature \(c_j\ge 0\) in \(\theta_j\) (so \(c_j\) is the negative second derivative of the data log-likelihood in \(\theta_j\), a measure of how firmly the data pin down \(\theta_j\); when the data are weakly informative the likelihood is nearly flat in \(\theta_j\), its second derivative is near zero, and \(c_j\to 0\), the prior-dominated limit). Holding \((\mu,\tau)\) fixed:

  1. In the centered parameterization the curvature of \(U\) in the \(\theta_j\) direction is \[ \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial\theta_j^2}=\frac{1}{\tau^2}+c_j . \tag{24.4}\]
  2. In the non-centered parameterization the curvature of \(U\) in the \(\tilde\theta_j\) direction is \[ \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial\tilde\theta_j^2}=1+\tau^2 c_j . \tag{24.5}\]

Proof. The potential is \(U=-\log\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)=-\log(\text{prior})-\log(\text{likelihood})+ \text{const}\). In CP the prior term involving \(\theta_j\) is \(-\log\mathcal N(\theta_j;\mu,\tau^2)= \frac{(\theta_j-\mu)^2}{2\tau^2}+\text{const}\), whose second derivative in \(\theta_j\) is \(1/\tau^2\); the data term adds \(c_j\) by definition, giving Equation 24.4. In NCP the prior term involving \(\tilde\theta_j\) is \(-\log\mathcal N(\tilde\theta_j;0,1)=\tfrac12\tilde\theta_j^2+\text{const}\), second derivative \(1\); the data term is the same function of \(\theta_j=\mu+\tau\tilde\theta_j\), and by the chain rule its second derivative in \(\tilde\theta_j\) is \((\partial\theta_j/\partial\tilde\theta_j)^2\) times its second derivative in \(\theta_j\), that is \(\tau^2 c_j\). Summing gives Equation 24.5. \(\square\)

Theorem 24.1 (Why the parameterization changes HMC geometry) In the prior-dominated regime \(c_j\to 0\) (a study or contrast about which the data are weakly informative):

  1. (Centered funnels.) The CP curvature Equation 24.4 is \(1/\tau^2\), so by Lemma 24.2 the largest stable leapfrog step in the \(\theta_j\) direction is \(\epsilon_{\max}(\tau)=2\tau\), which tends to \(0\) as \(\tau\to 0\). The posterior of \((\theta_j,\log\tau)\) is a “funnel,” narrowing without bound as \(\tau\downarrow 0\); no single global \(\epsilon\) is both stable in the neck of the funnel and efficient in its mouth.
  2. (Non-centered uniformizes.) The NCP curvature Equation 24.5 is \(1\), independent of \(\tau\), so the largest stable step is \(\epsilon_{\max}=2\) for every \(\tau\). The funnel is removed and a single step size suffices.

In the data-dominated regime \(\tau^2 c_j\gg 1\) (many informative studies, well-identified \(\tau\)) the roles reverse: NCP couples \(\tilde\theta_j\) to \(\tau\) through the data term Equation 24.5 and can mix worse than CP, whose curvature Equation 24.4 is then the well-conditioned \(c_j\). The practical rule is to use NCP when \(\tau\) is weakly identified (few studies) and CP when it is strongly identified.

Proof. Both claims are immediate from substituting the limiting curvatures of Lemma 24.3 into the stability threshold \(\epsilon_{\max}=2/\omega=2/\sqrt{\text{curvature}}\) of Lemma 24.2. For (1), curvature \(1/\tau^2\) gives \(\omega=1/\tau\) and \(\epsilon_{\max}=2\tau\to 0\); because \(\tau\) itself ranges over an interval reaching toward \(0\) under the posterior, the step size that is stable at the smallest visited \(\tau\) is far smaller than the step size that is efficient at typical \(\tau\), and HMC with one \(\epsilon\) must compromise. The Metropolis correction of Theorem 8.9 guards correctness by rejecting the divergent proposals that an over-large step produces in the neck, but at the cost of a collapse in acceptance and in distance traveled, which is observed in practice as “divergent transitions.” For (2), curvature \(1\) gives \(\epsilon_{\max}=2\) for all \(\tau\), so stability is uniform over the funnel. The reversal in the data-dominated regime is the same computation read in the other direction: when \(\tau^2 c_j\gg 1\) the NCP curvature Equation 24.5 grows with \(\tau\) while the CP curvature Equation 24.4 approaches the constant \(c_j\), so CP is the better-conditioned coordinate. \(\square\)

The theorem is the rigorous version of “change coordinates so the funnel becomes a cylinder,” and it also warns that the cure can become the disease when the data are strong. 24.5 The Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction statistic

Reparameterization makes the chains mix; diagnostics tell us whether they have. The first question is convergence: have the chains forgotten their starting points and settled to a common stationary distribution? The standard answer compares the spread of several independently started chains to the spread within each. If the chains agree, the between-chain spread is no larger than the within; if they have not converged, the between-chain spread is inflated. The Gelman-Rubin statistic makes this a number. This is catalogue result I.2, which we derive in full.

The bottom line first. The statistic is \(\widehat R=\sqrt{\widehat V/W}\), the ratio of a pooled, all-chains-together estimate of the posterior spread \(\widehat V\) to the average within-chain spread \(W\). Read it as a single question: are the separate chains telling the same posterior story? If they are, the two spreads agree and \(\widehat R\) sits at \(1\); if the chains have settled in different places or are still drifting, the pooled spread is inflated and \(\widehat R\) rises above \(1\). The working rule is to require \(\widehat R\le 1.01\) for every reported quantity. In the running example that means checking it not only on the headline contrast \(d_{AC}\) but on \(\tau\), the study baselines \(\mu_{AB},\mu_{BC}\), the effect modifier \(\boldsymbol\beta_2\), and the transformed marginal probabilities you report; the heterogeneity \(\tau\) is usually the last to converge and the one to watch. The rest of this section proves why this ratio has exactly that behavior; a reader who trusts the rule can skim to Section 24.6 on a first pass.

Throughout this section we have \(M\ge 2\) chains, each of post-warmup length \(N\), with draws \(\theta^{(m,t)}\) for chain \(m=1,\dots,M\) and iteration \(t=1,\dots,N\), of a scalar quantity \(\theta=h(\boldsymbol\xi)\).

Definition 24.2 (Within-chain, between-chain, and pooled variance estimators) With chain means \(\bar\theta^{(m)}=\frac1N\sum_t\theta^{(m,t)}\) and grand mean \(\bar{\bar\theta}=\frac1M\sum_m\bar\theta^{(m)}\), define \[ W=\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}s_m^2,\quad s_m^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\theta^{(m,t)}-\bar\theta^{(m)} \big)^2;\qquad B=\frac{N}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\big(\bar\theta^{(m)}-\bar{\bar\theta}\big)^2 ; \tag{24.6}\] the within-chain variance \(W\) (the average of the per-chain sample variances) and the between-chain variance \(B\) (the sample variance of the chain means, scaled by \(N\)). The pooled marginal-variance estimator is \[ \widehat V=\Big(1-\tfrac1N\Big)W+\tfrac1N B . \tag{24.7}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. \(W\) asks how much a single chain wiggles around its own average; \(B\) asks how far the chain averages sit from one another. If the chains have converged to the same distribution these measure the same spread, so their combination \(\widehat V\) neither over- nor under-states the posterior variance. If the chains disagree, \(B\) is inflated by the disagreement while \(W\) is not, and the ratio \(\widehat V/W\) climbs above \(1\). The scaling by \(N\) in \(B\) is what puts the between-chain and within-chain pieces on the same footing. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The rationale for these particular combinations is not obvious from the formulas. We supply it with two exact expectation identities, valid under an explicit idealization of what “converged” means, and then read off the behavior of the statistic.

Lemma 24.4 (Expectation of the within-chain variance) Suppose each chain is second-order stationary, meaning its mean is constant along the chain and the covariance between two draws depends only on how many iterations separate them, not on where in the chain they sit, with marginal variance \(\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(\theta)\), and let \(v_N=\mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta^{(m)})\) be the variance of a single chain mean. Then \(\mathbb E[s_m^2]= \frac{N}{N-1}(\sigma^2-v_N)\), and hence \[ \mathbb E[W]=\frac{N}{N-1}\big(\sigma^2-v_N\big). \tag{24.8}\]

Proof. Let \(m_0=\mathbb E[\theta^{(m,t)}]\), constant in \(t\) by stationarity. Using \(\sum_t(\theta^{(m,t)}-\bar\theta^{(m)})^2=\sum_t\theta^{(m,t)2}-N\bar\theta^{(m)2}\) and taking expectations, \[ \mathbb E\!\Big[\textstyle\sum_t(\theta^{(m,t)}-\bar\theta^{(m)})^2\Big] =\sum_t\big(\sigma^2+m_0^2\big)-N\big(v_N+m_0^2\big) =N\sigma^2-Nv_N , \] because \(\mathbb E[\theta^{(m,t)2}]=\mathrm{Var}(\theta^{(m,t)})+m_0^2=\sigma^2+m_0^2\) and \(\mathbb E[\bar\theta^{(m)2}]=v_N+m_0^2\). Dividing by \(N-1\) gives \(\mathbb E[s_m^2]=\frac{N}{N-1} (\sigma^2-v_N)\), and averaging over the \(M\) chains gives Equation 24.8. \(\square\)

Lemma 24.5 (Expectation of the between-chain variance) Suppose in addition that the \(M\) chains are mutually independent and share the common stationary distribution (in particular a common mean). Then \[ \mathbb E[B]=N v_N . \tag{24.9}\]

Proof. Under the stated assumptions the chain means \(\bar\theta^{(1)},\dots,\bar\theta^{(M)}\) are independent and identically distributed with common variance \(v_N\). The quantity \(\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_m(\bar\theta^{(m)}- \bar{\bar\theta})^2\) is the ordinary sample variance of these \(M\) numbers, which is an unbiased estimator of their common variance \(v_N\). Multiplying by \(N\), as in the definition Equation 24.6 of \(B\), gives \(\mathbb E[B]=Nv_N\). \(\square\)

Theorem 24.2 (Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction) Define the potential scale reduction factor \[ \widehat R=\sqrt{\widehat V/W}. \tag{24.10}\]

  1. (Unbiasedness of \(\widehat V\) at stationarity.) Under the idealization of Lemma 24.4 and Lemma 24.5 (independent chains sharing one stationary law of finite variance \(\sigma^2\)), the pooled estimator is exactly unbiased for the marginal posterior variance, \[ \mathbb E[\widehat V]=\sigma^2 , \] for every \(N\) and every within-chain autocorrelation structure.

  2. (One-sided bias and the limit.) For chains with non-negative autocorrelations, \(\mathbb E[\widehat V]\ge\mathbb E[W]\), with \[ \mathbb E[\widehat V]-\mathbb E[W]=\frac{N v_N-\sigma^2}{N-1}\ge 0 , \tag{24.11}\] so \(\widehat R\) is centered at or above \(1\); and if the chains are ergodic, so that \(v_N\to 0\) and \(Nv_N\to\sigma^2\big(1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k\big)\) as \(N\to\infty\), then \(\mathbb E[W]\to\sigma^2\) and \(\widehat R\xrightarrow{p}1\).

  3. (Detection of non-convergence.) If the chains are stationary but with different means \(m_1,\dots,m_M\) (over-dispersed starting points not yet forgotten, or distinct posterior modes), then \[ \mathbb E[\widehat V]=\sigma^2+\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m}\big(m_m-\bar m\big)^2, \qquad \bar m=\tfrac1M\textstyle\sum_m m_m , \] while \(\mathbb E[W]\) is unchanged at \(\frac{N}{N-1}(\sigma^2-v_N)\); the extra between-mean dispersion inflates \(\widehat V/W\) above \(1\) and does not vanish as \(N\to\infty\) unless the means coincide.

Proof. Part 1. By Equation 24.7 and linearity of expectation, \[ \mathbb E[\widehat V]=\Big(1-\tfrac1N\Big)\mathbb E[W]+\tfrac1N\mathbb E[B] =\frac{N-1}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{N-1}\big(\sigma^2-v_N\big)+\frac1N\cdot N v_N =\big(\sigma^2-v_N\big)+v_N=\sigma^2, \] using Equation 24.8 and Equation 24.9. The cancellation is exact and uses no assumption on the autocorrelations beyond second-order stationarity.

Part 2. Subtracting Equation 24.8 from \(\mathbb E[\widehat V]=\sigma^2\), \[ \mathbb E[\widehat V]-\mathbb E[W]=\sigma^2-\frac{N}{N-1}\big(\sigma^2-v_N\big) =\frac{(N-1)\sigma^2-N\sigma^2+Nv_N}{N-1}=\frac{Nv_N-\sigma^2}{N-1}. \] Now \(v_N=\mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta^{(m)})=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-\tfrac kN)\rho_k\big)\) by the variance-of-the-mean identity Proposition 24.1 (proved independently in Section 24.6, using nothing from this section, so there is no circular dependence), so \(Nv_N=\sigma^2\big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-\tfrac kN)\rho_k\big)\ge \sigma^2\) whenever every \(\rho_k\ge 0\); the gap Equation 24.11 is therefore non-negative, and since \(\widehat V\) and \(W\) are non-negative this gives \(\mathbb E[\widehat V]\ge\mathbb E[W]\), so \(\widehat R\) is centered at or above \(1\). For the limit, ergodicity makes \(v_N\to 0\), so \(\mathbb E[\widehat V]=\sigma^2\) forces \(\mathbb E[W]=\sigma^2-\frac{N-1}{N}^{-1}\!\cdots\to\sigma^2\) as well (equivalently, the gap Equation 24.11 is \(\frac{\sigma^2\cdot 2\sum_k(1-k/N)\rho_k}{N-1}\to 0\) because the bracket converges to the finite constant \(\sum_k\rho_k\)); both \(\widehat V\) and \(W\) converge to \(\sigma^2\) and their ratio to \(1\).

Part 3. When the chains have different means, the chain means \(\bar\theta^{(m)}\) are independent with \(\mathbb E[\bar\theta^{(m)}]=m_m\) and variance \(v_N\). The sample variance of independent variables with unequal means decomposes into the average variance plus the sample variance of the means: taking expectations in \(\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_m(\bar\theta^{(m)}-\bar{\bar\theta})^2\) gives \(v_N+\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_m (m_m-\bar m)^2\). Hence \(\mathbb E[B]=Nv_N+\frac{N}{M-1}\sum_m(m_m-\bar m)^2\), and \[ \mathbb E[\widehat V]=\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb E[W]+\frac1N\mathbb E[B] =\sigma^2+\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_m\big(m_m-\bar m\big)^2 . \] The within-chain expectation Equation 24.8 is untouched because it never references the cross-chain means. The extra term is a fixed positive constant independent of \(N\), so \(\widehat V/W\) stays bounded away from \(1\) as \(N\) grows, which is the diagnostic firing. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters, and the three ways \(\widehat R\) fails. For an indirect comparison, a large \(\widehat R\) on the contrast \(d_{AC}\) means the estimate is not yet stable: rerun the analysis with a different seed and you could get a materially different number, which is indefensible in a reimbursement submission. Part 3 of the theorem is the precise reason, and it fires in three recognizable patterns. (i) Different modes: the chains settle in genuinely different places, for example a bimodal posterior for an effect modifier, so the chain means \(m_m\) differ and the extra term in Part 3 never vanishes. (ii) Slow drift: a chain has not reached the typical set and is still trending, which split-\(\widehat R\) (below) is designed to catch by comparing each chain’s two halves. (iii) One rogue chain: a single chain stuck in the funnel neck while the others mix, dragging one \(m_m\) away from the rest. In all three the action is the same: do not read the posterior yet. Diagnose which parameter is bad and treat the cause, usually more warmup, the non-centered parameterization of Section 24.4, or a tighter prior on \(\tau\), rather than simply running longer. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reading \(\widehat R\), and modern refinements. Part 1 is the classical fact that \(\widehat V\) overestimates the posterior variance when the chains start over-dispersed (Part 3) and is unbiased once they have mixed (Part 1), while \(W\) underestimates it until each chain has explored the whole support (Part 2); \(\widehat R\) compresses both failures into a single number that descends to \(1\). The widely used threshold is \(\widehat R\le 1.01\) for every reported quantity. Two refinements are now standard: split- \(\widehat R\) divides each chain into two halves and treats them as separate chains, so that within-chain non-stationarity (a slow trend) inflates the between-chain term and is detected; and rank-normalized \(\widehat R\) applies the statistic to the normal scores of the pooled ranks, which makes it well defined for heavy-tailed or infinite-variance targets where \(\sigma^2\) in Theorem 24.2 would not exist. Both are due to a modern reassessment of the diagnostic and are the defaults in current software. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This development corresponds to I.2 in the proof catalogue.

24.6 Effective sample size for autocorrelated chains

A chain can be perfectly converged (\(\widehat R=1\)) and still nearly useless if its draws are highly correlated, because correlated draws carry redundant information. The effective sample size quantifies exactly how many independent draws the chain is worth for estimating a posterior expectation. This is catalogue result I.3. The development rests on one exact variance identity for the mean of a stationary sequence, which we also used in Theorem 24.2.

Proposition 24.1 (Variance of the mean of a stationary chain) Let \(\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(N)}\) be second-order stationary with variance \(\sigma^2\) and lag-\(k\) autocorrelation \(\rho_k=\mathrm{Corr}(\theta^{(t)},\theta^{(t+k)})\). Then \[ \mathrm{Var}\big(\bar\theta_N\big)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\Big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\Big(1-\frac kN\Big)\rho_k \Big),\qquad \bar\theta_N=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^N\theta^{(t)} . \tag{24.12}\] If moreover \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty|\rho_k|<\infty\), then \(N\,\mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta_N)\to\sigma^2\, \tau_{\mathrm{int}}\) as \(N\to\infty\), where \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=1+2\sum_{k=1}^\infty\rho_k\) is the integrated autocorrelation time.

Proof. Expanding the variance of the sum and using stationarity, \(\mathrm{Cov}(\theta^{(s)},\theta^{(t)})= \sigma^2\rho_{|s-t|}\), so \[ \mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta_N)=\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{s=1}^N\sum_{t=1}^N\sigma^2\rho_{|s-t|} =\frac{\sigma^2}{N^2}\Big(N+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(N-k)\rho_k\Big), \] because the lag \(|s-t|=0\) occurs for the \(N\) diagonal pairs and each lag \(k\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}\) occurs for \(2(N-k)\) ordered pairs. Factoring \(N\) out of the bracket gives Equation 24.12. For the limit, each summand \((1-\tfrac kN)\rho_k\to\rho_k\) pointwise as \(N\to\infty\) and is dominated by \(|\rho_k|\), which is summable by hypothesis; dominated convergence gives \(\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-\tfrac kN)\rho_k\to\sum_{k\ge1} \rho_k\), hence \(N\,\mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta_N)=\sigma^2(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-\tfrac kN)\rho_k)\to\sigma^2 \tau_{\mathrm{int}}\). \(\square\)

Definition 24.3 (Effective sample size of an MCMC chain) For a second-order stationary chain with integrated autocorrelation time \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=1+2\sum_{k \ge1}\rho_k\), the effective sample size for the posterior mean is \[ N_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{N}{\tau_{\mathrm{int}}}=\frac{N}{1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_k}. \tag{24.13}\] With \(M\) chains of length \(N\) the total is \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=MN/\tau_{\mathrm{int}}\). The ratio \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}/N\in(0,1]\) is the chain’s efficiency. In practice the infinite sum is truncated at the first lag where the estimated autocorrelations cease to be reliably positive (for example by Geyer’s initial-monotone-sequence rule), because sample autocorrelations at large lags are dominated by noise. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) answers “how many truly independent draws is this correlated chain worth?” A run of \(4000\) draws with \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=4000\) is as good as \(4000\) coin flips; one with \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=200\) carries the information of only \(200\). The integrated autocorrelation time \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}\) is the exchange rate: each draw is worth \(1/\tau_{\mathrm{int}}\) of an independent draw. A low \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) does not bias the estimate; it makes it noisy, which for an ITC shows up as jittery credible-interval endpoints on the contrast. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The definition is justified by the property that gives it its name: \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the number of independent draws that would estimate the posterior mean with the same Monte Carlo variance.

Corollary 24.1 (\(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) equalizes Monte Carlo variance) Under the summability hypothesis of Proposition 24.1, the asymptotic Monte Carlo variance of the chain mean is \[ \mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta_N)\sim\frac{\sigma^2}{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}\qquad(N\to\infty), \] the same as the variance \(\sigma^2/n\) of a mean of \(n=N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) independent draws. The Monte Carlo standard error of \(\bar\theta_N\) is therefore \(\sigma/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}\).

Proof. By Proposition 24.1, \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta_N)\sim\sigma^2\tau_{\mathrm{int}}/N=\sigma^2/(N/ \tau_{\mathrm{int}})=\sigma^2/N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) from the definition Equation 24.13. Equating to the iid variance \(\sigma^2/n\) gives \(n=N_{\mathrm{eff}}\), and the standard error is the square root. \(\square\)

Example 24.1 (Effective sample size of an AR(1) chain) The first-order autoregressive process \(\theta^{(t)}=\phi\,\theta^{(t-1)}+\varepsilon_t\) with \(|\phi|<1\) and white-noise \(\varepsilon_t\) is the canonical model for a sticky MCMC chain; its lag-\(k\) autocorrelation is \(\rho_k=\phi^{k}\). Summing the geometric series, \[ \tau_{\mathrm{int}}=1+2\sum_{k=1}^\infty\phi^k=1+\frac{2\phi}{1-\phi}=\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}, \qquad N_{\mathrm{eff}}=N\,\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}. \] A mildly autocorrelated chain with \(\phi=\tfrac13\) has \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=2\) and efficiency \(\tfrac12\): \(N=4000\) draws are worth \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=2000\). A sticky chain with \(\phi=0.9\) has \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=19\) and efficiency \(1/19\): the same \(4000\) draws are worth only \(N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 210\), a twentyfold loss. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

In the running example a small \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) on \(d_{AC}\) warns that its posterior mean is imprecisely pinned down by Monte Carlo, and a small tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (defined next) warns specifically that the \(95\%\) credible-interval endpoints reported to the payer are noisy.

Remark. Bulk and tail effective sample size. The estimate Equation 24.13 targets the posterior mean. Posterior quantiles, which determine credible intervals, are governed by the autocorrelation of indicator functions \(\mathbf 1\{\theta^{(t)}\le q\}\) rather than of \(\theta^{(t)}\) itself, and can mix far worse, especially deep in the tails. Current software therefore reports a bulk \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (from the rank-normalized draws, for the center of the distribution) and a tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (the minimum of the effective sizes for the \(5\%\) and \(95\%\) quantile indicators). A common standard is bulk and tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge 400\) across chains for inference, with larger tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) demanded when extreme quantiles are reported. A small tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) beside a healthy bulk \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the signature of a chain that estimates the posterior mean well but its interval endpoints poorly. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

24.7 Monitoring the numerical-integration error

We turn to the error peculiar to ML-NMR. Each aggregate-data study contributes to the likelihood through an integral of the individual-level integrand \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf x))\) over the study’s covariate distribution (the aggregation theorem, Theorem 20.1), and that integral is replaced in computation by a QMC average over \(N\) points on the unit hypercube (Theorem 22.6). The sampler therefore targets not the exact posterior but its QMC approximation, and we must certify that the substitution is harmless. This is catalogue result I.4.

Write the exact aggregate-cell quantity as \(\theta=\int_{[0,1]^d}f(\mathbf u)\,d\mathbf u\), where the integrand \(f=g^{-1}\!\circ\eta_{jk}\circ\Psi\) folds the inverse link, the linear predictor, and the copula-plus-inverse-CDF transform \(\Psi\) of Chapter 22 (Theorem 22.4, Definition 22.5) into a function on the cube; write \(\widehat\theta^{(N)}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(\mathbf u_i)\) for its QMC estimate on the first \(N\) points of a Sobol’ sequence (Definition 22.3). The error is deterministic given the point set, and is controlled by the Koksma-Hlawka inequality of Chapter 22.

In words, and tied to the running network: the aggregate study \(BC\) never shows us its patients, only arm summaries, so to evaluate its likelihood we must average the individual response over the covariate mix in that arm. The transform \(\Psi\) is the recipe that turns a uniform grid point \(\mathbf u\in[0,1]^d\) into a plausible patient’s covariate vector (age, severity) drawn from the arm’s fitted distribution; \(\eta_{jk}\) then gives that synthetic patient’s linear predictor, \(g^{-1}\) converts it to an event probability, and the QMC sum averages over a grid of such patients. So \(\theta\) is the arm’s expected event probability and \(\widehat\theta^{(N)}\) is our \(N\)-patient numerical approximation to it. Two glosses for the symbols carried over from Chapter 22: the Hardy-Krause variation \(V_{\mathrm{HK}} (f)\) is a multivariable generalization of total variation, a measure of how much the integrand wiggles (its room to be mis-sampled by a finite grid), and the star discrepancy \(D_N^{*}\) measures how unevenly the \(N\) points fill the cube (small discrepancy means they cover it evenly). The Koksma-Hlawka bound below simply multiplies the two.

Remark. The error is bounded, not random. By Theorem 22.3, for \(f\) of finite Hardy-Krause variation, \[ \big|\widehat\theta^{(N)}-\theta\big|\le V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\,D_N^{*}(P_N), \tag{24.14}\] the product of the integrand’s variation and the star discrepancy \(D_N^{*}\) of the point set \(P_N\), which for a Sobol’ net is \(O\big((\log N)^d/N\big)\). Unlike the \(O(N^{-1/2})\) root-mean-square error of plain Monte Carlo (Theorem 22.2), Equation 24.14 is a worst-case bound with no probability attached. The difficulty is that \(V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\) is unknown and usually uncomputable, so Equation 24.14 certifies a rate but not a usable constant. The operational diagnostic below sidesteps it by measuring convergence empirically. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Definition 24.4 (Doubling diagnostic for integration error) Let \(\widehat\theta^{(N)}\) and \(\widehat\theta^{(N/2)}\) be the QMC estimates of an aggregate-cell quantity using the first \(N\) and the first \(N/2\) points of the same Sobol’ sequence. The estimate is declared converged at tolerance \(\epsilon\) when the relative doubling increment satisfies \[ \frac{\big|\widehat\theta^{(N)}-\widehat\theta^{(N/2)}\big|}{\big|\widehat\theta^{(N)}\big|}<\epsilon \tag{24.15}\] for every aggregate cell, for a small tolerance such as \(\epsilon=0.005\). If the criterion fails for any cell, \(N\) is doubled and the test repeated. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation, and a caution on the denominator. The rule is the numerical analyst’s oldest trick: if halving the grid barely changes the answer, the answer has stopped moving, so you can stop refining. The relative form divides by \(|\widehat\theta^{(N)}|\) so that “small” means small compared to the quantity itself. That is exactly wrong when the quantity is near zero (a rare-event probability) or naturally lives on a transformed scale that passes through zero (a log-odds or log-hazard): there the relative increment blows up for no real reason, and one should switch to an absolute tolerance on the quantity, or run the test on the probability scale where the target is bounded in \([0,1]\). In the running example the \(BC\) arm’s event probability sits comfortably away from \(0\) and \(1\), so the relative test is safe; a sparse-outcome cell would call for the absolute version. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The diagnostic measures the observable increment \(\widehat\theta^{(N)}-\widehat\theta^{(N/2)}\), whereas what we care about is the unobservable error \(\widehat\theta^{(N)}-\theta\). The following proposition shows that, whenever the error decays at a definite rate, the observable increment controls the unobservable error up to a known constant, which is what makes Equation 24.15 a valid stopping rule.

Proposition 24.2 (The increment bounds the error under geometric decay) Write \(e_N=\widehat\theta^{(N)}-\theta\) for the error and \(\Delta_N=\widehat\theta^{(N)}- \widehat\theta^{(N/2)}=e_N-e_{N/2}\) for the observable increment. Suppose there is a contraction factor \(\rho\in[0,1)\) with \(|e_N|\le\rho\,|e_{N/2}|\) along the doubling sequence (a definite convergence rate). Then \[ |e_{N/2}|\le\frac{|\Delta_N|}{1-\rho}\qquad\text{and}\qquad |e_N|\le\frac{\rho\,|\Delta_N|}{1-\rho}. \tag{24.16}\] Consequently, passing the relative test Equation 24.15 certifies a relative integration error of at most \(\tfrac{\rho}{1-\rho}\,\epsilon\) in \(\widehat\theta^{(N)}\), to leading order.

Proof. From \(\Delta_N=e_N-e_{N/2}\) and the triangle inequality, \(|e_{N/2}|=|e_N-\Delta_N|\le|e_N|+|\Delta_N|\le \rho|e_{N/2}|+|\Delta_N|\). Rearranging, \((1-\rho)|e_{N/2}|\le|\Delta_N|\), which is the first bound in Equation 24.16 since \(\rho<1\); the second follows from \(|e_N|\le\rho|e_{N/2}|\). Dividing by \(|\theta|\approx|\widehat\theta^{(N)}|\) and using Equation 24.15, the relative error is at most \(\tfrac{\rho}{1-\rho}\,\epsilon\) to leading order in the small quantities. \(\square\)

Remark. What \(\rho\) is for QMC and for Monte Carlo. For a QMC rule with error envelope \(a_N=(\log N)^d/N\), the ratio of successive errors is \(a_N/a_{N/2}=\tfrac12\cdot\big(\tfrac{\log N}{\log N-\log 2}\big)^{d}\to \tfrac12\) as \(N\to\infty\), so \(\rho\approx\tfrac12\) in the regime where the logarithmic factor is slowly varying, and Equation 24.16 gives \(|e_N|\lesssim|\Delta_N|\): the increment is itself a good error estimate. For plain Monte Carlo, \(a_N=N^{-1/2}\) gives \(a_N/a_{N/2}=1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\), so \(\rho\approx 0.707\) and the true error can be roughly \(2.4\) times the observed increment, a reminder that the doubling rule is sharper for QMC than for Monte Carlo. The logarithmic factor means \(\rho\) is only asymptotically \(\tfrac12\); in the pre-asymptotic regime one should keep \(\epsilon\) conservative, which is why the recommended tolerances are small. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Practical grid sizes and the combined budget. Empirically, \(N\in\{64,128,256\}\) Sobol’ points suffice for ML-NMR with two to four covariates, and the reference software defaults to \(N=64\); high-dimensional covariate sets (\(d>6\)) call for \(N=256\) or for an Owen-scrambled Sobol’ rule, a randomized QMC variant whose error has better behavior on non-smooth integrands. The total error in a posterior summary is the sum of the two contributions of this chapter, \[ \text{total error}\ \approx\ \underbrace{O\big(1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}\big)}_{\text{MCMC error}} \ +\ \underbrace{O\big((\log N)^d/N\big)}_{\text{QMC error}}, \] and a credible analysis drives both below the precision required of the reported contrast. The two are controlled by different levers: longer or better-mixed chains for the first, more integration points for the second. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why not just use a huge grid? Because the QMC sum sits inside the likelihood. Every one of the millions of likelihood evaluations that HMC performs, at every leapfrog step of every draw, re-runs the \(N\)-point sum for every aggregate cell, so the wall-clock cost of the entire fit scales linearly in \(N\): doubling the grid doubles the run time. The doubling diagnostic exists precisely to find the smallest \(N\) that is accurate enough, rather than paying for a large grid everywhere out of caution. For the running network, with \(d=2\) covariates in the \(BC\) arm, \(N=64\) Sobol’ points typically pass the test at \(\epsilon=0.005\); a submission with six or more effect modifiers is where one must check the doubling rule seriously and may need \(N=256\) or an Owen-scrambled rule. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This development corresponds to I.4 in the proof catalogue.

24.8 Information criteria: DIC, WAIC, and leave-one-out cross-validation

We now turn from certifying a single fit to comparing fits. The right currency for Bayesian model comparison is out-of-sample predictive accuracy: how well would the fitted model predict a fresh observation? All three criteria of this section estimate the same target, the expected log pointwise predictive density, and the point of the section is to derive how they are related, why two of them (WAIC and LOO-CV) are nearly identical, and where the third (DIC) sits. This is catalogue result I.5.

Fix the data \(\mathbf y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)\) with a likelihood that factors across observations, \(p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol\xi)=\prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\).

Remark. What is an observation in ML-NMR? The index \(i\) ranges over whatever units the likelihood factorizes into, and in a mixed IPD-plus-AgD model these are of two kinds. For the IPD study \(AB\) each individual patient is one factor, with \(p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) a Bernoulli response probability. For the aggregate study \(BC\) the factor is one arm’s summary, with \(p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) the binomial probability of that arm’s responder count given its integrated (QMC-averaged) event probability. So \(n\) counts individual patients from \(AB\) plus arm-cells from \(BC\), not studies and not contrasts. This choice of unit is not cosmetic: WAIC’s penalty, LOO’s held-out density, PSIS’s \(\widehat k\), and the standard error of a model-comparison difference are all computed per factor, so leaving out unit \(i\) means leaving out one patient (fine-grained, low influence) or one whole aggregate arm (coarse, high influence). When you read a large \(\widehat k\) in this model, first ask whether the flagged unit is a single patient or an entire aggregate arm. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Write the per-observation log likelihood as \(\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)=\log p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) and its posterior mean and variance as \[ m_i=\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)], \qquad v_i=\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)]. \] The posterior predictive density of a held-out replicate is \(p(\,\cdot\mid\mathbf y)=\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[p(\,\cdot\mid\boldsymbol\xi)]\) (the posterior predictive distribution of Chapter 8). The expected log pointwise predictive density (elpd) is the target, \[ \mathrm{elpd}=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E_{\tilde y_i}\big[\log p(\tilde y_i\mid\mathbf y)\big], \] where \(\tilde y_i\) is a new observation from the true data-generating process at the \(i\)-th design point; larger elpd means better prediction. Its naive in-sample estimate, evaluating the predictive density at the observed data, is the log pointwise predictive density \[ \mathrm{lppd}=\sum_{i=1}^n\log p(y_i\mid\mathbf y) =\sum_{i=1}^n\log\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}\big[p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\big]. \tag{24.17}\] The lppd is optimistic, because each \(y_i\) was used both to form the posterior and to test it. The criteria differ in how they correct that optimism.

The engine of the section is one exact identity that re-expresses the leave-one-out predictive density as a posterior expectation under the full posterior, so that it can be computed from a single fit.

Lemma 24.6 (Leave-one-out importance-sampling identity) Let \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i})\) denote the posterior with the \(i\)-th observation removed. Then the leave-one-out predictive density is the posterior harmonic mean of the \(i\)-th likelihood, \[ p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=\frac{1}{\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}\big[\,1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi) \,\big]}, \tag{24.18}\] and more generally, for any integrable \(\psi\), \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i}}[\psi(\boldsymbol\xi)] =\frac{\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}\big[\psi(\boldsymbol\xi)\,r_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\big]} {\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}\big[r_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\big]}, \qquad r_i(\boldsymbol\xi)=\frac{1}{p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)} . \tag{24.19}\]

Proof. Strategy. We relate the leave-one-out posterior to the full posterior by a single importance ratio, then read off the harmonic-mean form by choosing the test function \(\psi\) cleverly. Every step is one line of Bayes’ theorem; there is no hidden machinery.

Because the likelihood factors, removing \(y_i\) divides the full likelihood by the \(i\)-th factor, so \[ \pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i}) =\frac{p(\mathbf y_{-i}\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\,\pi(\boldsymbol\xi)}{p(\mathbf y_{-i})} =\frac{p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\,\pi(\boldsymbol\xi)}{p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\,p(\mathbf y_{-i})} =\frac{p(\mathbf y)}{p(\mathbf y_{-i})}\cdot\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)}{p(y_i\mid \boldsymbol\xi)}, \] using Bayes’ theorem (Theorem 8.3 is the conjugate special case; here we use only the proportionality \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\propto p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\pi(\boldsymbol \xi)\)). Thus the importance ratio of the leave-one-out posterior to the full posterior is \(\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i})/\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)=\frac{p(\mathbf y)}{p(\mathbf y_{-i})}\,r_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\), proportional to \(r_i(\boldsymbol\xi)=1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\). For any \(\psi\), \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i}}[\psi] =\int\psi(\boldsymbol\xi)\,\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i})}{\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)}\, \pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\,d\boldsymbol\xi =\frac{p(\mathbf y)}{p(\mathbf y_{-i})}\,\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[\psi\,r_i] . \] Taking \(\psi\equiv 1\) gives \(1=\frac{p(\mathbf y)}{p(\mathbf y_{-i})}\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[r_i]\), which both pins the unknown constant and, on dividing, yields the self-normalized form Equation 24.19 (self-normalized because the denominator is estimated from the same draws, so the unknown constant \(p(\mathbf y)/p(\mathbf y_{-i})\) cancels and never has to be computed). Finally take \(\psi=p(y_i\mid\cdot)\): the numerator is \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\,r_i]=\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[1]=1\), so \[ p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i}}[p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)] =\frac{1}{\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[r_i]} =\frac{1}{\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)]}, \] which is Equation 24.18. \(\square\)

With the identity in hand we define the three criteria and prove how they relate. All are written so that lower is better, on the deviance scale (a factor of \(-2\) times a log predictive density), to match historical convention.

Definition 24.5 (DIC, WAIC, and LOO-CV) With \(D(\boldsymbol\xi)=-2\log p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) the deviance and \(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi}= \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[\boldsymbol\xi]\) the posterior mean:

  • the deviance information criterion is \(\mathrm{DIC}=D(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})+2p_{D}\) with effective parameter count \(p_{D}=\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[D(\boldsymbol\xi)]-D(\bar{\boldsymbol \xi})\);
  • the widely applicable information criterion is \(\mathrm{WAIC}=-2\big(\mathrm{lppd}-p_{\mathrm {WAIC}}\big)\) with \(p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=\sum_{i=1}^n v_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid \mathbf y}[\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)]\);
  • leave-one-out cross-validation is \(\mathrm{LOO}=-2\,\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}}\) with \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}}=\sum_{i=1}^n\log p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. All three are “fit minus a complexity penalty,” on the deviance scale where lower is better. The first term rewards fitting the data you have; the penalty (\(2p_D\), \(2p_{\mathrm {WAIC}}\), or the built-in leave-one-out of LOO) docks you for the optimism of having used each observation both to fit and to score the model. They differ only in how honestly they estimate that optimism, which the next theorem quantifies. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 24.3 (Relationships among the predictive criteria) Assume the posterior concentrates as \(n\to\infty\) in the standard regular way, so that for each \(i\) the posterior fluctuation of \(\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\) has variance \(v_i=O(1/n)\) and third cumulant \(\kappa_{3,i}=o(v_i)\). Then:

  1. (WAIC equals LOO to second order.) For each observation, \[ \log p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=\mathrm{lppd}_i-v_i+o(v_i), \tag{24.20}\] where \(\mathrm{lppd}_i=\log\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)]\). Summing, \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}}=\mathrm{lppd}-p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}+o(1)\), so \(\mathrm{WAIC}=\mathrm{LOO}+o(1)\).

  2. (DIC’s effective parameters.) For an approximately quadratic log likelihood with observed information \(\mathcal J=-\nabla^2\log p(\mathbf y\mid\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\) (the curvature of the log likelihood at the posterior mean, the sample analogue of the Fisher information of Chapter 5), \[ p_{D}=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\mathcal J\,\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\big)+o(1), \tag{24.21}\] which under a flat prior, where the Bernstein-von Mises approximation gives \(\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\approx\mathcal J^{-1}\), equals the parameter dimension \(p\) to leading order. The WAIC penalty satisfies the same trace formula, \(p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=\operatorname{tr} (\mathcal J\,\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y))+o(1)\), so \(p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=p_{D}+o(1)\) and all three criteria agree to leading order.

  3. (Parameterization.) WAIC and LOO depend on \(\boldsymbol\xi\) only through the pointwise densities \(p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) and are invariant under reparameterization of \(\boldsymbol\xi\); DIC depends on the posterior mean \(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi}\) through \(D(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\) and is not.

Proof. Strategy and vocabulary. The whole of Part 1 is one Taylor expansion of a cumulant generating function: for a random quantity \(Z\), this is the function \(K(t)=\log\mathbb E[e^{tZ}]\) whose derivatives at \(t=0\) are the cumulants of \(Z\) (the first cumulant is the mean, the second the variance, the third \(\kappa_3\) a measure of skewness). The one trick is that the two predictive densities we must compare turn out to be exactly \(K\) evaluated at \(t=+1\) and at \(t=-1\), so subtracting two Taylor series does the work.

Part 1. Fix \(i\) and let \(K_i(t)=\log\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[e^{t\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)}]\) be the cumulant generating function of \(\ell_i\) under the posterior, with \(K_i(0)=0\), \(K_i'(0)=m_i\), \(K_i''(0)=v_i\), and third derivative the third cumulant \(\kappa_{3,i}\). By Taylor expansion, \[ K_i(t)=m_i t+\tfrac12 v_i t^2+\tfrac16\kappa_{3,i}t^3+\cdots . \] Two evaluations give the two predictive densities. First, \(\mathrm{lppd}_i=\log\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi \mid\mathbf y}[p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)]=\log\mathbb E[e^{\ell_i}]=K_i(1)=m_i+\tfrac12 v_i+O(\kappa_{3,i})\). Second, by the leave-one-out identity Equation 24.18, \[ \log p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=-\log\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}\big[e^{-\ell_i}\big] =-K_i(-1)=-\big(-m_i+\tfrac12 v_i-\tfrac16\kappa_{3,i}+\cdots\big)=m_i-\tfrac12 v_i+O(\kappa_{3,i}). \] Subtracting, \(\mathrm{lppd}_i-\log p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=v_i+O(\kappa_{3,i})=v_i+o(v_i)\) under the hypothesis \(\kappa_{3,i}=o(v_i)\), which rearranges to Equation 24.20. Summing over \(i\) and recognizing \(\sum_i v_i=p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}\) gives \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}}=\mathrm{lppd}- p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}+o(1)\); multiplying by \(-2\), \(\mathrm{LOO}=\mathrm{WAIC}+o(1)\).

Part 2. Write \(\ell(\boldsymbol\xi)=\log p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) and expand to second order about the posterior mean \(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi}\), \[ \ell(\boldsymbol\xi)=\ell(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})+\nabla\ell(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})^\top(\boldsymbol\xi- \bar{\boldsymbol\xi})-\tfrac12(\boldsymbol\xi-\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})^\top\mathcal J\,(\boldsymbol\xi- \bar{\boldsymbol\xi})+\cdots, \] with \(\mathcal J=-\nabla^2\ell(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\). Taking the posterior expectation and using \(\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\xi-\bar{\boldsymbol\xi}]=\mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbb E[(\boldsymbol\xi-\bar{\boldsymbol \xi})(\boldsymbol\xi-\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})^\top]=\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\), \[ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y}[\ell(\boldsymbol\xi)]=\ell(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})-\tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}\!\big(\mathcal J\,\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\big)+o(1). \] Since \(p_D=\mathbb E[D(\boldsymbol\xi)]-D(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})=-2\big(\mathbb E[\ell(\boldsymbol\xi)]- \ell(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\big)\), substituting gives Equation 24.21. Under a flat prior the posterior is approximately \(\mathcal N(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi},\mathcal J^{-1})\) (Bernstein-von Mises), so \(\mathrm{Var} (\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\approx\mathcal J^{-1}\) and \(p_D\approx\operatorname{tr}(I_p)=p\). For the WAIC penalty, linearize each \(\ell_i\) about \(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi}\): \(\ell_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\approx\ell_i (\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})+\nabla\ell_i(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})^\top(\boldsymbol\xi-\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\), so \(v_i=\mathrm{Var}[\ell_i]\approx\nabla\ell_i^\top\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\nabla\ell_i\) and \[ p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=\sum_i v_i\approx\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y) \sum_i\nabla\ell_i\nabla\ell_i^\top\Big)=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\mathcal J\,\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi \mid\mathbf y)\big)+o(1), \] because the outer-product (empirical Fisher) information \(\sum_i\nabla\ell_i\nabla\ell_i^\top\) agrees with the observed information \(\mathcal J\) to leading order near the mode, where the score \(\sum_i\nabla\ell_i\) vanishes. Hence \(p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=p_D+o(1)\) and, combined with Part 1, \(\mathrm{DIC}=\mathrm{WAIC}+o(1)= \mathrm{LOO}+o(1)\).

Part 3. \(\mathrm{WAIC}\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}}\) are built only from the numbers \(\{p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\}\), which are densities of the data and are unchanged by any smooth invertible reparameterization \(\boldsymbol\xi\mapsto\boldsymbol\eta(\boldsymbol\xi)\) (the likelihood value at a parameter point does not depend on the coordinates used to name that point). DIC contains \(D(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\), and the posterior mean \(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi}\) is not equivariant under nonlinear reparameterization (\(\overline{\boldsymbol\eta(\boldsymbol\xi)}\neq\boldsymbol\eta(\bar {\boldsymbol\xi})\) in general), so \(D(\bar{\boldsymbol\xi})\), \(p_D\), and DIC change under reparameterization. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters, and a scorecard. The theorem says the three criteria estimate one thing, out-of-sample predictive accuracy, and agree to leading order, so the choice among them is about robustness and honesty, not about targeting different quantities. For an indirect comparison you will be asked to justify a modeling choice, say whether to include the age effect modifier \(\boldsymbol\beta_2\) or which outcome model to use, and predictive criteria are the Bayesian answer. The scorecard:

Property DIC WAIC LOO (PSIS)
Estimates out-of-sample elpd yes yes yes
Uses the whole posterior, not just its mean no yes yes
Pointwise (per-observation, gives a standard error) no yes yes
Invariant to reparameterization no yes yes
Per-observation reliability diagnostic no no yes (\(\widehat k\))
Can misbehave (negative penalty on skew posteriors) yes rarely flagged by \(\widehat k\)

The ranking is LOO, then WAIC, then DIC. Report WAIC or PSIS-LOO by default; report DIC only when an older NMA analysis you must compare against reported it, and then with these caveats stated. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Comparison is not checking. A subtle trap for the indirect-comparison analyst: a model can have the best WAIC or LOO in a candidate set and still answer the wrong question. Predictive criteria score fit to the observed evidence; they say nothing about whether the target population is right, whether the shared-effect-modifier assumption holds, or whether the networks overlap enough to transport an effect (Chapters 11, 12, and 17). Model checking, posterior predictive checks, prior-sensitivity analysis, and covariate-overlap diagnostics, is a separate obligation that must be met before model comparison is meaningful. Good LOO on a model that violates transportability is a precise answer to the wrong question. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Computation and recommendation. Every quantity here is computed from the posterior draws \(\{\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^S\) by replacing posterior expectations with sample averages: \(\mathrm{lppd}_i\approx\log\big(\tfrac1S\sum_s p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\big)\) and \(v_i\approx\) the sample variance of \(\{\log p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\}_s\). WAIC and LOO are pointwise (a contribution per observation), which yields a standard error for the criterion and for differences between models, and flags individual influential observations; DIC offers neither. Because DIC further depends on the non-equivariant posterior mean and can return a negative \(p_D\) for skewed posteriors, current practice prefers WAIC or, better, the LOO-CV of the next section, which estimates the same elpd while leaving each observation genuinely out. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

This development corresponds to I.5 in the proof catalogue, and to result D.14 (the DIC decomposition) of Chapter 15.

24.9 Pareto-smoothed importance sampling LOO

The leave-one-out identity Equation 24.18 expresses \(p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})\) as a posterior expectation, hence as a single Monte Carlo average over the draws we already have, with no refitting. The catch is that the importance ratios \(r_i(\boldsymbol\xi)=1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi)\) are heavy-tailed: they are largest exactly where the \(i\)-th likelihood is smallest, in regions the full posterior visits rarely, so a few draws can dominate the sum and inflate its variance, sometimes to infinity. Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) repairs the tail and, as a by-product, returns a diagnostic that says when the repair can be trusted. This is catalogue result I.6.

Definition 24.6 (Pareto-smoothed importance sampling LOO) Given posterior draws \(\{\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^S\) from the full posterior, form for each observation \(i\) the raw importance ratios \(r_i^{(s)}=1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\). PSIS proceeds:

  1. Fit a generalized Pareto distribution (GPD) to the largest ratios (the upper tail, in practice the top \(20\%\) or the top \(\min\{0.2S,\,3\sqrt S\}\) ratios), obtaining a shape estimate \(\widehat k\) and scale estimate.
  2. Replace those largest ratios by the expected GPD order statistics implied by the fit, and cap all ratios at the largest fitted value (smoothing and truncation), giving stabilized weights \(\bar r_i^{(s)}\).
  3. Estimate the leave-one-out predictive density by the self-normalized importance average of Equation 24.19, \[ \widehat p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=\frac{\sum_{s}\bar r_i^{(s)}\,p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})} {\sum_{s}\bar r_i^{(s)}}, \tag{24.22}\] and report \(\mathrm{PSIS\text{-}LOO}=-2\sum_i\log\widehat p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})\).

The fitted shape \(\widehat k_i\) is retained as a per-observation reliability diagnostic. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation: what is being smoothed, and what \(\widehat k\) measures. To reuse one posterior fit as if we had refit it without observation \(i\), we reweight the draws by \(r_i=1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol \xi)\): draws where the model fit \(y_i\) badly (small \(p\)) get large weight, because those are the draws the without-\(i\) posterior would favor. The danger is a handful of draws with tiny \(p(y_i\mid\cdot)\) and therefore enormous weights that swamp the sum, the way a single lottery winner swamps the mean income of a room. The generalized Pareto distribution (GPD) is the standard model for the extreme upper tail of such weights; its shape parameter \(k\) measures how heavy that tail is (larger \(k\) means a heavier tail, more prone to one dominating weight). PSIS fits a GPD to the largest weights, replaces them with the tamer values the fit predicts, and returns \(\widehat k\) as a thermometer for how dangerous the tail was. A large \(\widehat k\) says the reweighting is untrustworthy and, substantively, that unit \(i\) is influential. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

That the raw ratios are the correct importance weights is exactly Lemma 24.6: \(r_i\propto \pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y_{-i})/\pi(\boldsymbol\xi\mid\mathbf y)\), so Equation 24.19 targets the leave-one-out posterior. The reason smoothing is needed, and the meaning of the thresholds on \(\widehat k\), both follow from a single fact about the moments of a Pareto tail.

Lemma 24.7 (Moments of a generalized Pareto tail) Let \(X\) have a generalized Pareto upper tail with shape \(k>0\) and scale \(\sigma>0\), that is survival function \(\bar F(x)=(1+k x/\sigma)^{-1/k}\) for \(x\ge 0\). Then \(\mathbb E[X^{m}]<\infty\) if and only if \(m<1/k\). In particular the mean is finite iff \(k<1\) and the variance is finite iff \(k<1/2\).

Proof. The density is \(f(x)=\tfrac1\sigma(1+kx/\sigma)^{-1/k-1}\), so as \(x\to\infty\), \(f(x)\sim C\,x^{-1/k-1}\) for a positive constant \(C\) (since \((1+kx/\sigma)^{-1/k-1}\sim(kx/\sigma)^{-1/k-1}\)). The \(m\)-th moment is \(\int_0^\infty x^m f(x)\,dx\); its convergence is determined by the integrand’s decay at infinity, where \(x^m f(x)\sim C\,x^{m-1/k-1}\). The integral \(\int^{\infty}x^{m-1/k-1}\,dx\) converges if and only if the exponent is below \(-1\), that is \(m-1/k-1<-1\), equivalently \(m<1/k\). Setting \(m=1\) gives finiteness of the mean iff \(k<1\), and \(m=2\) gives finiteness of the variance iff \(k<1/2\). \(\square\)

Theorem 24.4 (The shape parameter governs reliability) Let the importance ratios \(r_i\) have a generalized Pareto upper tail with shape \(\widehat k_i\). Then:

  1. if \(\widehat k_i<\tfrac12\), the ratios have finite variance, the central limit theorem applies to the importance estimator, and the PSIS-LOO estimate Equation 24.22 is reliable;
  2. if \(\tfrac12\le\widehat k_i<1\), the ratios have finite mean but infinite variance, so the raw estimator has no \(\sqrt S\) central-limit guarantee, although PSIS smoothing keeps the bias and variance moderate in practice up to about \(\widehat k_i\approx 0.7\);
  3. if \(\widehat k_i\ge 1\), even the mean is infinite and the importance estimate is unusable; that observation must be evaluated by actually refitting the model without it.

Proof. By Lemma 24.7 the second moment of \(r_i\) is finite iff \(\widehat k_i<\tfrac12\), which is the classical condition for a \(\sqrt S\) central limit theorem for the self-normalized importance estimator Equation 24.19; this gives (1). For \(\tfrac12\le\widehat k_i<1\) the same lemma gives a finite mean but an infinite variance, so the importance average still converges to the right value by the law of large numbers but without a Gaussian error rate; the residual usefulness up to \(\widehat k_i\approx 0.7\) is the empirical finding that PSIS truncation and smoothing reduce the finite-sample error enough to be tolerable in that range, which is why \(0.7\) rather than \(0.5\) is the operational alarm. For \(\widehat k_i\ge 1\) the mean is infinite, the importance estimator does not even converge to a finite limit, and refitting is the only recourse; this is (3). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters: an influential study in the running network. Suppose the fitted model reports \(\widehat k=0.8\) for the aggregate \(BC\) arm and \(\widehat k<0.5\) everywhere else. Read it twice, exactly as Theorem 24.4 licenses. As a computational warning, \(0.8\) exceeds the \(0.7\) alarm, so the PSIS-LOO contribution of that arm is unreliable and should be recomputed by actually refitting without it. As a substantive signal, it says the \(d_{BC}\) contrast, and therefore the indirect \(d_{AC}\) that depends on it, rests heavily on this one study: the estimate is one study away from changing. The correct response is a robustness analysis, refit without \(BC\) and report how far \(d_{AC}\) moves, which is precisely the kind of sensitivity check an HTA reviewer demands of a two-study network. A high \(\widehat k\) is thus not only a caution about a number; it is the diagnostic pointing at the study your conclusion leans on. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why the harmonic-mean estimator fails, and PSIS as the fix. Setting all weights equal in Equation 24.18 recovers the notorious harmonic-mean estimator \(\widehat p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})= S/\sum_s 1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\), whose ratios \(r_i\) typically have \(\widehat k_i>\tfrac12\) and sometimes \(\widehat k_i\ge 1\); it is unstable precisely for the reason Theorem 24.4 isolates. PSIS replaces the offending upper tail of the \(r_i\) by the smooth GPD fit, which both stabilizes the estimate and yields \(\widehat k_i\) as a calibrated warning light. For ML-NMR and its unanchored extension, \(\widehat k_i\) flags aggregate cells or studies whose removal would most change the fit, so a large \(\widehat k_i\) is read twice: as a caution about the LOO number and as a substantive indication of an influential or poorly-fit data point. PSIS-LOO is the default method of the standard cross-validation software and the recommended criterion for these models. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

None of the results of this chapter has a counterpart in the companion Coq development; the formalized tree ends with the algebraic content of population adjustment, and the computational and diagnostic material here is established by the proofs above and validated empirically rather than by mechanized proof. 24.10 How to use this chapter in practice: a diagnostic workflow

The results above prove that each diagnostic does what it claims. This section assembles them into the order a health researcher should actually run, and into a single table to consult when one fires. The ordering principle is that model comparison is meaningless until the fit is computationally trustworthy, which is meaningless until the model has passed checking. So the sequence runs computation, then checking, then comparison, and never the reverse.

The order of operations.

  1. Divergences and tree depth first. Before reading any estimate, check the count of divergent transitions after warmup and whether trajectories are hitting the maximum tree depth. A nonzero divergence count is a red flag that the sampler could not enter part of the posterior; treat it as a geometry problem (Section 24.4), not a “run longer” problem.
  2. \(\widehat R\) next. Require \(\widehat R\le 1.01\) for every reported quantity: contrasts, \(\tau\), baselines, effect modifiers, and the transformed marginal probabilities. A failure here means the chains disagree (Section 24.5); do not read the posterior.
  3. Bulk and tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\). With convergence established, check that both exceed about \(400\) per quantity (Section 24.6). Low bulk \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) makes the posterior mean noisy; low tail \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) makes the credible-interval endpoints noisy.
  4. Inspect the culprits. For any parameter that failed, look at its trace and at pairs plots against \(\log\tau\); a funnel signature (divergences bunched at small \(\tau\)) points to the non-centered fix.
  5. QMC doubling for aggregate cells. Confirm the integration grid is fine enough
    1. so that the likelihood the sampler targeted is the one you meant.
  6. Posterior predictive and assumption checks. Only a model that fits and that respects its transportability and effect-modification assumptions (Chapters 11, 12, 17) is worth comparing.
  7. Then, and only then, PSIS-LOO or WAIC, reading \(\widehat k\) per unit before trusting the numbers (1).

Divergences in depth, because they are the check most often mishandled. A divergence is the leapfrog integrator’s report that it lost the trajectory: the simulated energy jumped by more than a threshold, so the proposed move was discarded and, more importantly, a region of the posterior was left unexplored. Two consequences follow. First, divergences usually signal geometry, not too few iterations: running four times as long reproduces them in the same place. The remedies are the ones this chapter derived or named, the non-centered parameterization when \(\tau\) is weakly identified (Theorem 24.1), a higher adapt_delta so warmup chooses a smaller step size, a tighter prior on \(\tau\) to keep the chain out of the extreme neck, and rescaling covariates so the mass matrix can round the geometry. Second, a few divergences are not negligible: because they concentrate exactly where the geometry is worst, often at small \(\tau\), the parameters most affected are the heterogeneity and the study effects tied to it, which for a sparse network are the very quantities the indirect comparison is most sensitive to. The correct reaction to “12 divergences” is to find where they sit and remove their cause, not to report the fit with a footnote.

24.11 A worked diagnostic example

We run the full diagnostic battery on a small, hand-checkable instance, mimicking the report one would produce for a fitted ML-NMR model: convergence (\(\widehat R\)), efficiency (\(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\)), integration error (the doubling rule), and model comparison (WAIC and PSIS-LOO). Every number can be reproduced with a calculator.

Example 24.2 (A complete diagnostic report) The numbers here are deliberately tiny so every step is checkable by hand: read each block as the hand-computation behind one row of the summary table of Section 24.10, applied to the running network. Here \(\theta=d_{AB}\) is the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) log-odds contrast, and the two fitted observations stand for one IPD patient and one aggregate arm.

Convergence: \(\widehat R\). Two chains of length \(N=4\) are run for a treatment contrast \(\theta=d_{AB}\), giving the post-warmup draws \[ \text{chain 1: }(2,4,6,8),\qquad \text{chain 2: }(4,6,8,10). \] The chain means are \(\bar\theta^{(1)}=5\) and \(\bar\theta^{(2)}=7\), grand mean \(\bar{\bar\theta}=6\). Each chain has the same sample variance, \(s_m^2=\frac{1}{3}\big[(\pm3)^2+(\pm1)^2\big]=\frac{1}{3}(9+1+1+9)= \frac{20}{3}\), so \(W=\frac{20}{3}\approx 6.667\). The between-chain variance is \(B=\frac{N}{M-1}\big[(5-6)^2 +(7-6)^2\big]=\frac{4}{1}(1+1)=8\). The pooled estimator is \[ \widehat V=\Big(1-\tfrac14\Big)\tfrac{20}{3}+\tfrac14\cdot 8=5+2=7, \qquad \widehat R=\sqrt{\widehat V/W}=\sqrt{\tfrac{7}{20/3}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{21}{20}}=\sqrt{1.05}\approx 1.025 . \] With \(\widehat R\approx 1.025>1.01\) the chains have not converged: the systematic offset between the chain means (5 versus 7) inflates \(\widehat V\) above \(W\), exactly the mechanism of part 3 of Theorem 24.2. The remedy is more warmup or a better parameterization (Section 24.4), after which \(\widehat R\) should fall below \(1.01\).

Efficiency: \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\). Suppose the converged chains behave like an AR(1) process. With lag-1 autocorrelation \(\phi=\tfrac13\), Example 24.1 gives \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{1+1/3}{1-1/3}=2\) and efficiency \(\tfrac12\), so \(4000\) total draws are worth \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=2000\) independent draws; the Monte Carlo standard error of the posterior mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt{2000}\). A stickier chain with \(\phi=0.9\) gives \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}=19\) and \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 210\), below the working threshold of \(400\) and a signal to run longer or improve mixing.

Integration error: the doubling rule. A logit-link aggregate cell requires \(\theta=\int_0^1\operatorname{expit}\!\big(0.4+0.8\,\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)\,du\), the marginal event probability when the linear predictor is normal with mean \(0.4\) and standard deviation \(0.8\). We integrate with the one-dimensional Sobol’ sequence (the van der Corput sequence in base \(2\), Definition 22.3), whose first points are \(u_1=\tfrac12,\,u_2=\tfrac14,\,u_3=\tfrac34,\,u_4=\tfrac18,\dots\); the first eight integrand values are \[ 0.599,\ 0.465,\ 0.719,\ 0.373,\ 0.658,\ 0.536,\ 0.789,\ 0.304, \] averaging to \(\widehat\theta^{(8)}=0.5554\). Doubling the grid gives the sequence \[ \widehat\theta^{(16)}=0.5683,\quad \widehat\theta^{(32)}=0.5765,\quad \widehat\theta^{(64)}=0.5812, \quad \widehat\theta^{(128)}=0.5838,\quad \widehat\theta^{(256)}=0.5853, \] converging to the exact value \(\theta=0.5868\) (computable independently by high-order Gauss-Hermite quadrature). The relative doubling increments Equation 24.15 are \[ \frac{|0.5812-0.5765|}{0.5812}=0.0082,\quad \frac{|0.5838-0.5812|}{0.5838}=0.0045,\quad \frac{|0.5853-0.5838|}{0.5853}=0.0024 . \] At tolerance \(\epsilon=0.005\) the criterion first passes at \(N=128\) (increment \(0.0045<0.005\)), and the next doubling confirms it (\(0.0024\)). The true relative error at \(N=128\) is \(|0.5838-0.5868|/0.5868= 0.0051\), consistent with the bound of Proposition 24.2 at \(\rho\approx\tfrac12\), which predicts a true relative error near the observed increment. So \(N=128\) points suffice for this cell at this tolerance.

Model comparison: WAIC and PSIS-LOO. Two observations are fit, and the pointwise likelihoods at \(S=4\) posterior draws are \[ p(y_1\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\in\{0.4,0.5,0.5,0.6\},\qquad p(y_2\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\in\{0.2,0.3,0.3,0.4\}. \] The pointwise predictive densities are the within-row means, \(\bar p_1=0.5\) and \(\bar p_2=0.3\), so \[ \mathrm{lppd}=\log 0.5+\log 0.3=-0.6931-1.2040=-1.8971 . \] The WAIC penalties are the within-row sample variances of the log likelihoods. For \(y_1\), the logs are \((-0.9163,-0.6931,-0.6931,-0.5108)\) with mean \(-0.7033\) and sample variance \(v_1=0.02754\); for \(y_2\), the logs are \((-1.6094,-1.2040,-1.2040,-0.9163)\) with mean \(-1.2334\) and sample variance \(v_2=0.08123\). Hence \(p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=v_1+v_2=0.1088\) and \[ \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=\mathrm{lppd}-p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}=-2.0059,\qquad \mathrm{WAIC}=-2\,(-2.0059)=4.012 . \] For LOO, the raw importance ratios are \(r_i^{(s)}=1/p(y_i\mid\boldsymbol\xi^{(s)})\), and because \(p\cdot r=1\) the self-normalized estimate Equation 24.22 with raw weights reduces to the harmonic mean \(\widehat p(y_i\mid\mathbf y_{-i})=S/\sum_s r_i^{(s)}\). For \(y_1\), \(\sum_s r_1^{(s)}=2.5+2+2+1.667=8.167\), so \(\widehat p_1=4/8.167=0.4898\); for \(y_2\), \(\sum_s r_2^{(s)}=5+3.333+3.333+2.5=14.167\), so \(\widehat p_2=4/14.167=0.2824\). Then \[ \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}}=\log 0.4898+\log 0.2824=-0.7137-1.2645=-1.9782,\qquad \mathrm{LOO}=3.956 . \] The two criteria agree to within \(4.012-3.956=0.056\) on the deviance scale, illustrating the asymptotic equivalence of part 1 of Theorem 24.3 even at this tiny sample size; the small gap is the higher-order \(o(v_i)\) term. With only \(S=4\) draws a generalized Pareto tail cannot be fit, so \(\widehat k\) is not reported here; in a real fit with thousands of draws one would read \(\widehat k_i\) for each observation against the \(0.7\) alarm of Theorem 24.4 before trusting the LOO numbers. \(\blacktriangleleft\)
