16  The Limits of Standard Network Meta-Analysis

16.1 Introduction

The previous two chapters built the standard machinery of indirect comparison. Bucher’s theorem (Theorem 14.1) makes the anchored estimator \(\hat d_{AB}=\hat d_{AC}-\hat d_{BC}\) unbiased for the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) network contrast, and the network meta-analysis of Chapter 15 generalizes the same subtraction to an arbitrary connected graph of treatments. Every one of those results rests on a single load-bearing hypothesis: the constancy of relative effects (Definition 14.2), equivalently transitivity (Definition 14.3), the assumption that the relative effect of one treatment versus another is the same number in every study population in the network. This chapter is the study of what happens when that hypothesis is false. It is, in a precise sense, the chapter that the remainder of the book exists to answer.

Two distinct failures are at work, and the chapter treats them in turn. The first is direct. We saw at the end of Chapter 14 (Section 14.12) that an effect modifier (Definition 11.3) whose distribution differs across the trial populations breaks transitivity, because the marginal relative effect a trial reports is then a population-specific average of a varying conditional effect. The failure-of-Bucher theorem of this chapter (Theorem 16.1) makes that statement quantitative: it gives the exact asymptotic bias of the unadjusted indirect comparison as an explicit pair of integrals against the difference of covariate distributions. The bias is not a small-sample artifact; it does not shrink as the trials grow, it is invisible in the Bucher standard error of Theorem 14.2, and it can be large enough to reverse the sign of the estimated treatment difference.

The second failure is subtler, and it concerns the natural first attempt at a cure. If the trouble is that an effect modifier is distributed differently across trials, why not regress the trial-level treatment effects on the trial-level covariate means and read off the modification? This is aggregate-level meta-regression, and the reason it cannot recover the within-patient effect-modifier coefficient is the ecological fallacy. Averaging a nonlinear inverse link over a covariate distribution is not the same as applying the inverse link to the average covariate; the gap is a Jensen gap (Theorem 4.6), the same nonlinearity that makes the odds ratio and the hazard ratio non-collapsible (Definition 7.7). We define ecological bias precisely (Definition 16.2) and prove, with a closed-form probit computation, that the slope of an aggregate regression is a strictly attenuated version of the individual-level interaction coefficient (Theorem 16.2). Aggregate data, in short, do not contain the information that the correction requires.

The synthesis is the motivation for Part IV. Both failures point to the same two missing ingredients: the individual-level effect-modifier coefficient, which needs patient-level data from at least one trial to estimate without ecological bias; and an integration of the individual model over the target covariate distribution, in place of the illegitimate plug-in of a covariate mean. A method that supplies both is a population-adjusted indirect comparison. Matching-adjusted indirect comparison (Chapter 18), the simulated treatment comparison (Chapter 19), and multilevel network meta-regression (Chapter 20) are three such methods, and the precise gap this chapter exhibits is exactly what they are built to close.

Throughout we keep the notation of Notation and Chapters 11 and 14. For a treatment \(k\) and covariate value \(x\), \(\mu_k(x)=\mathbb E[Y(k)\mid X=x]\) is the conditional mean potential outcome; \(g\) is the link of Definition 7.2; the conditional contrast of \(b\) versus \(a\) on scale \(g\) is \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)=g(\mu_b(x))-g(\mu_a(x))\) as in Definition 11.3; and a population \(\mathcal P\) has covariate density \(f_{\mathcal P}\) with mean \(\bar{\mathbf x}_{\mathcal P}=\int x\,f_{\mathcal P}(x)\,dx\). This chapter owns result E.3 of the proof catalogue and introduces the ecological-bias material that prepares Part IV.

16.1.1 A running clinical example

Before any definitions, here is a concrete scenario we will return to at every transition. Read the whole chapter with this picture in mind; the notation below is just a precise language for describing it.

Example 16.1 (Two biologics for plaque psoriasis) Consider moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. Two biologic drugs, \(A\) and \(B\), have each been compared in a randomized trial against the same standard systemic therapy \(C\), but the two drugs have never been compared head to head. We want the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) contrast, and because both trials share the comparator \(C\) we can form it indirectly (an anchored comparison: both routes pass through the shared comparator \(C\)).

The outcome is an unfavorable binary event, treatment failure (failing to reach an adequate skin response by week 16); a larger risk is worse. One baseline covariate matters: prior biologic therapy, \(X=1\) for biologic-experienced patients and \(X=0\) for biologic-naive patients. Prior therapy makes the disease harder to control, so it raises the failure risk under the comparator \(C\) and under biologic \(B\) by the same amount, leaving the \(B\)-versus-\(C\) benefit unchanged; but biologic \(A\) acts through a pathway unaffected by prior exposure, so its failure risk stays flat and its advantage over \(C\) is larger in experienced patients. Prior exposure is therefore an effect modifier of \(A\) versus \(C\) but not of \(B\) versus \(C\). The stratum-specific failure risks are

stratum \(x\) \(\mu_A(x)\) \(\mu_B(x)\) \(\mu_C(x)\)
\(x=0\) (naive) \(0.30\) \(0.25\) \(0.40\)
\(x=1\) (experienced) \(0.30\) \(0.45\) \(0.60\)

The two trials enrolled different case mixes. The \(A\)-versus-\(C\) trial recruited mostly biologic-naive patients (prevalence of prior exposure \(p_{AC}=0.2\)); the \(B\)-versus-\(C\) trial recruited mostly experienced patients (\(p_{BC}=0.8\)). The decision (target) population for the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) recommendation is evenly mixed, \(p_{*}=0.5\).

Why the shared comparator does not rescue us. Sharing \(C\) is what makes an indirect comparison possible; it does not make the two trials exchangeable. The \(A\)-versus-\(C\) trial measured \(A\)’s benefit almost entirely in easy-to-treat patients, where that benefit is small, while the \(B\)-versus-\(C\) trial measured \(B\)’s benefit almost entirely in hard-to-treat patients. Subtracting the two trial-level effects silently pits \(A\)’s benefit at one case mix against \(B\)’s benefit at another. That mismatch is exactly the bias this chapter quantifies; Example 16.2 computes it and finds it large enough to reverse the sign of the answer.

Why it matters for a decision. If the indirect estimate has the wrong sign, a health technology assessment (for example a NICE appraisal, Phillippo et al. (2016)) can recommend the inferior drug: patients may be denied the better biologic, or a payer may fund one that is in truth no better. The bias below is not academic; it can flip a reimbursement decision. This is why NICE Technical Support Document 18 (Phillippo et al. (2016)) requires an indirect comparison to justify its constancy assumption before the estimate is trusted. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Terms carried from earlier chapters. A novice can treat the following as a glossary and refer back as needed.

  • Contrast: a difference of two treatments on a chosen scale, for example a risk difference or a log odds ratio. Throughout, “\(a\) versus \(b\)”, and the symbols \(d_{ab}\) and \(\tau_{ab}\), mean \(b\) relative to \(a\): \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)=g(\mu_b(x))-g(\mu_a(x))\).
  • Anchored comparison: an indirect \(A\)-versus-\(B\) comparison in which both drugs are joined through a shared comparator \(C\) (Chapter 14). The alternative, an unanchored comparison of absolute arm outcomes, is Chapter 26.
  • Effect modifier versus prognostic variable: an effect modifier changes the size of a contrast across its levels; a purely prognostic variable shifts both arms equally and leaves the contrast unchanged (Chapter 11). This distinction is the most reused idea in the chapter.
  • Transitivity and consistency: transitivity is the assumption that relative effects are comparable across trials so indirect and direct evidence agree; consistency is the same idea checked inside a network (Chapter 15). Node-splitting is the diagnostic that compares direct and indirect evidence at one contrast. Heterogeneity is unexplained trial-to-trial variation beyond sampling error (Chapter 13); it is a variance phenomenon, distinct from the systematic bias studied here.
  • Link and inverse link \(g,g^{-1}\): \(g\) maps a probability or mean to the linear-predictor scale; \(g^{-1}\) maps back. For the logit link \(g^{-1}\) is the logistic function \(e^{\eta}/(1+e^{\eta})\); for the probit link \(g^{-1}\) is the standard normal distribution function \(\Phi\) (Chapter 7).
  • Directly collapsible scale: a scale on which the population-level (marginal) effect equals the covariate-average of the per-stratum (conditional) effects. The risk difference is directly collapsible; the odds ratio is not (Chapter 11). We keep the qualifier “directly” to match Chapter 11; for this chapter you may read it as “collapsible.”
  • Affine in \(x\): “linear plus a constant,” a function of the form \(a+b^{\top}x\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Sign convention. A positive \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)\) means \(b\) scores higher than \(a\) on scale \(g\) at covariate value \(x\). On the risk-difference scale a positive \(A\)-versus-\(B\) contrast means \(B\) carries the higher event risk; since the event is unfavorable, positive means \(B\) is worse than \(A\). The bias terms \(b_{AC},b_{BC}\) that appear in Equation 16.6 are bias contributions, not treatment contrasts: the lowercase \(b\) is a bias, never “treatment \(B\).” \(\blacktriangleleft\)

16.2 Conditional and marginal relative effects

Two relative effects can be attached to a population, and the distinction between them is the hinge of the whole chapter. The first is the one a trial reports from its arm summaries; the second is the one a patient-level regression estimates. They agree on a collapsible scale and diverge on a non-collapsible one, and even when they agree they respond differently to a change of covariate distribution.

Definition 16.1 (Marginal and average-conditional relative effects) Let \(\mathcal P\) be a population with covariate density \(f_{\mathcal P}\), let \(g\) be a strictly increasing link, and let \(a,b\) be two treatments with conditional means \(\mu_a(\cdot),\mu_b(\cdot)\). The marginal relative effect of \(b\) versus \(a\) in \(\mathcal P\) on scale \(g\) is the contrast of marginalized arm means, \[ d_{ab(\mathcal P)} \;=\; g\!\left(\int \mu_b(x)\,f_{\mathcal P}(x)\,dx\right)-g\!\left(\int \mu_a(x)\,f_{\mathcal P}(x)\,dx\right), \tag{16.1}\] exactly the quantity of Equation 14.2. The average-conditional relative effect of \(b\) versus \(a\) in \(\mathcal P\) on scale \(g\) is the covariate average of the conditional contrast, \[ \tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)} \;=\; \int \tau^g_{ab}(x)\,f_{\mathcal P}(x)\,dx \;=\; \int \bigl[g(\mu_b(x))-g(\mu_a(x))\bigr]\,f_{\mathcal P}(x)\,dx . \tag{16.2}\] The first marginalizes inside the link, the second outside. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader translation. The single distinguishing mark is the tilde, so fix the mnemonic: no tilde is marginal, the number a trial reports; the tilde is average-conditional, the number a patient-level regression gives. The difference is where the averaging sits relative to \(g\).

Quantity Recipe Where the averaging happens
Marginal \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) (Equation 16.1) average each arm’s outcome over patients, then apply \(g\) and subtract inside the link: the average is inside the argument of \(g\)
Average-conditional \(\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) (Equation 16.2) apply \(g\) within each stratum and subtract, then average the per-stratum contrasts outside the link: the average is applied after \(g\)

In a two-arm trial you observe only arm totals, so you can form the marginal effect \(d\) directly. With individual patient data you can estimate the per-patient contrast \(\tau^g_{ab}(\cdot)\) and then average it, giving \(\tilde d\). On a directly collapsible scale (for example the risk difference) the two recipes give the same number; on the odds-ratio scale they do not, and the difference is the non-collapsibility gap we revisit in Section 16.3.1.

The marginal effect Equation 16.1 is what a two-arm trial estimates from its observed arm means, since a randomized trial identifies \(\int\mu_a f\) and \(\int\mu_b f\) and then applies \(g\) to each (Proposition 14.1). The average-conditional effect Equation 16.2 is what an individual patient data regression estimates, since such a regression identifies the conditional contrast \(\tau^g_{ab}(\cdot)\) as a function of \(x\) and then averages it. The relationship between the two is exactly the collapsibility theory of Chapters 7 and 11.

Proposition 16.1 (When the two relative effects coincide) With the notation of Definition 16.1:

  1. If the scale \(g\) is the identity (the risk-difference or mean-difference scale), then \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}=\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) for every population \(\mathcal P\) and every pair of conditional means.
  2. More generally, if the effect measure based on \(g\) is directly collapsible over \(X\) in the sense of Definition 11.6, then \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}=\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) for every \(\mathcal P\).
  3. If \(g\) is non-collapsible (the logit or log-hazard scale, Definition 7.7, Theorem 11.2), the two differ in general; their difference is the non-collapsibility gap, a Jensen gap (Theorem 4.6) for the nonlinear link.

Proof. For (1), with \(g\) the identity, linearity of the integral gives \[ d_{ab(\mathcal P)}=\int\mu_b f-\int\mu_a f=\int(\mu_b-\mu_a)f=\int\tau^{\mathrm{id}}_{ab}(x)f(x)\,dx=\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)} . \] Statement (2) unpacks the definition of direct collapsibility. Direct collapsibility on scale \(g\) means precisely that the population marginal effect equals the covariate-weighted average of the conditional effects, for every distribution of \(X\); Equation 11.9 is that statement. Applied to the contrast of \(b\) versus \(a\) it reads \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}=\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\), which is what we want. The identity scale is the canonical directly collapsible case, Proposition 11.3. For (3), take \(g=\operatorname{logit}\) and a non-degenerate baseline; Theorem 11.2 exhibits stratum means with constant conditional effect for which the marginal effect strictly differs, so \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\neq\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\). The discrepancy is the difference between \(g\) applied to an average and the average of \(g\) applied pointwise, which is the Jensen gap of Theorem 4.6 for the convex or concave pieces of \(g\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. On the risk-difference scale (and any directly collapsible scale) you may treat “what the trial reports” and “the average of per-patient contrasts” as the same number. On the odds-ratio or hazard-ratio scale you may not, and pretending otherwise is a second, independent source of error that we isolate in Section 16.3.1. For the first half of the chapter we work on a directly collapsible scale, where Proposition 16.1 lets us use \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) and \(\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) interchangeably and the algebra is exact; the identity scale is the running example. The non-collapsible case, where the two relative effects come apart, returns in Section 16.4 as a second and independent source of bias. The pointwise additivity (sometimes called conditional additivity) that powers Bucher’s subtraction holds for the conditional contrasts pointwise.

Lemma 16.1 (Pointwise additivity of conditional contrasts) For treatments \(A,B,C\) and every covariate value \(x\), \[ \tau^g_{AB}(x) \;=\; \tau^g_{AC}(x)-\tau^g_{BC}(x). \tag{16.3}\] Consequently, on a directly collapsible scale, \(\tilde d_{AB(\mathcal P)}=\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P)}-\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P)}\) in every population \(\mathcal P\).

Proof. By definition \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)=g(\mu_b(x))-g(\mu_a(x))\), so at fixed \(x\) the three contrasts are differences of the three numbers \(g(\mu_A(x)),g(\mu_B(x)),g(\mu_C(x))\), and \[ \tau^g_{AC}(x)-\tau^g_{BC}(x)=\bigl[g(\mu_C(x))-g(\mu_A(x))\bigr]-\bigl[g(\mu_C(x))-g(\mu_B(x))\bigr]=g(\mu_B(x))-g(\mu_A(x))=\tau^g_{AB}(x), \] which is Equation 16.3; this is Lemma 14.1 applied at the value \(x\) rather than to the marginal positions. Integrating both sides against \(f_{\mathcal P}\) and using linearity gives \(\tilde d_{AB(\mathcal P)}=\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P)}-\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P)}\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. This pointwise identity is the entire license for Bucher’s subtraction: at each covariate value the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) contrast is exactly the \(A\)-versus-\(C\) contrast minus the \(B\)-versus-\(C\) contrast. Bucher subtracts the two trials’ effects in the hope that this identity survives averaging. The failure theorem below is precisely the statement that it need not survive, because the three populations average the same conditional contrasts over different covariate distributions.

16.2.1 The two faces of constancy

The constancy hypothesis of Bucher’s theorem can now be split into its two natural strengths. Both will appear in the failure analysis, and keeping them apart is what lets the theorem isolate the role of the covariate distribution.

The first is constancy of marginal relative effects, the original transitivity of Definition 14.3: the marginal effect \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_j)}\) is the same in every population. This is what Bucher’s theorem requires, and it is what we will show fails. The second is conditional constancy of relative effects on scale \(g\): there is a single function \(\tau^g_{ab}(\cdot)\) such that, in every population in play, the conditional contrast at covariate value \(x\) equals \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)\). Conditional constancy says the per-stratum effect transports; it is the assumption that an individual-level effect estimated in one trial is the same biological quantity in another population with the same within-stratum structure. In the trial-methodology literature this is the shared effect modifier assumption (SEMA) of Phillippo et al. (2016); “conditional constancy” is its estimand-level statement, and the two say the same thing. It is developed in full as the anchored-adjustment assumption in Chapter 17. The point of the failure analysis is that conditional constancy can hold while marginal constancy fails, and the gap between them is governed entirely by the covariate distributions.

The distinction is easiest to hold onto as a table.

Assumption What it says Needed by Bucher? Can fail while the other holds?
Marginal constancy (transitivity, Definition 14.3) the trial-reported effect \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_j)}\) is one number across populations yes: this is the hypothesis of Theorem 14.1 yes: it fails whenever an effect modifier is imbalanced
Conditional constancy (SEMA, Phillippo et al. (2016)) the per-stratum contrast \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)\) is one function across populations no yes: it can hold even when marginal constancy fails

Clinically: the biology of a treatment effect within, say, each prior-exposure stratum may transport perfectly (conditional constancy), yet the trial-level average still changes if one trial enrolled mostly experienced patients and the other mostly naive ones (marginal constancy fails). The whole chapter lives in that gap.

Lemma 16.2 (An imbalanced effect modifier breaks marginal constancy) Assume conditional constancy of relative effects on scale \(g\), so the conditional contrast \(\tau^g_{ab}(\cdot)\) is the same function in two populations \(\mathcal P_1,\mathcal P_2\). On a directly collapsible scale the marginal relative effects are then \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_i)}=\int\tau^g_{ab}(x)\,f_i(x)\,dx\), and

  1. if \(X\) is not an effect modifier of \(b\) versus \(a\) on scale \(g\), that is \(\tau^g_{ab}(\cdot)\equiv c\) is constant, then \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_1)}=d_{ab(\mathcal P_2)}=c\) regardless of the covariate distributions; marginal constancy holds automatically;
  2. if \(X\) is an effect modifier on scale \(g\), then there exist covariate distributions \(f_1\neq f_2\) with \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_1)}\neq d_{ab(\mathcal P_2)}\); marginal constancy fails.

Proof. Under conditional constancy and direct collapsibility, Proposition 16.1(2) gives \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_i)}=\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P_i)}=\int\tau^g_{ab}(x)f_i(x)\,dx\). For (1), if \(\tau^g_{ab}\equiv c\) then \(\int c\,f_i(x)\,dx=c\int f_i=c\) for either density, since a density integrates to one; the two marginal effects equal the common value \(c\). For (2), effect modification means (Definition 11.3) there are points \(x_1,x_2\) in the support with \(\tau^g_{ab}(x_1)\neq\tau^g_{ab}(x_2)\). If the support is discrete, take the two-point masses \(f_1=\delta_{x_1}\) and \(f_2=\delta_{x_2}\) directly; then \(d_{ab(\mathcal P_1)}=\tau^g_{ab}(x_1)\neq\tau^g_{ab}(x_2)=d_{ab(\mathcal P_2)}\) exactly, with no limit needed. If the covariate is continuous, take a sequence of admissible densities \(f_1^{(n)}\) concentrating on shrinking neighborhoods of \(x_1\); since \(\tau^g_{ab}\) is bounded on the common support and continuous at \(x_1\), dominated convergence gives \(\int\tau^g_{ab}\,f_1^{(n)}\to\tau^g_{ab}(x_1)\), and likewise \(\int\tau^g_{ab}\,f_2^{(n)}\to\tau^g_{ab}(x_2)\). For \(n\) large enough the two marginal effects lie within any prescribed tolerance of the unequal numbers \(\tau^g_{ab}(x_1)\) and \(\tau^g_{ab}(x_2)\), hence are themselves unequal, so the two marginal effects differ. \(\square\)

Reader translation. Part (1) is the formal statement that purely prognostic covariates do not threaten an anchored comparison: a covariate that fails to modify the contrast leaves the marginal effect unchanged however its distribution shifts, because its effect on the two arms cancels inside \(\tau^g_{ab}\). This is the machine-checked cancellation of Theorem 11.1. Part (2) is the mechanism of failure: only effect modifiers, and only when their distribution differs across populations, can move the marginal effect and so break the transitivity that Bucher’s theorem assumes. In the running example (Example 16.1), prior exposure modifies \(A\) versus \(C\), so the differing case mixes of the two trials will move that trial’s reported effect; it does not modify \(B\) versus \(C\), so the \(B\)-versus-\(C\) trial’s imbalance turns out to be harmless.

16.3 The failure-of-Bucher theorem

We now compute the bias exactly. The setup is the anchored triangle of Chapter 14 (three treatments joined through the shared comparator \(C\)): an \(AC\) trial in population \(\mathcal P_{AC}\), a \(BC\) trial in population \(\mathcal P_{BC}\), and a target population \(\mathcal P^{*}\) in which we wish to report the \(A\)-versus-\(B\) contrast. Conditional constancy holds, so the conditional contrasts \(\tau^g_{AC}(\cdot)\) and \(\tau^g_{BC}(\cdot)\) are common to all three populations; the covariate distributions \(f_{AC},f_{BC},f_{*}\) may all differ. The target estimand is the average-conditional contrast in \(\mathcal P^{*}\), \[ \Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*}) \;=\; \int \tau^g_{AB}(x)\,f_{*}(x)\,dx \;=\; \int\bigl[\tau^g_{AC}(x)-\tau^g_{BC}(x)\bigr]f_{*}(x)\,dx , \tag{16.4}\] the second equality by Lemma 16.1. Each trial reports its own average-conditional contrast, \(\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}=\int\tau^g_{AC}f_{AC}\) and \(\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}=\int\tau^g_{BC}f_{BC}\), and the Bucher procedure subtracts them.

Four quantities are now in play, and it is worth pinning down which is which before the theorem. This is the estimand roadmap for the rest of the chapter.

Symbol Plain meaning Estimated from Why it matters
\(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) (Equation 16.1) marginal effect: apply \(g\) to each arm’s average outcome, then subtract a two-arm trial’s arm summaries this is the number that shifts when an effect modifier is imbalanced
\(\tilde d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\) (Equation 16.2) average-conditional effect: per-stratum contrast, averaged over the population an individual-patient-data regression on a collapsible scale it equals \(d_{ab(\mathcal P)}\); it is what Bucher implicitly subtracts
\(\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*})\) (Equation 16.4) the target \(A\)-versus-\(B\) contrast in the decision population the estimand we want this is what a valid indirect comparison must deliver
\(d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}\) the Bucher estimand \(\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}-\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}\) subtracting the two trials’ contrasts what standard NMA delivers; differs from the target by Equation 16.6

Before the general integral statement, here is the binary-covariate special case. It contains the whole idea in one line of arithmetic, and the general theorem is its integral generalization.

Proposition 16.2 (The binary-covariate bias) In the anchored triangle above, suppose \(X\in\{0,1\}\) is binary with \(\Pr(X=1)\) equal to \(p_{AC},p_{BC},p_{*}\) in the three populations, and conditional constancy holds. Write the effect-modification jump of the \(t\)-versus-\(C\) contrast as \(s_{tC}=\tau^g_{tC}(1)-\tau^g_{tC}(0)\), for \(t\in\{A,B\}\). Then the asymptotic bias of the unadjusted indirect comparison \(d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}=\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}-\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}\) relative to the target Equation 16.4 is \[ d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}-\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*}) \;=\; s_{AC}\,(p_{AC}-p_{*}) \;-\; s_{BC}\,(p_{BC}-p_{*}) . \tag{16.5}\]

Proof. For a binary covariate, \(\tilde d_{tC(\mathcal P_j)}=(1-p_j)\tau^g_{tC}(0)+p_j\tau^g_{tC}(1)\) in any population with prevalence \(p_j\). Applying this to the trial population \(p_{tC}\) and to the target \(p_{*}\) and differencing, \[ \begin{aligned} \tilde d_{tC(\mathcal P_{tC})}-\int\tau^g_{tC}f_{*} &=\bigl[(1-p_{tC})\tau^g_{tC}(0)+p_{tC}\tau^g_{tC}(1)\bigr]-\bigl[(1-p_{*})\tau^g_{tC}(0)+p_{*}\tau^g_{tC}(1)\bigr]\\ &=\tau^g_{tC}(0)\,(p_{*}-p_{tC})+\tau^g_{tC}(1)\,(p_{tC}-p_{*})\\ &=\bigl[\tau^g_{tC}(1)-\tau^g_{tC}(0)\bigr](p_{tC}-p_{*})=s_{tC}\,(p_{tC}-p_{*}). \end{aligned} \] Call this \(b_{tC}\). Subtracting the \(BC\) term from the \(AC\) term and using additivity Equation 16.3 inside the target gives \(d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}-\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*})=b_{AC}-b_{BC} =s_{AC}(p_{AC}-p_{*})-s_{BC}(p_{BC}-p_{*})\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. On a binary effect modifier, each contrast’s bias is its effect-modification jump times how much the trial over- or under-represents the high-risk stratum relative to the target. No modification (\(s_{tC}=0\)) or no imbalance (\(p_{tC}=p_{*}\)) kills that term. For the psoriasis network of Example 16.1 on the risk-difference scale, \(s_{AC}=\tau_{AC}(1)-\tau_{AC}(0)=0.30-0.10=0.20\) and \(s_{BC}=0.15-0.15=0\), while \(p_{AC}-p_{*}=0.2-0.5=-0.3\); the bias is \(0.20\cdot(-0.3)-0=-0.06\). The \(A\)-versus-\(C\) trial under-represents the hard-to-treat stratum, and \(A\)-versus-\(C\) is the modified contrast, so the indirect comparison inherits a \(-0.06\) bias. Example 16.2 shows this is enough to flip the sign of the answer.

Theorem 16.1 (Failure of Bucher under differential effect modification) Fix a treatment triple \(\{A,B,C\}\) and a scale \(g\), and assume conditional constancy of relative effects on scale \(g\), with common conditional contrasts \(\tau^g_{AC}(\cdot),\tau^g_{BC}(\cdot)\). Let the Bucher estimand built from the two trials’ average-conditional contrasts be \[ d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}} \;=\; \tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}-\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})} \;=\; \int\tau^g_{AC}(x)\,f_{AC}(x)\,dx-\int\tau^g_{BC}(x)\,f_{BC}(x)\,dx , \] and let the target be \(\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*})\) of Equation 16.4. Then the asymptotic bias of the unadjusted indirect comparison is \[ d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}-\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*}) \;=\; \underbrace{\int\tau^g_{AC}(x)\,[f_{AC}(x)-f_{*}(x)]\,dx}_{\textstyle b_{AC}} \;-\; \underbrace{\int\tau^g_{BC}(x)\,[f_{BC}(x)-f_{*}(x)]\,dx}_{\textstyle b_{BC}} . \tag{16.6}\] Moreover:

  1. (Vanishing under no modification.) For each contrast \(t\in\{A,B\}\), the term \(b_{tC}\) vanishes for every pair of covariate distributions \((f_{tC},f_{*})\) if and only if \(\tau^g_{tC}\) is constant on the common support, that is, if and only if \(X\) is not an effect modifier of \(t\) versus \(C\) on scale \(g\). In particular, if neither contrast is modified, the bias is zero for all covariate distributions.

  2. (Genuineness of the bias.) If \(X\) is an effect modifier of at least one of \(A\) versus \(C\) or \(B\) versus \(C\) on scale \(g\), then there exist covariate distributions making the bias Equation 16.6 nonzero.

Proof. By Theorem 14.1, the Bucher subtraction would equal the target contrast if each trial reported the population-free network effect, that is if marginal constancy held; the present hypotheses grant only conditional constancy, and we compute the residual directly. Linearity of the integral gives, for the \(AC\) contrast, \[ \tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}-\int\tau^g_{AC}(x)f_{*}(x)\,dx =\int\tau^g_{AC}(x)f_{AC}(x)\,dx-\int\tau^g_{AC}(x)f_{*}(x)\,dx =\int\tau^g_{AC}(x)\,[f_{AC}(x)-f_{*}(x)]\,dx=b_{AC}, \] and identically \(\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}-\int\tau^g_{BC}f_{*}=b_{BC}\). Subtracting the second display from the first and using the additivity Equation 16.3 inside the target, \[ d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}-\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*}) =\Bigl(\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}-\textstyle\int\tau^g_{AC}f_{*}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}-\int\tau^g_{BC}f_{*}\Bigr)=b_{AC}-b_{BC}, \] which is Equation 16.6.

For (1), suppose first that \(\tau^g_{tC}\equiv c\) is constant. Then \(b_{tC}=c\int[f_{tC}(x)-f_{*}(x)]\,dx=c\,(1-1)=0\), since each density integrates to one; this holds for any pair of distributions. Conversely suppose \(\tau^g_{tC}\) is not constant, so there are support points \(x_1,x_2\) with \(\tau^g_{tC}(x_1)\neq\tau^g_{tC}(x_2)\). Choosing \(f_{tC}\) concentrated near \(x_2\) and \(f_{*}\) near \(x_1\) (point masses if the support is discrete) gives \(b_{tC}\to\tau^g_{tC}(x_2)-\tau^g_{tC}(x_1)\neq 0\), so the term does not vanish for all pairs. Hence \(b_{tC}\equiv 0\) over all distribution pairs if and only if \(\tau^g_{tC}\) is constant, i.e. \(X\) is not an effect modifier for \(t\) versus \(C\). The final clause of (1) is the case where both contrasts are constant, so \(b_{AC}=b_{BC}=0\) identically.

For (2), if (say) \(X\) modifies the \(A\)-versus-\(C\) contrast, the converse direction of (1) furnishes a pair \((f_{AC},f_{*})\) with \(b_{AC}\neq0\); choosing \(f_{BC}=f_{*}\) makes \(b_{BC}=0\), so the total bias is \(b_{AC}\neq0\). The same construction works if \(X\) modifies the \(B\)-versus-\(C\) contrast. \(\square\)

This is result E.3 of the proof catalogue, and Proposition 16.2 is its two-point special case (\(b_{tC}=s_{tC}(p_{tC}-p_{*})\) when \(X\) is binary). The bias decomposition Equation 16.6 has a transparent reading. Each term \(b_{tC}\) is the difference between the trial-population mean of the conditional contrast and its target-population mean; it is a covariance-like quantity, the inner product of the effect-modification function \(\tau^g_{tC}\) with the signed measure \(f_{tC}-f_{*}\). Physically, a positive \(b_{AC}\) says trial \(AC\) over-represents the strata where \(A\)’s advantage is large relative to the target: it sampled too many high-contrast patients, so its reported \(A\)-versus-\(C\) effect overshoots the target average. The bias is large when the contrast varies steeply over \(x\) (strong effect modification) and when the trial and target distributions are far apart (strong imbalance), and it is exactly zero when either factor is absent. The cure is now legible in the formula: replace \(f_{tC}\) by \(f_{*}\), that is, reweight or re-regress each trial’s conditional contrast to the target covariate distribution, and each \(b_{tC}\) collapses to zero. That operation is population adjustment.

Why this matters. Standard NMA computes \(d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}\); the estimand a decision needs is \(\Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},g}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*})\). The theorem says these differ by a term that a health researcher can neither see in the standard error nor remove without extra data, which is the entire reason population-adjusted methods exist.

We put numbers on the theorem immediately, in the running example.

Example 16.2 (A sign-reversing Bucher bias on the risk-difference scale) This is the psoriasis network of Example 16.1 with the arithmetic filled in. The outcome is a binary event (treatment failure, an unfavorable outcome, so larger risk is worse), \(X\in\{0,1\}\) is prior biologic exposure, and we work on the identity (risk-difference) scale, \(g=\mathrm{id}\), which is directly collapsible (Proposition 11.3). Conditional constancy holds, so the stratum risks below are common to all three populations:

stratum \(x\) \(\mu_A(x)\) \(\mu_B(x)\) \(\mu_C(x)\)
\(x=0\) \(0.30\) \(0.25\) \(0.40\)
\(x=1\) \(0.30\) \(0.45\) \(0.60\)

so treatment \(A\) has constant risk \(0.30\) while \(B\) and \(C\) have stratum-dependent risk. The conditional contrasts on the risk-difference scale, \(\tau_{tC}(x)=\mu_C(x)-\mu_t(x)\), are \[ \tau_{AC}(0)=0.40-0.30=0.10,\quad \tau_{AC}(1)=0.60-0.30=0.30, \] \[ \tau_{BC}(0)=0.40-0.25=0.15,\quad \tau_{BC}(1)=0.60-0.45=0.15 . \] Thus \(X\) is an effect modifier of \(A\) versus \(C\) (the contrast moves from \(0.10\) to \(0.30\)) and is not an effect modifier of \(B\) versus \(C\) (the contrast is \(0.15\) in both strata). The target contrast \(\tau_{AB}(x)=\mu_B(x)-\mu_A(x)\) is \(\tau_{AB}(0)=0.25-0.30=-0.05\) and \(\tau_{AB}(1)=0.45-0.30=0.15\), which matches \(\tau_{AC}-\tau_{BC}\) stratum by stratum as Lemma 16.1 requires.

The three populations differ only in the prevalence \(p=\Pr(X=1)\) of the effect modifier: \[ p_{AC}=0.2,\qquad p_{BC}=0.8,\qquad p_{*}=0.5 . \]

Trial reports. Each trial reports its average-conditional contrast, which on this scale equals what its arm means give: \[ \tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}=(1-p_{AC})\,\tau_{AC}(0)+p_{AC}\,\tau_{AC}(1)=0.8(0.10)+0.2(0.30)=0.14, \] \[ \tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}=(1-p_{BC})\,\tau_{BC}(0)+p_{BC}\,\tau_{BC}(1)=0.15 . \]

Bucher estimand. \[ d_{AB}^{\mathrm{Bucher}}=\tilde d_{AC(\mathcal P_{AC})}-\tilde d_{BC(\mathcal P_{BC})}=0.14-0.15=-0.01 . \]

Target. \[ \Delta^{\mathrm{Cond},\mathrm{id}}_{AB}(\mathcal P^{*})=(1-p_{*})\,\tau_{AB}(0)+p_{*}\,\tau_{AB}(1)=0.5(-0.05)+0.5(0.15)=0.05 . \]

Bias. The unadjusted indirect comparison reports \(-0.01\) when the true target risk difference is \(+0.05\): it is biased by \(-0.06\), and it has the wrong sign. On the unadjusted estimate \(B\) looks marginally protective relative to \(A\); in truth \(B\) raises the event risk by five percentage points in the target population. In clinical terms, the indirect comparison would recommend \(B\) when \(A\) is in fact the better drug.

Verification through Equation 16.6. The bias decomposition gives \[ b_{AC}=\tau_{AC}(0)\,[(1-p_{AC})-(1-p_{*})]+\tau_{AC}(1)\,[p_{AC}-p_{*}]=0.10(0.3)+0.30(-0.3)=0.03-0.09=-0.06, \] \[ b_{BC}=0.15\cdot\bigl([(1-p_{BC})-(1-p_{*})]+[p_{BC}-p_{*}]\bigr)=0.15\cdot 0=0, \] the second vanishing because \(\tau_{BC}\) is constant, exactly Theorem 16.1(1). The total \(b_{AC}-b_{BC}=-0.06-0=-0.06\) reproduces the bias, and matches the binary formula \(s_{AC}(p_{AC}-p_{*})=0.20\cdot(-0.3)\) of Proposition 16.2. Note that \(B\) versus \(C\) is the more imbalanced contrast, \(p_{BC}=0.8\) against \(p_{*}=0.5\), yet it contributes nothing, because \(X\) does not modify it; all the bias comes from \(A\) versus \(C\), which is only modestly imbalanced but is modified.

The cure. Reweighting the modified contrast to the target distribution replaces \(p_{AC}\) by \(p_{*}\): \(\int\tau_{AC}f_{*}=0.5(0.10)+0.5(0.30)=0.20\), and then \(0.20-0.15=0.05\) recovers the target exactly. That reweighting is what a population-adjusted method performs. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The corollary that follows is the single most useful practical fact in the chapter for an analyst: it says which covariates you actually have to worry about.

Before the algebra, the plain reason. In a contrast on a collapsible scale, any covariate effect that enters both arms by the same amount cancels: if a covariate raises the failure risk under \(A\) and under \(C\) equally, it leaves \(\mu_C-\mu_A\) untouched, so it cannot bias the \(A\)-versus-\(C\) contrast no matter how imbalanced it is. Only a covariate that changes the difference between the arms survives. That is the whole content of the corollary. The following one-line table is worth memorizing.

Covariate role Effect on the two arms Effect on the contrast \(\tau^g\) Anchored ITC consequence
Purely prognostic (e.g., a severity marker that raises failure risk under both \(A\) and \(C\) equally) shifts both arms by the same amount unchanged: it cancels imbalance is harmless; contributes no Bucher bias
Effect modifier (e.g., prior biologic exposure) shifts the arms by different amounts changes with the covariate imbalance biases the indirect comparison

Corollary 16.1 (Purely prognostic covariates contribute no Bucher bias) A covariate that is a prognostic variable (Definition 11.5) for the arms but is not an effect modifier of \(A\) versus \(C\) or of \(B\) versus \(C\) on scale \(g\) contributes zero to the bias Equation 16.6, however imbalanced its distribution across \(\mathcal P_{AC},\mathcal P_{BC},\mathcal P^{*}\).

[machine-checked: theories/EffectModifiers.v, Theorem anchored_PV_cancellation]

Proof. Decompose the covariate vector as \(X=(X_{\mathrm{EM}},W)\), where \(W\) is the purely prognostic component. By the definition of “not an effect modifier,” the conditional contrast does not depend on \(W\): \(\tau^g_{tC}(x_{\mathrm{EM}},w)=\tau^g_{tC}(x_{\mathrm{EM}})\) for all \(w\), for \(t\in\{A,B\}\). This is the content of anchored_PV_cancellation in the Coq development: if the contrast \(\mu_t(\cdot,w)-\mu_C(\cdot,w)\) on the model scale is constant in \(w\), it equals its value at any other \(w'\). Now write \(f_{tC}\) and \(f_{*}\) as joint densities of \((X_{\mathrm{EM}},W)\). Because \(\tau^g_{tC}\) does not depend on \(w\), we may interchange the order of integration and integrate out \(W\) first; this interchange is Fubini’s theorem, valid because \(\tau^g_{tC}\) is bounded on the support and the densities are integrable. Integrating each density’s \(W\)-slice down to its \(X_{\mathrm{EM}}\)-marginal \(f^{\mathrm{EM}}(x_{\mathrm{EM}})=\int f(x_{\mathrm{EM}},w)\,dw\), \[ b_{tC}=\int\tau^g_{tC}(x_{\mathrm{EM}})\Bigl[\int\bigl(f_{tC}(x_{\mathrm{EM}},w)-f_{*}(x_{\mathrm{EM}},w)\bigr)\,dw\Bigr]dx_{\mathrm{EM}} =\int\tau^g_{tC}(x_{\mathrm{EM}})\,[f^{\mathrm{EM}}_{tC}(x_{\mathrm{EM}})-f^{\mathrm{EM}}_{*}(x_{\mathrm{EM}})]\,dx_{\mathrm{EM}}, \] which depends only on the marginal distribution of the effect-modifying component \(X_{\mathrm{EM}}\). Any imbalance confined to \(W\) has integrated away and contributes nothing. \(\square\)

Corollary 16.1 is the rigorous form of the slogan that “prognostic variables cancel in anchored comparisons.” It is the precise reason an anchored adjustment need balance only the effect modifiers, a fact that Chapter 17 turns into the central anchored-consistency theorem and that makes anchored methods far less data-hungry than unanchored ones. The cancellation is purely a property of the contrast; it has no analogue when one compares absolute arm outcomes, which is why unanchored comparison (Chapter 26) must control prognostic variables too.

Remark. Warning: the standard error cannot see this bias. The bias Equation 16.6 is a deterministic functional of the true conditional contrasts and covariate distributions. It does not depend on the trial sample sizes and does not vanish as they grow; it is a bias, not a variance. The Bucher standard error of Theorem 14.2 estimates only the sampling variability of \(\hat d_{AC}\) and \(\hat d_{BC}\) around their own population means, so it is blind to Equation 16.6 entirely. A confidently narrow indirect confidence interval can be centered on the wrong number. This is the formal sense in which a transitivity failure is undetectable from the precision of the estimate alone: a tight interval buys you no protection here. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Warning: in the anchored triangle this bias is untestable. When the same contrast carries both direct and indirect evidence, the two can be compared, and a discrepancy signals that some constancy assumption has failed; this is the node-splitting and consistency machinery of Chapter 15 (Definition 15.4, Theorem 15.1). But in the minimal anchored triangle there is no direct \(A\)-versus-\(B\) evidence, nothing to compare the indirect estimate against, and Equation 16.6 is then untestable from the trial outcomes. It can be assessed only by examining the covariate distributions \(f_{AC},f_{BC},f_{*}\) and asking whether any effect modifier is imbalanced, which requires knowing which covariates modify the effect and on what scale. That is a substantive, external judgment, and the reason NICE Technical Support Document 18 (Phillippo et al. (2016)) insists that an indirect comparison justify constancy before relying on it. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

16.3.1 The non-collapsibility surcharge

So far we worked on a directly collapsible scale, where the trial-reported marginal contrast and the average-conditional contrast are one number. On a non-collapsible scale a second discrepancy appears, independent of any imbalance, and it is worth isolating because health researchers meet it constantly: everyone knows odds ratios “behave strangely.”

Remark. The non-collapsibility surcharge. Theorem 16.1 is exact for the average-conditional estimand Equation 16.4 on any scale, and on a directly collapsible scale (identity, log risk ratio) it is also the exact bias of the marginal contrast a trial reports, by Proposition 16.1. On a non-collapsible scale, such as the log odds ratio, the trial’s reported marginal contrast \(d_{tC(\mathcal P_j)}\) is not the average-conditional \(\tilde d_{tC(\mathcal P_j)}\), and a second discrepancy, the non-collapsibility gap, is added to Equation 16.6. That second gap is itself a Jensen gap for the nonlinear link. We illustrate it numerically just below, and the same mechanism reappears as ecological bias in Section 16.4. The two effects compound: an imbalanced effect modifier biases the indirect comparison, and a nonlinear link distorts what each trial reports in the first place. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

A numeric illustration in its purest form. Take a single trial, a treatment versus a control, and a binary covariate \(X\) balanced at \(\Pr(X=1)=0.5\), so there is no imbalance at all. Let the control risks be \(\mu_0(0)=0.1\) and \(\mu_0(1)=0.5\), and let the conditional odds ratio be a constant \(3\) in both strata, so \(X\) is not even an effect modifier. Solving for the treated risks gives \(\mu_1(0)=0.25\) and \(\mu_1(1)=0.75\), and the conditional log odds ratio is \(\log 3=1.0986\) in each stratum. The marginalized arm risks are \(\bar\mu_0=(0.1+0.5)/2=0.30\) and \(\bar\mu_1=(0.25+0.75)/2=0.50\), so the marginal odds ratio is \[ \frac{0.50/0.50}{0.30/0.70}=\frac{1}{3/7}=\frac{7}{3}\approx 2.33,\qquad \log\frac{7}{3}=0.8473 . \] Even with the conditional odds ratio identical in both strata and the covariate perfectly balanced, the marginal odds ratio is \(2.33\), not \(3\): the marginal log odds ratio \(0.847\) sits below the conditional \(1.099\). That gap, about \(0.25\), is pure non-collapsibility; it owes nothing to imbalance and would be present in a single perfectly balanced trial.

The two problems are genuinely separate, and it helps to tabulate them.

Source of discrepancy What it is When it appears Scale dependence
Effect-modifier imbalance (Bucher bias) Equation 16.6: trial and target case mixes differ on a modified contrast whenever an effect modifier is imbalanced across trial and target present on every scale, including collapsible ones
Non-collapsibility surcharge the marginal (trial-reported) contrast differs from the average-conditional contrast whenever the link is nonlinear (odds ratio, hazard ratio), even with balanced covariates only on non-collapsible scales

In a real anchored odds-ratio comparison the two effects add: each trial’s reported marginal log odds ratio already differs from its average-conditional log odds ratio by a non-collapsibility gap (this illustration), and then the imbalance of effect modifiers biases the subtraction (Equation 16.6). Exercise 16.7 asks you to compute both for the psoriasis network of Example 16.2 and confirm that the total discrepancy exceeds the \(-0.06\) of the risk-difference analysis.

16.4 Aggregate meta-regression and ecological bias

Theorem 16.1 shows that the cure is to reweight each conditional contrast to the target covariate distribution. The obstacle, in the typical health-technology-assessment setting, is that the analyst has individual patient data (IPD) for at most one trial and only aggregate data (AgD), the covariate means \(\bar{\mathbf x}_j\) and arm-level outcome summaries, for the others. A natural idea is to sidestep the need for IPD by regressing trial-level treatment effects on trial-level covariate means, an aggregate-level meta-regression, and to use the fitted slope to adjust for imbalance. This section proves that the idea fails: the aggregate slope is not the individual-level effect-modifier coefficient, and the error has a definite source and sign. The phenomenon is the ecological fallacy, and the error is ecological bias.

The analyst’s temptation, and the two slopes it confuses. Put yourself in the analyst’s chair: “I do not have patient-level data for the other trial, but I do have its mean covariate, say mean prior-exposure prevalence or mean age. If I collect several trials, each reporting a covariate mean \(\bar x_j\) and a treatment effect \(D_j\), surely the slope of \(D_j\) against \(\bar x_j\) tells me how the effect changes with the covariate?” Concretely, suppose three trials report

trial covariate mean \(\bar x_j\) trial contrast \(D_j\)
1 \(0.0\) \(0.332\)
2 \(0.5\) \(0.427\)
3 \(1.0\) \(0.521\)

The fitted between-trial slope is \((0.521-0.332)/1.0\approx 0.19\). Call it \(b_2\). The temptation is to read \(b_2\) as the effect-modification coefficient. But there are two different objects here:

  • \(b_2\) is the between-trial slope: how a trial’s aggregate contrast changes as the trial’s mean covariate changes, read off aggregate summaries.
  • \(\beta_2\) is the within-patient interaction: how one patient’s contrast changes as that patient’s own covariate changes, a coefficient in the individual-level model.

Theorem 16.2 proves that these are not equal; \(b_2\) is a strictly shrunken image of \(\beta_2\). In the numbers above the true \(\beta_2\) is \(0.6\), not \(0.19\). The root cause is one line of calculus: the aggregate arm mean is an average of a nonlinear inverse link over the covariate distribution, and that is not the inverse link evaluated at the average covariate.

Lemma 16.3 (The aggregation gap is a Jensen gap) Let the individual-level model have link \(g\) and a linear predictor \(\eta_t(x)\) that is affine in \(x\) (that is, of the form \(a+b^{\top}x\), linear plus a constant), so the conditional mean of arm \(t\) is \(\mu_t(x)=g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))\). For a population with covariate density \(f\) and mean \(\bar x=\int x\,f(x)\,dx\), \[ \int g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))\,f(x)\,dx \;\neq\; g^{-1}\!\Bigl(\int\eta_t(x)\,f(x)\,dx\Bigr)=g^{-1}(\eta_t(\bar x)) \qquad\text{in general.} \tag{16.7}\] If \(g^{-1}\) is convex the left side is the larger; if \(g^{-1}\) is concave the right side is the larger; if \(g^{-1}\) is affine on the range of \(\eta_t\), the two are equal for every \(f\). When \(g^{-1}\) is strictly convex or strictly concave on that range and \(X\) is non-degenerate, the inequality is strict.

Proof. Because \(\eta_t\) is affine, \(\int\eta_t(x)f(x)\,dx=\eta_t(\bar x)\) exactly, which gives the displayed equality on the right. Apply Jensen’s inequality (Theorem 4.6) to the random variable \(\eta_t(X)\) with the convex function \(\varphi=g^{-1}\): \(\varphi(\mathbb E[\eta_t(X)])\le\mathbb E[\varphi(\eta_t(X))]\), that is \(g^{-1}(\eta_t(\bar x))\le\int g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))f(x)\,dx\), with strict inequality when \(g^{-1}\) is strictly convex and \(\eta_t(X)\) is non-degenerate, the latter holding when \(X\) is non-degenerate and the affine map \(\eta_t\) is non-constant. The concave case is the same inequality reversed, applied to \(-g^{-1}\). If \(g^{-1}\) is affine on the range of \(\eta_t\) then it commutes with the expectation and the two sides agree for every \(f\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. The load-bearing move is that Jensen is applied to the random variable \(\eta_t(X)\), the linear predictor, not to \(X\) itself; the convex function is the inverse link \(g^{-1}\). In plain words: with a curved inverse link, the average of the patients’ risks is not the risk of the average patient. Evaluating the model at the mean covariate (the “plug-in”) therefore misses the true aggregate mean whenever the link is nonlinear.

A one-line numeric check. Take the probit inverse link \(g^{-1}=\Phi\) and \(\eta(X)=0.5+X\) with \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). The true aggregate mean, using the Gaussian identity Equation 16.10 below, is \(\mathbb E[\Phi(0.5+X)]=\Phi(0.5/\sqrt{2})=\Phi(0.354)\approx 0.638\), whereas the plug-in value is \(\Phi(0.5)\approx 0.691\). The plug-in overstates the aggregate risk by about five percentage points; that gap is the Jensen gap, and it is exactly the quantity we now name.

The only link for which Equation 16.7 is an equality for all covariate distributions is the one whose inverse is affine, namely the identity link, where \(g^{-1}\) is the identity map. For every nonlinear link, the logit with \(g^{-1}(\eta)=e^{\eta}/(1+e^{\eta})\), the log with \(g^{-1}(\eta)=e^{\eta}\), the probit with \(g^{-1}=\Phi\), the aggregation gap is generically nonzero. We name it.

Definition 16.2 (Ecological (aggregation) bias) Let an individual-level outcome model have link \(g\) and a linear predictor \(\eta_t(x)\) affine in the covariates \(x\), so that the conditional mean of arm \(t\) is \(\mu_t(x)=g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))\). For a population with covariate density \(f\) and mean \(\bar x=\int x\,f(x)\,dx\), the aggregate (population-averaged) arm mean is \[ \bar\mu_t \;=\; \int g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))\,f(x)\,dx , \] and the plug-in arm mean is \(g^{-1}(\eta_t(\bar x))\), obtained by evaluating the individual model at the mean covariate. The ecological bias (or aggregation bias) of arm \(t\) in this population is the discrepancy between them, \[ \mathrm{EB}_t(f) \;=\; g^{-1}(\eta_t(\bar x))-\int g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))\,f(x)\,dx \;=\; g^{-1}(\eta_t(\bar x))-\bar\mu_t . \tag{16.8}\] At the level of the relative effect, suppose the individual interaction slope of the contrast \(\eta_1(x)-\eta_0(x)\) in \(x\) is \(\beta_2\) (the effect-modification coefficient of Lemma 11.2), and let \(b_2\) be the slope in \(\bar x\) of the aggregate marginal contrast \(g(\bar\mu_1)-g(\bar\mu_0)\) regarded as a function of the covariate mean across populations. The ecological bias of the effect-modification coefficient is \[ \mathrm{EB}_{\mathrm{coef}} \;=\; b_2-\beta_2 , \tag{16.9}\] the difference between the between-trial (ecological) slope \(b_2\) and the within-patient interaction \(\beta_2\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

By Lemma 16.3 the arm-level ecological bias Equation 16.8 is nonzero whenever the link is nonlinear and the covariate is non-degenerate, with sign fixed by the convexity of \(g^{-1}\). The ecological bias of the coefficient Equation 16.9 is the consequential one for indirect comparison, because it is the slope \(\beta_2\), the strength of effect modification, that the adjustment in Theorem 16.1 needs. We now compute \(b_2\) in closed form for the probit link and a normal covariate, the one case where the aggregation integral Equation 16.8 is elementary, and read off the bias exactly.

First the computational tool. The next lemma is a self-contained calculation; we flag it as such and use it only inside the theorem that follows.

Lemma 16.4 (The Gaussian probit integral) Let \(Z\sim\mathcal N(m,\sigma^2)\) and let \(a,b\in\mathbb R\). Then \[ \mathbb E[\Phi(a+bZ)] \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{a+bm}{\sqrt{1+b^2\sigma^2}}\right). \tag{16.10}\]

Proof. Recall the meaning of \(\Phi\): for a standard normal \(U\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), the value \(\Phi(t)=P(U\le t)\) is literally the probability that \(U\) falls at or below \(t\). Let \(U\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) be independent of \(Z\). Then for fixed \(Z\) this reading gives \(\Phi(a+bZ)=P(U\le a+bZ\mid Z)\). Taking expectations over \(Z\) and using the law of total probability, \[ \mathbb E[\Phi(a+bZ)]=P(U\le a+bZ)=P(U-bZ\le a). \] The variable \(U-bZ\) is a linear combination of independent normals. Its moment generating function is, by Theorem 4.12 applied to \(U\) and \(-bZ\), \[ M_{U-bZ}(s)=M_U(s)\,M_{-bZ}(s)=e^{\frac12 s^2}\cdot e^{-bms+\frac12 b^2\sigma^2 s^2}=\exp\!\Bigl(-bm\,s+\tfrac12(1+b^2\sigma^2)s^2\Bigr), \] which is the moment generating function of \(\mathcal N(-bm,\,1+b^2\sigma^2)\); by uniqueness (Theorem 4.11) that is the law of \(U-bZ\). Standardizing, \[ P(U-bZ\le a)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-(-bm)}{\sqrt{1+b^2\sigma^2}}\right)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{a+bm}{\sqrt{1+b^2\sigma^2}}\right), \] which is Equation 16.10. \(\square\)

We can now state the attenuation theorem. Because several symbols enter at once, here is the map to keep beside the statement.

Symbol Meaning
\(a_0,a_1\) arm intercepts (control, treated) on the probit scale
\(\rho_0,\rho_1\) arm slopes in the covariate \(x\) on the probit scale
\(\beta_2=\rho_1-\rho_0\) within-patient effect-modification coefficient (the target quantity)
\(b_2\) between-trial (ecological) slope from aggregate meta-regression
\(\sigma^2\) common within-trial covariate variance
\(\bar x_j\) covariate mean of trial \(j\)
\(\Phi,\Phi^{-1}\) standard normal distribution function (the probit inverse link) and its inverse

Theorem 16.2 (Aggregate meta-regression does not recover the individual effect-modifier coefficient) Let the individual-level model be probit, \(\Phi^{-1}(\mu_t(x))=\eta_t(x)\), with arm-specific affine linear predictors \[ \eta_0(x)=a_0+\rho_0 x,\qquad \eta_1(x)=a_1+\rho_1 x , \] so the individual-level effect-modification coefficient is \(\beta_2=\rho_1-\rho_0\). Suppose each trial \(j\) has a normal within-trial covariate distribution \(X\sim\mathcal N(\bar x_j,\sigma^2)\) with a common variance \(\sigma^2\). Then the aggregate marginal contrast on the probit scale is an affine function of the covariate mean, \[ \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{1j})-\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{0j}) \;=\; c+b_2\,\bar x_j , \tag{16.11}\] with intercept and slope \[ c=\frac{a_1}{\sqrt{1+\rho_1^2\sigma^2}}-\frac{a_0}{\sqrt{1+\rho_0^2\sigma^2}}, \qquad b_2=\frac{\rho_1}{\sqrt{1+\rho_1^2\sigma^2}}-\frac{\rho_0}{\sqrt{1+\rho_0^2\sigma^2}} . \tag{16.12}\] The ecological slope \(b_2\) equals the individual coefficient \(\beta_2=\rho_1-\rho_0\) if and only if \(\sigma^2=0\) or \(\rho_0=\rho_1\). Whenever \(\sigma^2>0\) and \(\rho_0\neq\rho_1\), the ecological slope is a strict attenuation toward zero: \(0<b_2<\beta_2\) if \(\beta_2>0\), and \(\beta_2<b_2<0\) if \(\beta_2<0\).

Proof. By Lemma 16.4 with \(Z=X\sim\mathcal N(\bar x_j,\sigma^2)\), \(a=a_t\), \(b=\rho_t\), \[ \bar\mu_{tj}=\mathbb E[\Phi(a_t+\rho_t X)]=\Phi\!\left(\frac{a_t+\rho_t\bar x_j}{\sqrt{1+\rho_t^2\sigma^2}}\right), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{tj})=\frac{a_t+\rho_t\bar x_j}{\sqrt{1+\rho_t^2\sigma^2}} , \] applying \(\Phi^{-1}\) to both sides. Differencing the arms, \[ \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{1j})-\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{0j}) =\frac{a_1+\rho_1\bar x_j}{\sqrt{1+\rho_1^2\sigma^2}}-\frac{a_0+\rho_0\bar x_j}{\sqrt{1+\rho_0^2\sigma^2}} =\underbrace{\Bigl(\tfrac{a_1}{\sqrt{1+\rho_1^2\sigma^2}}-\tfrac{a_0}{\sqrt{1+\rho_0^2\sigma^2}}\Bigr)}_{c} +\underbrace{\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_1}{\sqrt{1+\rho_1^2\sigma^2}}-\tfrac{\rho_0}{\sqrt{1+\rho_0^2\sigma^2}}\Bigr)}_{b_2}\bar x_j, \] which is Equation 16.11 with the coefficients Equation 16.12; the relationship is affine in \(\bar x_j\) because \(\sigma^2\) is common across trials.

Write \(h(\rho)=\rho/\sqrt{1+\rho^2\sigma^2}\), so that \(b_2=h(\rho_1)-h(\rho_0)\) and \(\beta_2=\rho_1-\rho_0\). Differentiating by the quotient rule, \[ h'(\rho)=\frac{\sqrt{1+\rho^2\sigma^2}-\rho\cdot\frac{\rho\sigma^2}{\sqrt{1+\rho^2\sigma^2}}}{1+\rho^2\sigma^2} . \] Multiplying numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{1+\rho^2\sigma^2}\) clears the inner fraction, and the numerator becomes \((1+\rho^2\sigma^2)-\rho^2\sigma^2=1\), so \[ h'(\rho)=\frac{(1+\rho^2\sigma^2)-\rho^2\sigma^2}{(1+\rho^2\sigma^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{1}{(1+\rho^2\sigma^2)^{3/2}} , \] whence \(0<h'(\rho)\le 1\) for all \(\rho\), with \(h'(\rho)=1\) only at \(\rho=0\) (assuming \(\sigma^2>0\)). By the fundamental theorem of calculus, for \(\rho_1>\rho_0\), \[ b_2=h(\rho_1)-h(\rho_0)=\int_{\rho_0}^{\rho_1}h'(\rho)\,d\rho . \] Since \(0<h'(\rho)\le1\) with equality only on the single point \(\rho=0\), the integrand is positive, giving \(b_2>0\); and it is strictly below \(1\) except at that one point. A single point has Lebesgue measure zero, so it does not affect the value of the integral, and therefore \(b_2<\int_{\rho_0}^{\rho_1}1\,d\rho=\rho_1-\rho_0=\beta_2\). Hence \(0<b_2<\beta_2\) when \(\beta_2>0\); the case \(\beta_2<0\) is identical with the inequalities reversed. If \(\sigma^2=0\) then \(h'(\rho)\equiv1\) and \(b_2=\rho_1-\rho_0=\beta_2\); if \(\rho_0=\rho_1\) then \(\beta_2=0\) and \(b_2=h(\rho_0)-h(\rho_0)=0=\beta_2\). These are the only equality cases. \(\square\)

Why this matters. The slope Equation 16.12 is the ecological coefficient an analyst would obtain by regressing the aggregate probit contrasts on the trial covariate means, and Theorem 16.2 shows it is a shrunk image of the true within-patient interaction \(\beta_2\), with shrinkage controlled by the within-trial spread \(\sigma^2\). An analyst who reads the between-trial slope \(b_2\) as the effect-modification coefficient underestimates the modification, and so under-corrects the Bucher bias. We put numbers on it before drawing the two general consequences.

Example 16.3 (Ecological attenuation of an effect-modifier coefficient) Take the probit model of Theorem 16.2 with one covariate and parameters \[ \alpha=-0.5,\quad \beta_1=0.8,\quad \gamma=0.4,\quad \beta_2=0.6, \] in the parameterization \(\Phi^{-1}(\mu_t(x))=\alpha+\beta_1 x+(\gamma+\beta_2 x)t\), so the arm linear predictors are \(\eta_0(x)=-0.5+0.8x\) and \(\eta_1(x)=-0.1+1.4x\), with slopes \(\rho_0=0.8\) and \(\rho_1=\beta_1+\beta_2=1.4\). The individual-level effect-modification coefficient is \(\beta_2=0.6\). Let the common within-trial covariate variance be \(\sigma^2=1\).

Shrinkage factors. From Equation 16.12, \[ \sqrt{1+\rho_0^2\sigma^2}=\sqrt{1.64}=1.28062,\qquad \sqrt{1+\rho_1^2\sigma^2}=\sqrt{2.96}=1.72047 , \] \[ h(\rho_0)=\frac{0.8}{1.28062}=0.62470,\qquad h(\rho_1)=\frac{1.4}{1.72047}=0.81373 . \]

Ecological coefficient. \[ b_2=h(\rho_1)-h(\rho_0)=0.81373-0.62470=0.18903 . \] An aggregate meta-regression would estimate the effect-modification coefficient as \(0.189\), against the true individual value \(\beta_2=0.6\): an attenuation to about \(31.5\%\) of its size. The ecological bias of the coefficient is \(b_2-\beta_2=0.189-0.600=-0.411\). These are the numbers that appear in the illustrative table at the start of Section 16.4.

Two trials, checked directly. With intercepts \(a_0=\alpha=-0.5\) and \(a_1=\alpha+\gamma=-0.1\), a trial with covariate mean \(\bar x=0\) has \[ \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_0)=\frac{-0.5}{1.28062}=-0.39044,\qquad \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_1)=\frac{-0.1}{1.72047}=-0.05813, \] so its aggregate probit contrast is \(-0.05813-(-0.39044)=0.33231\). A trial with \(\bar x=1\) has \[ \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_0)=\frac{0.3}{1.28062}=0.23426,\qquad \Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_1)=\frac{1.3}{1.72047}=0.75560, \] with contrast \(0.75560-0.23426=0.52134\). The slope between the two trials is \(0.52134-0.33231=0.18903=b_2\), confirming Equation 16.11.

The Jensen gap, concretely. At \(\bar x=0\) the aggregate control-arm mean is \(\bar\mu_0=\Phi(-0.39044)=0.34811\), whereas the plug-in value is \(\Phi(\eta_0(0))=\Phi(-0.5)=0.30854\). The arm-level ecological bias is \(\mathrm{EB}_0=0.30854-0.34811=-0.03957\) of Equation 16.8: evaluating the individual model at the mean covariate understates the true aggregate event probability by about four percentage points.

Against the average-conditional contrast. The average-conditional contrast in a trial with mean \(\bar x\) is \(\gamma+\beta_2\bar x=0.4+0.6\bar x\), with slope \(0.6=\beta_2\) exactly. At \(\bar x=0\) it is \(0.40\) versus the marginal \(0.33231\), and at \(\bar x=1\) it is \(1.00\) versus the marginal \(0.52134\). The gap, \(0.06769\) at \(\bar x=0\) growing to \(0.47866\) at \(\bar x=1\), is the non-collapsibility gap, and its growth is precisely what bends the marginal slope down from \(0.60\) to \(0.189\); Proposition 16.3 below makes this identity exact. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Two consequences deserve emphasis, and the first is important enough to state as a proposition.

Proposition 16.3 (Ecological bias and non-collapsibility are the same Jensen gap) In the probit model of Theorem 16.2, the average-conditional contrast in trial \(j\) is affine in \(\bar x_j\) with slope exactly \(\beta_2\), while the marginal (aggregate) contrast Equation 16.11 is affine in \(\bar x_j\) with the strictly smaller slope \(b_2\). The difference between the two contrasts, in each single population, is the non-collapsibility gap of Definition 7.7; equivalently it is the arm-differenced Jensen gap of Lemma 16.3. Ecological bias of the coefficient (Equation 16.9) is thus non-collapsibility applied to the slope of the effect-modification function.

Proof. By linearity of the linear predictor, the average-conditional contrast is \[ \tilde d_{10(\mathcal P_j)}=\mathbb E_{f_j}[\eta_1(X)-\eta_0(X)]=(a_1-a_0)+(\rho_1-\rho_0)\bar x_j=\gamma+\beta_2\bar x_j, \] with \(\gamma=a_1-a_0\), so its slope in \(\bar x_j\) is \(\beta_2\). The marginal contrast is \(\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{1j})-\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{0j})=c+b_2\bar x_j\) by Equation 16.11, with \(b_2<\beta_2\) by Theorem 16.2. Their difference in population \(j\) is \[ \bigl[\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{1j})-\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{0j})\bigr]-\tilde d_{10(\mathcal P_j)} =\bigl[\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{1j})-\mathbb E_{f_j}\eta_1\bigr]-\bigl[\Phi^{-1}(\bar\mu_{0j})-\mathbb E_{f_j}\eta_0\bigr], \] and each bracket is, up to sign, the arm-level gap between \(g\) applied to the aggregate mean and the average of \(g\) applied pointwise, that is the arm-level Jensen gap of Lemma 16.3 and the non-collapsibility gap of Definition 7.7 for that arm. The contrast-level gap is their difference, and since it equals \((c-\gamma)+(b_2-\beta_2)\bar x_j\), its slope in \(\bar x_j\) is exactly the coefficient-level ecological bias \(b_2-\beta_2\) of Equation 16.9. \(\square\)

Remark. Aggregate data do not carry the missing information. The shrinkage depends on \(\sigma^2\), the within-trial variance of the covariate. If \(\sigma^2\) varied across trials, Equation 16.11 would not even be affine in \(\bar x_j\), and no single ecological slope would exist. The dependence on a within-trial moment that aggregate means do not report is the precise reason aggregate meta-regression cannot recover \(\beta_2\): the quantity it needs has been integrated away in forming the trial summaries. Only patient-level data, where \(\sigma^2\) and the individual interaction are both visible, restore it. This is the information-theoretic core of why population adjustment requires IPD from at least one trial. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The constant term shows the same phenomenon. Proposition 16.3 is about the slope; the intercept tells the same story. When there is no effect modification, \(\rho_0=\rho_1=\rho\) and Equation 16.12 gives \(b_2=0\) but \(c=(a_1-a_0)/\sqrt{1+\rho^2\sigma^2}=\gamma/\sqrt{1+\rho^2\sigma^2}<\gamma\): the marginal probit effect is strictly attenuated from the conditional effect \(\gamma\) even with a constant conditional contrast. That is non-collapsibility of the probit in its purest form, the intercept-level twin of the surcharge illustration in Section 16.3.1. Both the slope shrinkage and the intercept shrinkage are instances of Lemma 16.3 for the nonlinear inverse link. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

16.5 Reading the two failures side by side

The two worked examples, Example 16.2 and Example 16.3, are the two failures of this chapter in miniature. Placing them side by side isolates what each one teaches and shows that they are two faces of a single problem: an averaging done in the wrong place.

Feature Bucher failure (Example 16.2) Ecological failure (Example 16.3)
Scale risk difference (directly collapsible) probit (non-collapsible)
What breaks an effect modifier is imbalanced across trials the aggregate slope shrinks the interaction
Data assumed conditional contrasts known only trial means and trial contrasts
Symptom indirect estimate has the wrong sign (\(-0.01\) vs \(+0.05\)) \(b_2=0.189\) against \(\beta_2=0.60\)

Both are averaging failures. The first averages the correct conditional contrast against the wrong covariate distribution, so it is repaired by changing the distribution you average against (reweight to \(f_{*}\)). The second destroys the conditional contrast by averaging through a nonlinear link before differencing, so it is repaired by keeping the individual model and integrating it honestly. Population adjustment does both at once: it estimates the conditional contrast from IPD and integrates it over the target population. That is the subject of the next section and of Part IV.

16.6 The gap that population adjustment fills

The two failures of this chapter have a common diagnosis, and it is the diagnosis that defines Part IV. Standard NMA fails because it averages the wrong conditional effects over the wrong populations: it subtracts each trial’s marginal effect, which is the trial’s own conditional contrast averaged over the trial’s own covariate distribution, and neither the contrast nor the distribution is the one the target estimand requires. Population-adjusted methods are needed because they estimate conditional effects and average them over the target population.

The failure-of-Bucher theorem (Theorem 16.1) says that an unadjusted indirect comparison is biased by \(\int\tau^g_{AC}(f_{AC}-f_{*})-\int\tau^g_{BC}(f_{BC}-f_{*})\), and that the bias is removed by re-expressing each trial’s conditional contrast as an average against the target covariate distribution \(f_{*}\) rather than the trial’s own. Two ingredients are needed to perform that re-expression: the conditional contrast function \(\tau^g_{tC}(\cdot)\), which is the effect-modification structure of the treatment, and the target distribution \(f_{*}\). The ecological-bias theorem (Theorem 16.2) says that the first ingredient cannot be recovered from aggregate data alone, because aggregating a nonlinear link over a covariate distribution destroys exactly the within-trial information that pins down the interaction slope \(\beta_2\). The remedy must therefore (i) estimate \(\tau^g_{tC}(\cdot)\), and in particular its effect-modification slope, from individual patient data in at least one trial, and (ii) integrate the resulting individual model over the target covariate distribution, never substituting the target mean into the individual model, since by Lemma 16.3 that substitution is itself biased.

Remark. What a valid indirect comparison must compute. This is the checklist Part IV builds on.

  1. The conditional contrast function \(\tau^g_{tC}(\cdot)\) (the effect-modification structure), estimated from individual patient data in at least one trial. Aggregate data cannot recover it (Theorem 16.2).
  2. The target covariate distribution \(f_{*}\), with the individual model integrated against it, never the target mean plugged into the model (Lemma 16.3).

A method that supplies both closes the gap; a method that supplies neither is standard NMA. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The three methods of Part IV and Part V realize the two ingredients differently; each bullet ends with the data it needs.

  • Matching-adjusted indirect comparison (Chapter 18) reweights the IPD trial’s individuals so that their weighted covariate moments match the aggregate trial’s, then forms the contrast on the reweighted sample. The weights perform the integration against \(f_{*}\) implicitly; the effective sample size (Theorem 4.7, the Cauchy-Schwarz bound on \((\sum w_i)^2/\sum w_i^2\)) measures how much precision the reweighting costs. Data needed: IPD in one study and aggregate covariate moments in the target.
  • Simulated treatment comparison (Chapter 19) fits the conditional outcome model \(\mu_t(\cdot)\) on the IPD trial and then evaluates it at the aggregate trial’s covariate distribution, integrating the model rather than plugging in the mean, which is the correct response to Lemma 16.3. Data needed: an IPD-fitted outcome model plus the target covariate summaries or distribution.
  • Multilevel network meta-regression (Chapter 20) writes the aggregate likelihood as the exact integral of the individual model over the within-study covariate distribution, \(\bar\mu_{tj}=\int g^{-1}(\eta_t(x))f_j(x)\,dx\), the very integral of Equation 16.8, and estimates the shared individual-level coefficients, including \(\beta_2\), from the IPD and AgD studies jointly. It is the method that confronts the aggregation gap head on by computing it instead of ignoring it. Data needed: IPD and AgD modeled jointly, with the covariate distribution of every study.

Each method, in its own idiom, supplies the conditional contrast and the target integration that Theorem 16.1 and Theorem 16.2 prove are necessary. That is the sense in which the limits established in this chapter are not a dead end but a specification: they say exactly what a valid indirect comparison must compute, and the rest of the book is the construction and analysis of methods that compute it.
