13  Pairwise Meta-Analysis

Meta-analysis is the arithmetic of evidence synthesis. Several studies have each estimated the same treatment effect, and we wish to combine their estimates into one summary that is more precise than any single trial and that lays bare whether the studies agree. This chapter develops that arithmetic from first principles. It is the foundation of every later chapter: the indirect comparison of Chapter 14 is a meta-analysis of contrasts, the network meta-analysis of Chapter 15 is a multivariate meta-analysis, and the population-adjusted methods of Parts IV and V are meta-analyses in which the within-study estimates are first recalibrated to a common population. Master the pairwise case and the rest is structure built on top of it.

We work throughout with the two-stage view. In the first stage, each study \(i\) has been analyzed on its own and has produced a point estimate \(\hat\theta_i\) of an effect (a log odds ratio, a log hazard ratio, a mean difference, whatever the outcome scale demands) together with an estimate \(s_i^2\) of the variance of that point estimate. In the second stage, which is the subject of this chapter, we treat the pairs \((\hat\theta_i, s_i^2)\), \(i = 1, \dots, k\), as the data and combine them. The within-study variances \(s_i^2\) are treated as known constants. This is an idealization: in reality \(s_i^2\) is itself estimated from the \(n_i\) patients of study \(i\). The idealization is excellent when the \(n_i\) are large, because then \(s_i^2\) is estimated with negligible relative error, and it is the standard convention in the meta-analysis and network meta-analysis literature (Dias et al. (2018)). We adopt it and flag, where it matters, what changes when it is relaxed.

Remark. Indexing convention for this chapter. The unit of analysis here is the study, and we follow the universal meta-analysis convention of letting \(i = 1, \dots, k\) index the \(k\) studies. This differs from the book-wide convention of Notation, where \(j\) indexes studies and \(i\) indexes individuals within a study; that convention returns in Chapter 15, where studies again contain many individuals. Within the present chapter each study contributes exactly one summary estimate, so a single index suffices and we use \(i\) for it. Mnemonic: in this chapter a study is a single data point, so the lone index does double duty; wherever you see \(i = 1, \dots, k\), read it as “study \(i\) of \(k\).” The symbols \(\tau^2\) (between-study variance), \(w_i = 1/s_i^2\) (inverse-variance weight), and \(\tilde w_i = 1/(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\) (random-effects weight) are used in the standard meta-analytic sense. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Two orientations before the mathematics begins, both aimed at a reader who runs meta-analyses in software but has not seen one built from the ground up.

Reader translation of the two-stage view. Stage 1 happens inside each trial: a statistician fits a model (a logistic regression for a binary outcome, a Cox model for time to event, a linear model for a continuous outcome) and reports two numbers, a point estimate \(\hat\theta_i\) and its standard error. Squaring the standard error gives \(s_i^2\). Stage 2, the whole of this chapter, never revisits the patients; it works only with the pairs \((\hat\theta_i, s_i^2)\). So \(s_i^2\) is “the square of the standard error you read off the trial report,” and treating it as known is the statement that we trust each trial’s own reported precision. The one place this idealization bites is the Hartung-Knapp interval of Section Section 13.7, which exists precisely because pretending we know more than we do makes intervals too narrow when the trials are few.

Why we pool on the link (log) scale. The estimate \(\hat\theta_i\) is almost never an odds ratio or a hazard ratio itself; it is the logarithm of one, or a raw mean difference. There are two reasons, one statistical and one mathematical. Statistically, a log odds ratio is far closer to normally distributed, and its confidence interval far more symmetric, than the odds ratio itself, so the Gaussian machinery below is trustworthy on the log scale and not on the ratio scale. Mathematically, the odds ratio and hazard ratio are non-collapsible: the population-average ratio is not the average of subgroup ratios, so averaging ratios directly does not even estimate a well-defined effect (Definition 11.6, Theorem 11.2). Throughout the chapter \(\theta\) is a contrast on the linear-predictor scale (the “link scale”); the word contrast simply means a difference of two effects, here two linear predictors. Keep in mind that the pooled \(\theta\) inherits whatever scale the inputs were on, and that this choice of scale is exactly what the later ITC chapters must respect.

13.1 The fixed-effect model and inverse-variance weighting

The simplest assumption is that every study estimates one and the same effect \(\theta\), and that the studies differ only through sampling error.

Definition 13.1 (Fixed-effect meta-analysis model) The fixed-effect model for \(k\) study estimates is \[ \hat\theta_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i] = 0, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = s_i^2, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0 \ (i \ne j), \tag{13.1}\] where \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}\) is the common effect and the within-study variances \(s_i^2 > 0\) are known. When distributional results are needed we add the Gaussian assumption \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s_i^2)\), independently across \(i\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The fixed-effect model makes a strong claim: there is one number \(\theta\) (say, one true log odds ratio for \(B\) versus \(A\)), and the five trials differ only because each saw a finite sample. A fair question is “the same effect for whom, and on what scale?” The answer is: the same effect on the chosen link scale, in whatever common population the trials are taken to share. If that claim is false, the model is misspecified, and the rest of the chapter (Cochran’s \(Q\), then random effects) is the diagnosis and the repair. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Write \(\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_k)\) for the inverse-variance weights \[ w_i = \frac{1}{s_i^2}, \qquad W = \sum_{i=1}^k w_i , \] and define the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) estimator \[ \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i \hat\theta_i}{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i} = \frac{1}{W}\sum_{i=1}^k w_i \hat\theta_i . \tag{13.2}\]

Remark. Why these weights? (intuition before the theorem.) Inverse-variance weighting says: trust each trial in proportion to \(1/s_i^2\), its precision. Why the reciprocal variance, rather than the reciprocal standard error, the sample size, or a quality score? Because variance is the currency in which independent estimates combine. If you average with weights \(a_i\) summing to one, the variance of the average is \(\sum_i a_i^2 s_i^2\), and minimizing that forces \(a_i \propto 1/s_i^2\). Concretely, in the running trio study \(C\) has \(s_C^2 = 0.01\) and study \(D\) has \(s_D^2 = 0.04\): \(D\)’s estimate is four times as variable, so \(C\) earns four times the weight (\(w_C = 100\) versus \(w_D = 25\)). In standard-error units \(C\) fluctuates half as much as \(D\) (\(0.10\) versus \(0.20\)), and squaring that halving is exactly the factor of four; reciprocal standard error would give \(C\) only twice \(D\)’s weight, which does not minimize the variance. On the running trio \(C, D, E\) the total weight is \(W = 137.5\) and \[ \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} = \frac{100(0.15) + 25(0.60) + 12.5(0.50)}{137.5} = \frac{36.25}{137.5} = 0.264, \] with standard error \(1/\sqrt{137.5} = 0.085\): the precise study \(C\) alone carries \(100/137.5 = 73\%\) of the weight and pulls the summary down toward its own \(0.15\). The next theorem makes “minimizes the variance” precise and proves these weights optimal. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The next theorem is the meta-analytic incarnation of the Gauss-Markov theorem (Theorem 6.3): among all unbiased estimators that are linear in the data, the IVW estimator has the smallest variance.

Theorem 13.1 (Inverse-variance weighting is best linear unbiased) Under the fixed-effect model Equation 13.1, consider the class of linear estimators \(\hat\theta = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i \hat\theta_i\) with fixed coefficients \(a_i\). Such an estimator is unbiased for \(\theta\) for every value of \(\theta\) if and only if \(\sum_i a_i = 1\). Among all linear unbiased estimators, the IVW estimator Equation 13.2 is the unique minimizer of the variance, and \[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}) = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i} = \frac{1}{W} . \tag{13.3}\] (Corresponds to C.1 in the proof catalogue.)

Remark. Why this matters, and how to read the proof. “Best linear unbiased” (BLUE) is the principled answer to “why these weights and not others”: among all ways of averaging the trials without introducing bias, inverse-variance weighting is the uniquely most precise. This is why the inverse-variance default in standard meta-analysis software is not arbitrary, and it is the very same optimality that will justify the contrast weights of the Bucher indirect comparison in Chapter 14 and the network estimator of Chapter 15. Two proofs follow. The second, by Cauchy-Schwarz, is the short transparent one, and a reader meeting this material for the first time may prefer to read it first. The first connects the result to the regression theory of Part I, and uses three pieces of vocabulary worth pinning down in advance: \(\mathbf{1}_k\) is the \(k\)-vector of all ones (the design matrix of a single common mean); “\(\mathbf{S} \succ 0\)” means the covariance matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) is positive definite, which here just says every \(s_i^2 > 0\); and to whiten is to rescale each study’s equation by \(1/s_i\) so every error has variance \(1\), converting the unequal-variance (heteroscedastic) problem into the equal-variance one that Gauss-Markov handles. The step calling \(\mathbf{a} \mapsto \mathbf{S}^{1/2}\mathbf{a}\) a “bijection” is only saying the rescaling is invertible, so every estimator before whitening corresponds to exactly one after and nothing is gained or lost by working in the whitened data. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proof. Unbiasedness holds iff \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] = \sum_i a_i \theta = \theta\) for all \(\theta\), that is iff \(\sum_i a_i = 1\). We prove optimality by reducing the problem to ordinary least squares and applying Theorem 6.3, the route that exhibits the result as a genuine Gauss-Markov statement.

Collect the data into \(\mathbf{y} = (\hat\theta_1, \dots, \hat\theta_k)^\top\). Model Equation 13.1 reads \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{1}_k \theta + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \mathbf{S} := \mathrm{diag}(s_1^2, \dots, s_k^2) \succ 0\). This is a linear model with design matrix \(\mathbf{1}_k\), but with heteroscedastic errors, so Theorem 6.3 does not apply directly. We whiten the errors. Let \(\mathbf{S}^{-1/2} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1^{-1}, \dots, s_k^{-1})\) and set \[ \tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{S}^{-1/2}\mathbf{y}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{S}^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_k = (s_1^{-1}, \dots, s_k^{-1})^\top, \qquad \tilde{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}} = \mathbf{S}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \] Then \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \tilde{\mathbf{X}}\theta + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\) with \(\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}) = \mathbf{S}^{-1/2}\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{S}^{-1/2} = I_k\), so the whitened model satisfies the Gauss-Markov hypotheses G1 and G2 of Definition 6.1 with error covariance \(\sigma^2 I_k\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\), and full column rank \(\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}) = 1\).

The whitening is an invertible linear change of data: \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{S}^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\), and a linear estimator \(\sum_i a_i \hat\theta_i = \mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{y} = (\mathbf{S}^{1/2}\mathbf{a})^\top \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\) is a linear function of \(\mathbf{y}\) if and only if it is a linear function of \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\), the map \(\mathbf{a} \mapsto \mathbf{S}^{1/2}\mathbf{a}\) being a bijection of \(\mathbb{R}^k\). Likewise it is unbiased in one parameterization iff in the other. Hence the class of linear unbiased estimators of \(\theta\) is identical whether expressed in \(\mathbf{y}\) or in \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\), and minimizing variance over the class is the same problem either way. By Theorem 6.3 the unique minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator in the whitened model is the OLS estimator \[ \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} = (\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \frac{\mathbf{1}_k^\top \mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{1}_k^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{1}_k} = \frac{\sum_i \hat\theta_i / s_i^2}{\sum_i 1/s_i^2} = \frac{\sum_i w_i \hat\theta_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \] which is Equation 13.2. Its variance is, by Theorem 6.2 specialized here or by direct computation, \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}) = (\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1} = (\mathbf{1}_k^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_k)^{-1} = 1/\sum_i w_i\), which is Equation 13.3. Uniqueness in Theorem 6.3 transfers through the bijection. \(\square\)

The same conclusion has a one-line elementary proof by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which also pins down the equality case; we record it because it is so transparent.

Remark. Cauchy-Schwarz proof and the equality case. For any unbiased weights, \(\sum_i a_i = 1\). The variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) = \sum_i a_i^2 s_i^2\). By Cauchy-Schwarz applied to the vectors \((a_i s_i)_i\) and \((1/s_i)_i\), \[ 1 = \Big(\sum_i a_i\Big)^2 = \Big(\sum_i (a_i s_i)\cdot \tfrac{1}{s_i}\Big)^2 \le \Big(\sum_i a_i^2 s_i^2\Big)\Big(\sum_i \tfrac{1}{s_i^2}\Big) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\cdot W , \] so \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/W\), with equality iff \((a_i s_i)_i \propto (1/s_i)_i\), that is iff \(a_i \propto 1/s_i^2 = w_i\). The normalization \(\sum_i a_i = 1\) then forces \(a_i = w_i/W\), the IVW weights. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Under the Gaussian version of Equation 13.1 the IVW estimator is more than best linear unbiased: it is fully efficient, coinciding with the maximum likelihood estimator and attaining the Cramer-Rao bound.

Proposition 13.1 (IVW is the Gaussian MLE and attains the Cramer-Rao bound) Under Equation 13.1 with \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s_i^2)\) independent, the maximum likelihood estimator of \(\theta\) is \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\), the Fisher information for \(\theta\) is \(\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \sum_i w_i = W\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}) = 1/W\) equals the Cramer-Rao lower bound, so \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\) is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator among all (not merely linear) unbiased estimators.

Proof. The log-likelihood is \(\ell(\theta) = -\tfrac12 \sum_i \log(2\pi s_i^2) - \tfrac12 \sum_i (\hat\theta_i - \theta)^2/s_i^2\). The score is \(\ell'(\theta) = \sum_i (\hat\theta_i - \theta)/s_i^2 = \sum_i w_i (\hat\theta_i - \theta)\), which vanishes exactly at \(\theta = \sum_i w_i \hat\theta_i/\sum_i w_i = \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\); since \(\ell''(\theta) = -\sum_i w_i < 0\), this is the maximizer. The Fisher information (Definition 5.7) is \(\mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}[\ell''(\theta)] = \sum_i w_i = W\). The Cramer-Rao bound (Theorem 5.7) for an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\) is therefore \(1/\mathcal{I}(\theta) = 1/W\), which Equation 13.3 shows \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\) attains. An unbiased estimator attaining the Cramer-Rao bound is minimum-variance unbiased. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. Under the Gaussian model, inverse-variance weighting is not merely best among linear estimators; it is best among all unbiased estimators and coincides with maximum likelihood. So there is nothing cleverer to be attempted: the standard pooled estimate is already fully efficient. The two Part I ingredients are Definition 5.7, the Fisher information (the curvature of the log-likelihood, a measure of how sharply the data pin down \(\theta\)), and Theorem 5.7, the Cramer-Rao bound (no unbiased estimator can have variance below \(1/\mathcal{I}(\theta)\)). All of this still rests on the fixed-effect premise of a single \(\theta\); the next section puts that premise on trial. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

13.2 Cochran’s \(Q\) and the test for heterogeneity

If the fixed-effect model holds, the studies should agree up to their sampling errors, and the weighted spread of the \(\hat\theta_i\) about \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\) should be no larger than sampling noise predicts. Cochran’s \(Q\) measures that spread.

Definition 13.2 Cochran’s \(Q\) statistic is \[ Q = \sum_{i=1}^k w_i (\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}})^2, \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{s_i^2}. \tag{13.4}\]

Remark. Reader translation, and where this is going. Once we commit to a single common effect and estimate it by \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\), we look at what is left over: how far does each trial sit from the pooled value? Cochran’s \(Q\) adds up those gaps, each squared and weighted by the trial’s precision. It is exactly a weighted residual sum of squares after fitting one mean, the meta-analytic twin of the residual sum of squares in regression, so it should behave like one: on \(k\) studies, one degree of freedom is spent locating the common effect and \(k - 1\) independent disagreements remain. Knowing its distribution is what turns “do the trials agree?” into a heterogeneity \(p\)-value. On the running trio, \[ Q = 100(0.15 - 0.264)^2 + 25(0.60 - 0.264)^2 + 12.5(0.50 - 0.264)^2 = 4.82, \] against an expected \(k - 1 = 2\): the trio disagrees by more than sampling error alone would predict. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We first isolate the linear-algebra fact that drives its distribution: a quadratic form in independent standard normals, with an orthogonal projector in the middle, is chi-squared with degrees of freedom equal to the rank of the projector. This is the same mechanism behind the residual sum of squares in regression (Theorem 6.6); we state it once, cleanly, for reuse.

Two reminders make the lemma readable. A matrix \(P\) is idempotent if \(P^2 = P\); such a \(P\) is a projection, and its rank equals its trace (if \(P^2 = P\), then each eigenvalue \(\lambda\) satisfies \(\lambda^2 = \lambda\), forcing \(\lambda \in \{0, 1\}\), so the number of nonzero eigenvalues, the rank, is their sum, the trace). The spectral theorem (Theorem 3.2) writes a symmetric matrix as \(Q\Lambda Q^\top\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda\) the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues, and Lemma 6.3 says a linear image \(A\mathbf{Z}\) of a Gaussian vector is again Gaussian with covariance \(A A^\top\) (an orthogonal \(Q^\top\) leaves the standard covariance \(I_k\) unchanged).

Lemma 13.1 (Chi-squared law of a projected Gaussian) Let \(\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{0}, I_k)\) and let \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\) be symmetric and idempotent, \(P = P^\top = P^2\), with \(\mathrm{rank}(P) = r\). Then \(\mathbf{Z}^\top P \mathbf{Z} \sim \chi^2_r\).

Proof. By the spectral theorem (Theorem 3.2) write \(P = Q\Lambda Q^\top\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)\). Idempotence forces \(\lambda_i^2 = \lambda_i\), hence \(\lambda_i \in \{0, 1\}\); the number of unit eigenvalues equals \(\mathrm{rank}(P) = r\) (equivalently \(\mathrm{tr}(P) = r\), the rank of an idempotent matrix equaling its trace). Reorder so that \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(I_r, \mathbf{0})\). Put \(\mathbf{U} = Q^\top\mathbf{Z}\). By Lemma 6.3, \(\mathbf{U} \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{0}, Q^\top I_k Q) = \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{0}, I_k)\), so its coordinates are independent standard normals. Then \[ \mathbf{Z}^\top P \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{Z}^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{U}^\top\Lambda\mathbf{U} = \sum_{i=1}^r U_i^2 , \] a sum of \(r\) independent squared standard normal variables, which by definition has the \(\chi^2_r\) distribution. \(\square\)

Remark. Reader translation. The lemma is the engine of the whole section: pass a standard Gaussian vector through a projection and take its squared length, and the result is chi-squared with degrees of freedom equal to the dimension the projection keeps. Cochran’s \(Q\) is precisely of this form, with the projection removing the one dimension used up by estimating the common effect. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Theorem 13.2 (Distribution of Cochran’s \(Q\) under homogeneity) Under the fixed-effect model Equation 13.1 with Gaussian errors \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s_i^2)\), \[ Q \sim \chi^2_{k-1}. \] (Corresponds to C.2 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof. Standardize each study: \(Z_i := \sqrt{w_i}\,(\hat\theta_i - \theta) = (\hat\theta_i - \theta)/s_i\). Under the Gaussian fixed-effect model the \(Z_i\) are independent \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), so \(\mathbf{Z} = (Z_1, \dots, Z_k)^\top \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{0}, I_k)\), and \(\hat\theta_i - \theta = Z_i/\sqrt{w_i}\). Subtracting the common \(\theta\) from numerator and denominator leaves the IVW deviation unchanged, \[ \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} - \theta = \sum_i \frac{w_i}{W}(\hat\theta_i - \theta) = \frac{1}{W}\sum_i w_i \cdot \frac{Z_i}{\sqrt{w_i}} = \frac{1}{W}\sum_i \sqrt{w_i}\,Z_i . \] Introduce the unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) with components \(u_i = \sqrt{w_i/W}\); then \(\sum_i u_i^2 = \sum_i w_i/W = 1\), and \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} - \theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}\sum_i u_i Z_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}\,\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{Z}\). Therefore \[ \sqrt{w_i}\,(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}) = \sqrt{w_i}\,(\hat\theta_i - \theta) - \sqrt{w_i}\,(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} - \theta) = Z_i - \frac{\sqrt{w_i}}{\sqrt{W}}\,\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{Z} = Z_i - u_i\,\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{Z} = \big[(I_k - \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top)\mathbf{Z}\big]_i . \] Squaring and summing, \[ Q = \sum_i w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}})^2 = \sum_i \big[(I_k - \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top) \mathbf{Z}\big]_i^2 = \big\|(I_k - \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top)\mathbf{Z}\big\|_2^2 = \mathbf{Z}^\top (I_k - \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top)\mathbf{Z}, \] the last step using that \(P := I_k - \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top\) is symmetric and idempotent (\(P^2 = I_k - 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top + \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}^\top = I_k - \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top\) because \(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} = 1\)). Its rank is \(\mathrm{tr}(P) = k - \|\mathbf{u}\|_2^2 = k - 1\). By Lemma 13.1, \(Q \sim \chi^2_{k-1}\). \(\square\)

Remark. Why this matters. This distribution is what makes \(Q\) usable. Under homogeneity \(Q \sim \chi^2_{k-1}\), so a value in the upper tail is evidence that the trials disagree by more than chance, and the tail probability \(P(\chi^2_{k-1} > Q_{\mathrm{obs}})\) is the classical heterogeneity \(p\)-value. The \(k - 1\) has the plain reading promised above: of the \(k\) standardized deviations, one linear combination is spent locating the common effect \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\), leaving \(k - 1\) free to disagree. For the running trio, \(Q = 4.82\) on \(k - 1 = 2\) degrees of freedom gives \(P(\chi^2_2 > 4.82) = 0.09\): suggestive but not decisive with only three trials, which is typical. For indirect comparison this test is the first alarm bell, because heterogeneity in a direct \(A\)-versus-\(C\) or \(B\)-versus-\(C\) comparison is exactly what threatens the transitivity that Chapter 14 relies on. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The mean of the \(\chi^2_{k-1}\) law is \(k - 1\), so under homogeneity we expect \(Q \approx k - 1\). A value of \(Q\) far above \(k - 1\) is evidence that the studies disagree by more than sampling error allows, that is, evidence of heterogeneity. To quantify “far above,” we need the behavior of \(Q\) when the effects genuinely differ. The following second-moment computation requires no Gaussianity and is the engine behind both the \(I^2\) statistic and the DerSimonian-Laird estimator.

Proposition 13.2 (Expected value of \(Q\) under heterogeneity) Suppose the study-specific effects vary: \(\hat\theta_i = \theta + b_i + \varepsilon_i\) where the \(b_i\) have mean \(0\) and variance \(\tau^2\), the \(\varepsilon_i\) have mean \(0\) and variance \(s_i^2\), and all \(2k\) variables \(b_1, \dots, b_k, \varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_k\) are uncorrelated. With the fixed-effect weights \(w_i = 1/s_i^2\) used in \(Q\), \[ \mathbb{E}[Q] = (k - 1) + \tau^2 c, \qquad c := \sum_i w_i - \frac{\sum_i w_i^2}{\sum_i w_i} = W - \frac{\sum_i w_i^2}{W} . \tag{13.5}\] Moreover \(c > 0\) whenever \(k \ge 2\).

Proof. Let \(\delta_i = \hat\theta_i - \theta = b_i + \varepsilon_i\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_i] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\delta_i) = \tau^2 + s_i^2\). The common \(\theta\) cancels in the deviations, \(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} = \delta_i - \bar\delta_w\) with \(\bar\delta_w = \frac{1}{W}\sum_j w_j \delta_j\). The weighted sum-of-squares identity gives \[ Q = \sum_i w_i(\delta_i - \bar\delta_w)^2 = \sum_i w_i \delta_i^2 - W \bar\delta_w^2 , \] which is the weighted version of the familiar identity. Expanding the square and using \(\sum_i w_i \delta_i = W\bar\delta_w\) (the definition of \(\bar\delta_w\)) together with \(\sum_i w_i = W\), \[ \sum_i w_i(\delta_i - \bar\delta_w)^2 = \sum_i w_i \delta_i^2 - 2\bar\delta_w \sum_i w_i \delta_i + \bar\delta_w^2 \sum_i w_i = \sum_i w_i \delta_i^2 - 2\bar\delta_w(W\bar\delta_w) + W\bar\delta_w^2 = \sum_i w_i \delta_i^2 - W\bar\delta_w^2 . \]

Take expectations term by term. First, using \(w_i s_i^2 = 1\), \[ \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i w_i \delta_i^2\Big] = \sum_i w_i(\tau^2 + s_i^2) = \tau^2 W + \sum_i w_i s_i^2 = \tau^2 W + k . \] Second, \(\mathbb{E}[\bar\delta_w] = 0\), so \(\mathbb{E}[W\bar\delta_w^2] = W\,\mathrm{Var}(\bar\delta_w)\), and by uncorrelatedness, using \(w_j^2 s_j^2 = w_j\), \[ \mathrm{Var}(\bar\delta_w) = \frac{1}{W^2}\sum_j w_j^2 \mathrm{Var}(\delta_j) = \frac{1}{W^2}\sum_j w_j^2 (\tau^2 + s_j^2) = \frac{\tau^2 \sum_j w_j^2 + \sum_j w_j}{W^2} = \frac{\tau^2\sum_j w_j^2 + W}{W^2}. \] Hence \(\mathbb{E}[W\bar\delta_w^2] = \tau^2 \frac{\sum_j w_j^2}{W} + 1\). Subtracting, \[ \mathbb{E}[Q] = \big(\tau^2 W + k\big) - \Big(\tau^2 \tfrac{\sum_j w_j^2}{W} + 1\Big) = (k - 1) + \tau^2 \Big(W - \tfrac{\sum_j w_j^2}{W}\Big), \] which is Equation 13.5. Finally \(c = (W^2 - \sum_i w_i^2)/W\), and since \(W^2 = (\sum_i w_i)^2 = \sum_i w_i^2 + \sum_{i \ne j} w_i w_j\) with all \(w_i > 0\), the cross sum is strictly positive once \(k \ge 2\), so \(c > 0\). \(\square\)

Remark. Reader translation. This is the first point in the chapter where trials are allowed to genuinely differ: the term \(b_i\), with variance \(\tau^2\), is the real effect difference of trial \(i\) (its population, protocol, or setting), as opposed to the sampling noise \(\varepsilon_i\). The formula \(\mathbb{E}[Q] = (k - 1) + \tau^2 c\) says \(Q\) carries a signal (\(\tau^2 c\)) sitting on a baseline (\(k - 1\)): with no heterogeneity \(Q\) averages \(k - 1\), and each unit of heterogeneity variance \(\tau^2\) inflates it by the known constant \(c\). Solving that relation for \(\tau^2\) is exactly how the DerSimonian-Laird estimator of Section Section 13.5 will work. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Setting \(\tau^2 = 0\) recovers \(\mathbb{E}[Q] = k - 1\), consistent with Theorem 13.2. The coefficient \(c\) depends only on the known weights, a fact we exploit repeatedly below.

13.3 The \(I^2\) statistic

Cochran’s \(Q\) has a defect as a description of heterogeneity: its size grows with the number of studies, so a large \(Q\) may reflect many studies rather than severe heterogeneity. Higgins and Thompson proposed a scale-free and dimension-free summary.

From detection to description to estimation. The next three sections form one arc, and it helps to name the three roles up front. \(Q\) answers yes or no; \(I^2\) puts the answer on a \(0\) to \(100\%\) scale that does not grow with the number of studies; and \(\tau^2\) is the quantity the random-effects model actually needs.

Statistic Section What it does
Cochran’s \(Q\) Section 13.2 detects excess disagreement (is there heterogeneity?)
\(I^2\) Section 13.3 describes its share (what fraction of variability is heterogeneity?)
\(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}\), \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}}\) Section 13.5, Section 13.6 estimate the between-study variance \(\tau^2\)

Definition 13.3 (The \(I^2\) and \(H^2\) heterogeneity statistics) Given Cochran’s \(Q\) on \(k\) studies, define \[ H^2 = \frac{Q}{k - 1}, \qquad I^2 = \max\!\left\{0,\; \frac{Q - (k - 1)}{Q}\right\} = \max\!\left\{0,\; 1 - \frac{1}{H^2}\right\}. \tag{13.6}\] The statistic \(I^2\), usually reported as a percentage, lies in \([0, 1]\) and is interpreted as the proportion of the total variability in the effect estimates that is due to between-study heterogeneity rather than to within-study sampling error. (Corresponds to C.3 in the proof catalogue.)

Remark. Reader translation, and a notation warning. \(I^2\) rescales \(Q\) so that it no longer grows merely because there are more studies: it reports the share of the observed variability that exceeds what sampling error alone would produce. Note a symbol clash: the \(H^2\) here is the Higgins-Thompson heterogeneity ratio, unrelated to the hat matrix \(H\) of Chapter 6. The letters collide but the meanings do not. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. What \(I^2\) is not. \(I^2\) is not the probability that heterogeneity exists, not the percentage of trials that are biased or discordant, not the amount by which the pooled effect is wrong, and not a decision rule on its own. An \(I^2\) of \(30\%\) does not mean “\(30\%\) of the studies disagree”; it means that, of the spread seen among the estimates, roughly \(30\%\) is in excess of sampling error. With few studies \(I^2\) is itself estimated very imprecisely, so the conventional \(25/50/75\%\) labels are rough descriptions, not test thresholds. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The interpretation is made precise by replacing the random quantity \(Q\) with its expectation and reading off a variance-ratio.

Proposition 13.3 (\(I^2\) as a variance ratio) Under the heterogeneity model of Proposition 13.2 with \(\tau^2 \ge 0\) and \(k \ge 2\), define the typical within-study variance \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}} = (k - 1)/c\), with \(c\) as in Equation 13.5. Replacing \(Q\) by \(\mathbb{E}[Q]\) in Equation 13.6 gives the population analogue \[ I^2_{\ast} := \frac{\mathbb{E}[Q] - (k - 1)}{\mathbb{E}[Q]} = \frac{\tau^2 c}{(k - 1) + \tau^2 c} = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}}} . \tag{13.7}\] Thus \(I^2_\ast\) is the fraction of total variance \(\tau^2 + \sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}}\) attributable to the between-study component \(\tau^2\). It equals \(0\) when \(\tau^2 = 0\), increases monotonically in \(\tau^2\), and tends to \(1\) as \(\tau^2 \to \infty\).

Proof. By Equation 13.5, \(\mathbb{E}[Q] - (k - 1) = \tau^2 c\), so \(I^2_\ast = \tau^2 c/((k-1) + \tau^2 c)\). Dividing numerator and denominator by \(c\) and writing \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}} = (k - 1)/c\) gives the middle and right expressions of Equation 13.7. The map \(t \mapsto t/(t + \sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}})\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \infty)\) with value \(0\) at \(t = 0\) and limit \(1\) at infinity, applied at \(t = \tau^2\). \(\square\)

Remark. The quantity \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}} = (k-1)/c\) is a weighted-harmonic-type average of the \(s_i^2\), and in the special case of equal within-study variances \(s_i^2 \equiv s^2\) one computes \(c = (k - 1)/s^2\), whence \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}} = s^2\) exactly: \(I^2_\ast = \tau^2/(\tau^2 + s^2)\) is then literally the intraclass correlation of the random-effects model. The conventional benchmarks are \(I^2 \approx 25\%\) low, \(50\%\) moderate, \(75\%\) high; they are descriptive rules of thumb, not test thresholds, and \(I^2\) itself is estimated with considerable uncertainty when \(k\) is small. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. Read Equation 13.7 as \(I^2_\ast = \tau^2 / (\tau^2 + \sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}})\), the between-study variance divided by the total. The denominator’s second piece, \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{typ}} = (k-1)/c\), is just a single “typical” within-study variance standing in for the whole collection of \(s_i^2\): when the \(s_i^2\) are all equal to \(s^2\) it is \(s^2\), and in general it is a precision-weighted average of them (the subscript “typ” is only a label for that stand-in, nothing deeper). On the running trio, \(Q = 4.82\) gives \[ I^2 = \frac{Q - (k-1)}{Q} = \frac{4.82 - 2}{4.82} = 0.585, \] about \(59\%\): a moderate-to-high share, consistent with the visibly scattered forest plot. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

13.4 The random-effects model

When \(Q\) signals heterogeneity, the fixed-effect premise that all studies estimate one \(\theta\) is untenable. The random-effects model lets each study have its own effect, drawn from a common distribution.

Fixed effect versus random effects: the contrast that matters most. Before the definition, it is worth stating plainly what changes, because this is the distinction a health researcher most needs and the one on which the later ITC methods hinge.

Aspect Fixed-effect model Random-effects model
Assumption one common true effect \(\theta\) for all trials each trial has its own effect \(\theta_i\), drawn from a distribution
What is pooled that single \(\theta\) the mean \(\theta\) of the distribution of trial effects
Weight for trial \(i\) \(w_i = 1/s_i^2\) \(\tilde w_i = 1/(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\)
Meaning of the summary the effect, estimated more precisely the average effect across the population of trial settings
When heterogeneity is large untenable; interval too narrow weights flatten, interval widens honestly
Why it matters for ITC licenses transitivity: one effect transports forces the question which population’s effect (Chapters 12, 15)

The mathematical content of the right-hand column is one sentence: adding \(\tau^2\) to every \(s_i^2\) shrinks the weights toward equality, so a single dominant precise trial no longer controls the pooled estimate, the summary widens to admit real between-trial variation, and the target of inference changes from “the common effect” to “the mean of the trial-specific effects.” That last change is not cosmetic: a mean of effects is an average over some population of trials, and pinning down which population is the whole subject of Part IV.

Definition 13.4 (Random-effects meta-analysis model) The random-effects model for \(k\) study estimates is the two-level Gaussian hierarchy \[ \hat\theta_i \mid \theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_i, s_i^2), \qquad \theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \tau^2), \qquad i = 1, \dots, k, \tag{13.8}\] with the \(\theta_i\) independent of one another and of the sampling errors, and the \(s_i^2 > 0\) known. Here \(\theta\) is the mean effect across the population of studies and \(\tau^2 \ge 0\) is the between-study variance (heterogeneity variance). Marginalizing over the study effects, \[ \hat\theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, s_i^2 + \tau^2) \quad \text{independently across } i. \tag{13.9}\] This is the meta-analytic instance of the general random-effects construction of Definition 8.3; the fixed-effect model Equation 13.1 is the boundary case \(\tau^2 = 0\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation. The two lines of Equation 13.8 read: within trial \(i\), the estimate scatters around that trial’s own true effect \(\theta_i\) with variance \(s_i^2\); and the trial’s own effect scatters around a grand mean \(\theta\) with variance \(\tau^2\). Marginally, each estimate then has variance \(s_i^2 + \tau^2\), its own sampling error plus the heterogeneity. So random-effects weighting is just inverse-variance weighting again, with the enlarged variance \(s_i^2 + \tau^2\) in place of \(s_i^2\). Setting \(\tau^2 = 0\) collapses everything back to the fixed-effect model, and the target \(\theta\) is now the mean of the trial effects, an average over some population of trial settings, not a single shared value. Which population that is becomes a central question for the transportability of Chapter 12 and the networks of Chapter 15. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Writing \(\tilde w_i = 1/(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\) for the random-effects weights, the marginal log-likelihood of \((\theta, \tau^2)\) from Equation 13.9 is \[ \ell(\theta, \tau^2) = -\frac12 \sum_{i=1}^k \log\!\big(2\pi(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\big) - \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{(\hat\theta_i - \theta)^2}{s_i^2 + \tau^2} . \tag{13.10}\] Before estimating the parameters we must check they are identifiable, that is, that distinct parameter values give distinct data distributions. Otherwise no amount of data could distinguish them.

Proposition 13.4 (Identifiability of the random-effects parameters) Fix known \(s_1^2, \dots, s_k^2 > 0\) with \(k \ge 1\). The map \[ \Psi: (\theta, \tau^2) \in \mathbb{R}\times[0, \infty) \longmapsto \bigotimes_{i=1}^k \mathcal{N}(\theta, s_i^2 + \tau^2) \] is injective. Hence \((\theta, \tau^2)\) is identifiable from the joint law of \((\hat\theta_1, \dots, \hat\theta_k)\). (Corresponds to C.4 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof. Suppose \(\Psi(\theta, \tau^2) = \Psi(\theta', \tau'^2)\). Equality of the joint laws implies equality of every marginal. The first marginal is \(\mathcal{N}(\theta, s_1^2 + \tau^2) = \mathcal{N}(\theta', s_1^2 + \tau'^2)\); equating means gives \(\theta = \theta'\), and equating variances gives \(s_1^2 + \tau^2 = s_1^2 + \tau'^2\), hence \(\tau^2 = \tau'^2\). Thus \((\theta, \tau^2) = (\theta', \tau'^2)\), and \(\Psi\) is injective. \(\square\)

Remark. Identifiability is not estimability, and the role of known \(s_i^2\). The proposition says the parameters are determined by the data distribution; it does not say they can be estimated well from a single realization. With \(k = 1\), \(\tau^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_1) - s_1^2\) is identified, yet one draw of \(\hat\theta_1\) carries essentially no information about its own variance, so no consistent estimator of \(\tau^2\) exists. Consistency of the moment and likelihood estimators below requires \(k \to \infty\); in practice \(\tau^2\) is estimated stably only with \(k \gtrsim 5\) studies, and the catalogue’s working condition “\(k \ge 2\) with the \(s_i^2\) not all equal” is the practical requirement for \(\hat\tau^2\) to draw on more than a single contrast and to behave well. The hypothesis that the \(s_i^2\) are known is essential to the proof: if the within-study variances were free parameters, only the sums \(s_i^2 + \tau^2\) would be identified from Equation 13.9 and \(\tau^2\) would be confounded with the \(s_i^2\). Meta-analysis breaks the confounding by estimating each \(s_i^2\) within study \(i\) from its \(n_i\) patients, where it is consistent as \(n_i \to \infty\), and then treating it as fixed. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation, concretely. “Identifiable but not estimable” is less mysterious than it sounds. With a single trial you could write down \(\hat\tau^2 = \hat\theta_1^2 - s_1^2\) as a formal solution, but one number cannot estimate a variance: you need several trials to see the spread that \(\tau^2\) describes. This is why heterogeneity is genuinely hard to estimate in the small meta-analyses typical of health technology assessment, and why the rules of thumb (roughly \(k \gtrsim 5\) trials for a stable \(\hat\tau^2\), and at least \(k \ge 2\) with unequal \(s_i^2\) for the estimator to draw on more than a single comparison) are practical guidance rather than theorems; the “\(5\)” is an empirical convention from simulation studies, not a derived constant. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

We now turn to estimating \(\tau^2\). Two estimators dominate practice: the closed-form method-of-moments estimator of DerSimonian and Laird, and the restricted maximum likelihood estimator.

13.5 Estimating the heterogeneity variance: DerSimonian-Laird

The method of moments equates the observed \(Q\) to its expected value Equation 13.5 and solves for \(\tau^2\).

The idea in one line: the DerSimonian-Laird estimator converts the excess of \(Q\) over its null mean into an estimate of \(\tau^2\). Since Equation 13.5 says \(\mathbb{E}[Q] = (k-1) + \tau^2 c\), the excess \(Q - (k-1)\) is on average \(\tau^2 c\), so dividing the observed excess by the known constant \(c\) inverts the relation. Here \(c\) is the conversion factor between excess \(Q\) and heterogeneity variance; it depends only on the weights, so the same excess \(Q\) implies more or less \(\tau^2\) according to how precise the trials are.

Theorem 13.3 (The DerSimonian-Laird estimator) Under the random-effects model Equation 13.8, the method-of-moments estimator obtained by equating \(Q\) to \(\mathbb{E}[Q] = (k - 1) + \tau^2 c\) is \[ \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \max\!\left\{0,\; \frac{Q - (k - 1)}{c}\right\}, \qquad c = \sum_i w_i - \frac{\sum_i w_i^2}{\sum_i w_i}, \quad w_i = \frac{1}{s_i^2}. \tag{13.11}\] The untruncated version \(\tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = (Q - (k-1))/c\) is exactly unbiased for \(\tau^2\), and the truncated estimator Equation 13.11 is biased upward. (Corresponds to C.5 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof. Solving \(Q = (k - 1) + \tau^2 c\) for \(\tau^2\) gives \(\tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = (Q - (k - 1))/c\); the truncation \(\max\{0, \cdot\}\) enforces the constraint \(\tau^2 \ge 0\). Since \(c\) depends only on the known weights and is therefore a constant, Equation 13.5 gives \[ \mathbb{E}[\tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}] = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Q] - (k - 1)}{c} = \frac{\big((k - 1) + \tau^2 c\big) - (k - 1)}{c} = \tau^2 , \] so the untruncated estimator is exactly unbiased; note this uses only the second-moment assumptions of Proposition 13.2, not Gaussianity. The truncated estimator satisfies \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \max\{0, \tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}\} \ge \tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}\) pointwise, with strict inequality on the event \(\{\tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} < 0\}\), which has positive probability whenever \(\tau^2\) is small. Taking expectations, \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}] \ge \mathbb{E}[\tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}] = \tau^2\), with strict inequality there, so truncation introduces upward bias. \(\square\)

Remark. The DerSimonian-Laird estimator is the historical default and remains ubiquitous because it is non-iterative. Its exact unbiasedness before truncation is a genuine finite-sample property, not merely asymptotic. Consistency as \(k \to \infty\) holds under mild conditions on the weights (for example \(w_i\) bounded above and below by positive constants), since then \(\mathrm{Var}(\tilde\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}) \to 0\); we cite DerSimonian and Laird (1986) for the original argument and Dias et al. (2018) for the meta-analytic context. The price of the closed form is statistical: in small samples DL can be inefficient relative to likelihood-based estimators, which motivates REML. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Reader translation, and the running trio. DerSimonian-Laird is the non-iterative default: compute \(Q\), subtract its null mean \(k - 1\), divide by \(c\), and floor at zero. For the running trio, \(c = W - \sum_i w_i^2/W = 137.5 - 10781.25/137.5 = 59.1\), so \[ \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \frac{Q - (k-1)}{c} = \frac{4.82 - 2}{59.1} = 0.048 . \] Feeding this back as \(\tilde w_i = 1/(s_i^2 + 0.048)\) shrinks the weights toward one another: study \(C\)’s share of the total weight falls from \(73\%\) (fixed-effect) to \(47\%\) (random-effects), and the pooled estimate moves from \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}} = 0.264\) up to \(\hat\theta_R = 0.365\), no longer dragged so hard toward the one precise trial. This down-weighting of the dominant trial is the entire practical meaning of “random effects gives smaller trials more say.” \(\blacktriangleleft\)

13.6 Estimating the heterogeneity variance: REML

Maximizing the marginal likelihood Equation 13.10 jointly in \((\theta, \tau^2)\) gives the maximum likelihood estimator of \(\tau^2\), which is biased downward because it ignores the degree of freedom spent estimating \(\theta\), exactly the \(n\)-versus-\((n-1)\) phenomenon of the ordinary sample variance. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML, Definition 8.4) removes the bias by basing inference for the variance on error contrasts, linear combinations of the data orthogonal to the mean, whose distribution is free of \(\theta\).

What REML is trying to fix (read this before the derivation). Plain maximum likelihood maximizes Equation 13.10 in \(\theta\) and \(\tau^2\) at once. The trouble is that it estimates \(\theta\) from the same data and then acts as if \(\theta\) were known, spending a degree of freedom it does not pay for; the result is a \(\tau^2\) biased downward, exactly as the naive sample variance \(\frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\) underestimates the truth and must be corrected to the \(\frac{1}{n-1}\) version. A downward-biased \(\tau^2\) is dangerous for a decision-maker because it makes the pooled interval too narrow, overstating confidence. REML fixes this by estimating \(\tau^2\) from error contrasts, combinations of the data that carry no information about \(\theta\) because they are orthogonal to the mean, so no degree of freedom is silently spent. The payoff is visible in the sanity check right after the theorem: when all trials have equal variance, REML reproduces the honest \(\frac{1}{k-1}\) divisor exactly. The derivation below is the densest in the chapter, but the single fact that makes it work is that the random-effects-weighted residuals sum to zero, which annihilates the awkward chain-rule term. Read the equal-variance remark first if the general algebra feels like a wall.

Theorem 13.4 (REML estimator of the heterogeneity variance) Consider the random-effects model Equation 13.8, written as \(\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{1}_k \theta, \mathbf{V})\) with \(\mathbf{y} = (\hat\theta_1, \dots, \hat\theta_k)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{V} = \mathrm{diag}(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\). Writing \(\tilde w_i = 1/(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\) and \(\hat\theta_R(\tau^2) = \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i / \sum_i \tilde w_i\), the restricted log-likelihood of \(\tau^2\) is, up to an additive constant, \[ \ell_R(\tau^2) = -\frac12 \sum_{i=1}^k \log(s_i^2 + \tau^2) - \frac12 \log\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i \Big) - \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i \big(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R(\tau^2)\big)^2 . \tag{13.12}\] Its maximizer \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}}\) (truncated at \(0\)) solves the estimating equation \[ \sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i^2 \big(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R\big)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i}, \tag{13.13}\] equivalently the fixed point \[ \tau^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i^2 \big[(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2 - s_i^2\big]}{\sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i^2} + \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^k \tilde w_i} . \tag{13.14}\] (Corresponds to C.6 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof. For the Gaussian linear model \(\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{X}\theta, \mathbf{V})\) with \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}_k\), the general restricted log-likelihood established for Definition 8.4 is, up to an additive constant, \[ \ell_R = -\frac12 \log\det\mathbf{V} - \frac12 \log\det(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}) - \frac12 (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat\theta_R)^\top \mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat\theta_R), \] with \(\hat\theta_R = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{y}\) the generalized least squares estimator. Specialize to \(\mathbf{V} = \mathrm{diag}(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\), \(\mathbf{V}^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(\tilde w_i)\), and \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}_k\). Then \(\log\det\mathbf{V} = \sum_i \log(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\); \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}_k^\top \mathrm{diag}(\tilde w_i)\mathbf{1}_k = \sum_i \tilde w_i\), so \(\log\det(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1} \mathbf{X}) = \log(\sum_i \tilde w_i)\); the GLS estimator is \(\hat\theta_R = \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i /\sum_i \tilde w_i\); and the quadratic form is \(\sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2\). Substituting yields Equation 13.12.

Now differentiate Equation 13.12 in \(\tau^2\). Abbreviate \(v_i = s_i^2 + \tau^2\), so \(\tilde w_i = v_i^{-1}\) and \(\mathrm{d}\tilde w_i/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 = -\tilde w_i^2\), and write \(r_i = \hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R\) and \(S_1 = \sum_i \tilde w_i\), \(S_2 = \sum_i \tilde w_i^2\). The three terms differentiate as follows.

First term. Since \(\mathrm{d}\log v_i/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 = \tilde w_i\), \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2}\Big(-\tfrac12 \sum_i \log v_i\Big) = -\tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i = -\tfrac12 S_1 . \]

Second term. Since \(\mathrm{d}S_1/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 = \sum_i (-\tilde w_i^2) = -S_2\), \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2}\Big(-\tfrac12 \log S_1\Big) = -\tfrac12 \frac{-S_2}{S_1} = \tfrac12 \frac{S_2}{S_1} . \]

Third term. Here \(r_i = \hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R(\tau^2)\) depends on \(\tau^2\) through \(\hat\theta_R\). Differentiating, \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2}\Big(-\tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i r_i^2\Big) = -\tfrac12 \sum_i\Big[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde w_i}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2} r_i^2 + \tilde w_i \cdot 2 r_i \frac{\mathrm{d}r_i} {\mathrm{d}\tau^2}\Big] = -\tfrac12\Big[ -\sum_i \tilde w_i^2 r_i^2 - 2\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat\theta_R} {\mathrm{d}\tau^2}\sum_i \tilde w_i r_i\Big]. \] The crucial simplification is that the chain-rule term through \(\hat\theta_R\) vanishes: by the very definition of the GLS estimator, the random-effects-weighted residuals sum to zero, \[ \sum_i \tilde w_i r_i = \sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R) = \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R \sum_i \tilde w_i = 0 , \] so regardless of \(\mathrm{d}\hat\theta_R/\mathrm{d}\tau^2\) the second summand is \(0\). Hence the third term contributes \(+\tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i^2 r_i^2 = \tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i^2 r_i^2\).

Adding the three contributions, the REML score is \[ \ell_R'(\tau^2) = -\tfrac12 S_1 + \tfrac12 \frac{S_2}{S_1} + \tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i^2 r_i^2 . \] Setting \(\ell_R'(\tau^2) = 0\) gives \(\sum_i \tilde w_i^2 r_i^2 = S_1 - S_2/S_1\), which is Equation 13.13. To reach the fixed-point form, multiply the target identity \(\tau^2 = \frac{1}{S_2}\sum_i \tilde w_i^2 (r_i^2 - s_i^2) + 1/S_1\) by \(S_2\) and use \(s_i^2 + \tau^2 = 1/\tilde w_i\): \[ \tau^2 S_2 + \sum_i \tilde w_i^2 s_i^2 - \frac{S_2}{S_1} = \sum_i \tilde w_i^2(\tau^2 + s_i^2) - \frac{S_2}{S_1} = \sum_i \tilde w_i^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\tilde w_i} - \frac{S_2}{S_1} = S_1 - \frac{S_2}{S_1}, \] so \(\sum_i \tilde w_i^2 r_i^2 = S_1 - S_2/S_1\) is equivalent to Equation 13.14. The estimating equation Equation 13.13 is solved by iterating Equation 13.14, recomputing \(\tilde w_i\) and \(\hat\theta_R\) at each step from the current \(\tau^2\), starting from a nonnegative value such as \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{DL}}\), and truncating any negative iterate at \(0\). \(\square\)

Remark. Equal-variance check. When the within-study variances are equal, \(s_i^2 \equiv s^2\), all random-effects weights coincide, \(\tilde w_i \equiv \tilde w = 1/(s^2 + \tau^2)\), and \(\hat\theta_R = \bar{\hat\theta} = \frac1k\sum_i \hat\theta_i\). Then Equation 13.13 reads \(\tilde w^2 \sum_i (\hat\theta_i - \bar{\hat\theta})^2 = k\tilde w - (k\tilde w^2)/(k\tilde w) = (k - 1)\tilde w\), that is \[ s^2 + \tau^2 = \frac{1}{\tilde w} = \frac{1}{k - 1}\sum_{i=1}^k (\hat\theta_i - \bar{\hat\theta})^2 , \qquad \text{so} \qquad \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}} = \frac{1}{k - 1}\sum_{i=1}^k (\hat\theta_i - \bar{\hat\theta})^2 - s^2 . \] The divisor is \(k - 1\), not \(k\): REML reproduces the unbiased sample-variance correction, exactly the one-sample phenomenon of Theorem 8.8 with \(n - 1 = k - 1\) degrees of freedom. This is the sanity check that REML has done its job. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. REML by iteration, on the running trio. The fixed point Equation 13.14 is solved by starting from a nonnegative guess and recomputing. Take the trio \(C, D, E\) and start at the DerSimonian-Laird value \(\tau^2_{(0)} = 0.048\). Each step recomputes the weights \(\tilde w_i = 1/(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\), then \(\hat\theta_R = \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i / \sum_i \tilde w_i\), then the right-hand side of Equation 13.14. Iterating gives approximately \[ \tau^2_{(0)} = 0.048 \;\to\; 0.046 \;\to\; 0.0454 \;\to\; 0.0452 \;\to\; \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}} \approx 0.045, \] converging in a handful of steps. Here REML settles just below DerSimonian-Laird, and the two agree to the second decimal; the point is only that “solve the estimating equation” is a short loop, not a mystery. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

13.7 The Hartung-Knapp adjustment

Once \(\tau^2\) has been estimated, the random-effects summary is the IVW estimator with random-effects weights, \[ \hat\theta_R = \frac{\sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i}{\sum_i \tilde w_i}, \qquad \tilde w_i = \frac{1} {s_i^2 + \hat\tau^2}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R) = \frac{1}{\sum_i \tilde w_i}. \] The conventional Wald interval \(\hat\theta_R \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R)}\) treats the weights as known and the Gaussian quantile as exact; both approximations fail when \(k\) is small, and the interval is then too narrow. The Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment fixes the calibration by estimating an overall scale and switching to a \(t\) reference distribution.

The idea first (why the usual interval is too narrow). When only a few trials are available, two things the Wald interval pretends to know are actually uncertain: the heterogeneity \(\tau^2\) baked into the weights, and the assumption that a normal quantile is exact. Pretending we know them makes the interval too short, so its true coverage falls below the advertised \(95\%\); and few trials is the typical case in health technology assessment. Hartung-Knapp repairs the calibration with a device from ordinary regression. It treats the random-effects fit as a one-parameter working model \(\hat\theta_i = \theta + e_i\) with \(\mathrm{Var}(e_i) = \sigma^2/\tilde w_i\), in which the trials share one unknown overall scale \(\sigma^2\); the real random-effects model is the special case \(\sigma^2 = 1\). Instead of imposing \(\sigma^2 = 1\) (the Wald choice), it estimates \(\sigma^2\) from the weighted residuals, exactly as a regression estimates its error variance, and pays for that estimate by widening the quantile from the normal \(z\) to a Student \(t_{k-1}\). That is the whole idea; the theorem shows the resulting interval is exact under the working model.

Theorem 13.5 (Exactness of the Hartung-Knapp \(t\) interval under a working model) Suppose \(\hat\theta_i = \theta + e_i\) with \(e_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2/\tilde w_i)\) independent, \(i = 1, \dots, k \ge 2\), where the weights \(\tilde w_i > 0\) are known and \(\sigma^2 > 0\) is an unknown common scale. Then, with \[ \hat\theta_R = \frac{\sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i}{\sum_i \tilde w_i}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}}(\hat\theta_R) = \frac{1}{k - 1}\,\frac{\sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2}{\sum_j \tilde w_j}, \tag{13.15}\] the Hartung-Knapp statistic has an exact Student distribution, \[ T_{\mathrm{HK}} = \frac{\hat\theta_R - \theta}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}}(\hat\theta_R)}} \sim t_{k-1}, \] so \(\hat\theta_R \pm t_{k-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}}(\hat\theta_R)}\) is an exact \(1 - \alpha\) confidence interval for \(\theta\). (Corresponds to C.7 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof. Whiten the working model. Put \(\tilde y_i = \sqrt{\tilde w_i}\,\hat\theta_i\) and \(x_i = \sqrt{\tilde w_i}\), and let \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = (\tilde y_1, \dots, \tilde y_k)^\top\), \(\mathbf{X} = (x_1, \dots, x_k)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times 1}\). Multiplying \(\hat\theta_i = \theta + e_i\) by \(\sqrt{\tilde w_i}\) gives \[ \tilde y_i = \theta\, x_i + \tilde e_i, \qquad \tilde e_i = \sqrt{\tilde w_i}\, e_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \ \text{independent}, \] which is the Gaussian linear model of Definition 6.1 with \(n = k\) observations, \(p = 1\) coefficient \(\theta\), homoscedastic variance \(\sigma^2\), and full column rank. Here \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \sum_i \tilde w_i\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i\), so the OLS estimator is \(\hat\theta_R = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i/\sum_i \tilde w_i\), matching the stated \(\hat\theta_R\). The residual sum of squares is \[ \mathrm{RSS} = \sum_i (\tilde y_i - \hat\theta_R x_i)^2 = \sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2, \] and the unbiased error-variance estimate (Theorem 6.4) is \(s^2 = \mathrm{RSS}/(k - 1) = \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2\), with \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}} (\hat\theta_R) = s^2 \,(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} = s^2/\sum_i \tilde w_i\), which is Equation 13.15. Applying the \(t\)-statistic theorem Theorem 6.8 with contrast \(\mathbf{c} = 1\) (a scalar) gives directly \[ \frac{\hat\theta_R - \theta}{s\sqrt{(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}}} = \frac{\hat\theta_R - \theta} {\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}}(\hat\theta_R)}} = T_{\mathrm{HK}} \sim t_{k-1}. \] Inverting the two-sided \(t\) test yields the stated confidence interval. \(\square\)

Remark. Why this calibrates the random-effects interval. The actual random-effects model Equation 13.8, with \(\tau^2\) replaced by its estimate, corresponds to the working model with the “true” scale \(\sigma^2 = 1\), because then \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_i) = 1/\tilde w_i\). The Wald interval imposes \(\sigma^2 = 1\) and uses a normal quantile. Hartung-Knapp instead estimates \(\sigma^2\) from the weighted residuals and uses a \(t_{k-1}\) quantile, exactly as one estimates the error variance in a regression rather than assuming it. Writing \(q = \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2\) for the estimated scale, the two variance estimators are related by \[ \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}}(\hat\theta_R) = q \cdot \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R), \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R) = \frac{1}{\sum_i \tilde w_i}, \] so Hartung-Knapp rescales the Wald variance by the data-driven factor \(q\) and widens the quantile from \(z\) to \(t_{k-1}\). When the random-effects model fits, \(q \approx 1\) and the adjustment is mild for large \(k\); for small \(k\) the wider \(t\) quantile restores coverage that the Wald interval loses. The factor \(q\) can fall below \(1\), occasionally yielding a Hartung-Knapp interval narrower than the Wald interval; a common conservative safeguard takes \(\max\{1, q\}\). The method is recommended whenever \(k \le 10\) (Dias et al. (2018)). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Running trio: the interval to report. With only \(k = 3\) trials the difference is dramatic. The trio’s random-effects fit gives \(\hat\theta_R = 0.365\) with \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R) = 1/\sum_i \tilde w_i = 0.027\), so the Wald interval is \(0.365 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0.027} = (0.04, 0.69)\). The scale factor is \(q = \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2 = 0.79\), giving \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HK}} = q\,\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R) = 0.0215\). Even though \(q < 1\) shrinks the variance a little, the Student quantile \(t_{2,\,0.975} = 4.303\) (a heavy penalty for having only two degrees of freedom) dominates, and the interval \[ 0.365 \pm 4.303\sqrt{0.0215} = (-0.27,\, 1.00) \] is far wider than the Wald interval, and honest about how little three trials pin down. A cautious analyst reports the wider one. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

13.8 Bayesian meta-analysis

The Bayesian treatment places priors on the parameters and reports the posterior. It is the framework that generalizes most cleanly to networks (Chapter 15) and to the multilevel models of Part V, and it handles the uncertainty in \(\tau^2\) coherently rather than by plug-in. We work the conjugate core: with \(\tau^2\) fixed, the mean effect has a closed-form Gaussian posterior.

Concretely, the network meta-analysis models of Chapter 15 and the multilevel models of Part V are Bayesian hierarchical models fit by Markov chain Monte Carlo; the conjugacy proved here is the one piece of them that can be done by hand, and it is worth seeing once in closed form before the machine takes over.

Theorem 13.6 (Normal-normal conjugacy for the mean effect) Take the random-effects model Equation 13.8 with the conjugate prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_0, \sigma_0^2)\) on the mean effect and any prior \(\pi(\tau^2)\) on the heterogeneity variance. Conditional on \(\tau^2\) and the data, the posterior of \(\theta\) is Gaussian, \[ \theta \mid \hat\theta_1, \dots, \hat\theta_k, \tau^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(m_\ast, v_\ast), \qquad \frac{1}{v_\ast} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{s_i^2 + \tau^2}, \qquad m_\ast = v_\ast\Big(\frac{\theta_0}{\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{\hat\theta_i}{s_i^2 + \tau^2}\Big). \tag{13.16}\] (Corresponds to C.8 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof. Given \(\tau^2\), the marginal model Equation 13.9 makes the \(\hat\theta_i\) independent with \(\hat\theta_i \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, s_i^2 + \tau^2)\) and known variances \(v_i := s_i^2 + \tau^2\). We process the observations sequentially and invoke normal-normal conjugacy Theorem 8.3 at each step. Start from the prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu^{(0)}, \varsigma^{(0)2})\) with \(\mu^{(0)} = \theta_0\) and \(\varsigma^{(0)2} = \sigma_0^2\). Suppose after incorporating \(\hat\theta_1, \dots, \hat\theta_{i-1}\) the posterior is \(\mathcal{N}(\mu^{(i-1)}, \varsigma^{(i-1)2})\). Treating this as the prior and observing \(\hat\theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, v_i)\), Theorem 8.3 gives the updated posterior \(\mathcal{N}(\mu^{(i)}, \varsigma^{(i)2})\) with \[ \frac{1}{\varsigma^{(i)2}} = \frac{1}{\varsigma^{(i-1)2}} + \frac{1}{v_i}, \qquad \frac{\mu^{(i)}} {\varsigma^{(i)2}} = \frac{\mu^{(i-1)}}{\varsigma^{(i-1)2}} + \frac{\hat\theta_i}{v_i} . \] These recursions are additive, so after all \(k\) updates \[ \frac{1}{\varsigma^{(k)2}} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{v_i}, \qquad \frac{\mu^{(k)}} {\varsigma^{(k)2}} = \frac{\theta_0}{\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{\hat\theta_i}{v_i} . \] Identifying \(v_\ast = \varsigma^{(k)2}\) and \(m_\ast = \mu^{(k)}\) gives Equation 13.16. The conclusion that the posterior is exactly Gaussian at each step, hence at the last, is the content of Theorem 8.3. \(\square\)

Remark. Reader translation. Read Equation 13.16 as precision bookkeeping: a Gaussian prior contributes precision \(1/\sigma_0^2\) centered at \(\theta_0\), each trial contributes precision \(1/(s_i^2 + \tau^2)\) centered at \(\hat\theta_i\), the posterior precision is their sum, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of all of them. Theorem 8.3 is the fact that a normal prior and normal data give a normal posterior, so the update is exact rather than approximate. Numerically, for the running trio with \(\tau^2\) fixed at the DerSimonian-Laird value \(0.048\) and a vague prior, the posterior is \(\mathcal{N}(0.365,\, 0.027)\), matching the frequentist random-effects estimate and its Wald variance exactly, which is the content of the next remark. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Flat-prior limit and the frequentist tie. Let the prior become uninformative, \(\sigma_0^2 \to \infty\). Then \(v_\ast^{-1} \to \sum_i \tilde w_i\) and \(m_\ast \to \sum_i \tilde w_i \hat\theta_i/\sum_i \tilde w_i = \hat\theta_R\), with \(v_\ast \to 1/\sum_i \tilde w_i = \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_R)\). The conditional Bayesian posterior mean and variance coincide exactly with the random-effects point estimate and its Wald variance. The Bayesian and frequentist random-effects analyses agree at the conditional level; they differ in how they propagate the uncertainty in \(\tau^2\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The full posterior and the link to REML. With \(\tau^2\) unknown there is no conjugate prior for it in this model, so the joint posterior \(\pi(\theta, \tau^2 \mid \text{data}) \propto \pi(\tau^2)\,\pi(\theta) \prod_i \mathcal{N}(\hat\theta_i; \theta, s_i^2 + \tau^2)\) is computed numerically, typically by Markov chain Monte Carlo (Chapter 24) with a half-normal or half-\(t\) prior on \(\tau\), which behave better than inverse-gamma priors when \(\tau\) may be near zero. Integrating \(\theta\) out of the likelihood under the improper flat prior \(\pi(\theta) \propto 1\) gives, by the Gaussian integral (obtained by completing the square in \(\theta\)) \(\int \exp(-\tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \theta)^2)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \sqrt{2\pi/\sum_i \tilde w_i}\,\exp(-\tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i(\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2)\), the marginal likelihood \[ L_R(\tau^2) = \int \prod_i \mathcal{N}(\hat\theta_i; \theta, s_i^2 + \tau^2)\,\mathrm{d}\theta \;\propto\; \Big(\prod_i (s_i^2 + \tau^2)\Big)^{-1/2}\Big(\sum_i \tilde w_i\Big)^{-1/2}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 \sum_i \tilde w_i (\hat\theta_i - \hat\theta_R)^2\Big), \] whose logarithm is exactly Equation 13.12. The REML estimator of \(\tau^2\) is therefore the Bayesian marginal-likelihood (type-II maximum likelihood, that is, maximizing the likelihood after integrating the mean \(\theta\) out) estimator under a flat prior on the mean effect, which is why REML and the marginal posterior mode of \(\tau^2\) so often agree. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

13.9 Egger’s test for small-study effects

Small studies are more prone to selective reporting than large ones, and the resulting small-study effects distort the pooled estimate. The funnel plot displays \(\hat\theta_i\) against its precision; under no bias it is symmetric, and asymmetry signals small-study effects. Egger’s test quantifies the asymmetry by a regression.

The funnel plot, and what “precision” means. Plot each trial as a point: its effect estimate \(\hat\theta_i\) on the horizontal axis, its precision \(1/s_i\) (large for big, tight trials; small for little, noisy ones) on the vertical axis. Large precise trials sit near the top and, if all is well, cluster tightly around the pooled effect; small imprecise trials sit near the bottom and scatter widely to both sides. The cloud then resembles an inverted funnel that is symmetric about the pooled effect when there is no bias. Asymmetry, small trials leaning to one side, is the signature of small-study effects, for instance small trials with unimpressive results going unpublished. For the running example’s five trials:

Theorem 13.7 (Egger’s regression test) Define the standardized effect \(z_i = \hat\theta_i/s_i\) and the precision \(p_i = 1/s_i\), and consider the ordinary least squares regression \[ z_i = \alpha + \beta\, p_i + u_i . \tag{13.17}\] Two facts hold.

  1. Null behavior. Under the fixed-effect model Equation 13.1 with no small-study effects, \(z_i = \theta p_i + u_i\) with \(u_i = \varepsilon_i/s_i\) having mean \(0\) and variance \(1\); the population regression Equation 13.17 then has intercept \(\alpha = 0\) and slope \(\beta = \theta\).

  2. Algebraic identity. The fitted Egger intercept \(\hat\alpha\) equals the slope coefficient in the precision-weighted (weights \(w_i = 1/s_i^2\)) regression of the effect \(\hat\theta_i\) on its standard error \(s_i\); and the fitted Egger slope \(\hat\beta\) equals the intercept of that weighted regression.

Consequently the test of \(H_0: \alpha = 0\), carried out as the usual \(t\) test on the intercept of Equation 13.17 with \(k - 2\) degrees of freedom, is a test for a linear dependence of the effect estimate on its standard error, that is, for funnel-plot asymmetry. (Corresponds to C.9 in the proof catalogue.)

Proof.

  1. Under Equation 13.1, \(\hat\theta_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = s_i^2\). Divide by \(s_i\): \[ z_i = \frac{\hat\theta_i}{s_i} = \frac{\theta}{s_i} + \frac{\varepsilon_i}{s_i} = \theta\, p_i + u_i, \qquad u_i := \frac{\varepsilon_i}{s_i}, \] and \(\mathbb{E}[u_i] = 0\), \(\mathrm{Var}(u_i) = s_i^2/s_i^2 = 1\), so the errors are homoscedastic. Hence the data-generating line through the origin has intercept \(0\) and slope \(\theta\); the population least squares fit of Equation 13.17 recovers \(\alpha = 0\), \(\beta = \theta\).

  2. Egger’s regression minimizes \(\sum_i (z_i - \alpha - \beta p_i)^2\) over \((\alpha, \beta)\). Substitute \(z_i = \hat\theta_i/s_i\) and \(p_i = 1/s_i\) and factor out \(1/s_i^2\): \[ \sum_i (z_i - \alpha - \beta p_i)^2 = \sum_i \Big(\frac{\hat\theta_i}{s_i} - \alpha - \frac{\beta}{s_i} \Big)^2 = \sum_i \frac{1}{s_i^2}\big(\hat\theta_i - \beta - \alpha\, s_i\big)^2 = \sum_i w_i\big(\hat \theta_i - (\beta + \alpha s_i)\big)^2 . \] The right-hand side is precisely the objective of the weighted least squares regression of \(\hat\theta_i\) on the covariate \(s_i\) with weights \(w_i = 1/s_i^2\), where the role of the intercept is played by \(\beta\) and the role of the slope on \(s_i\) by \(\alpha\). Minimizing the identical objective produces identical minimizers, so the weighted regression of \(\hat\theta_i\) on \(s_i\) has intercept \(\hat\beta\) and slope \(\hat\alpha\). This is the claimed identity. Inference on \(\hat\alpha\) uses the standard normal-theory \(t\) statistic for a regression coefficient (Theorem 6.8) with \(n = k\) points and \(p = 2\) parameters, hence \(k - 2\) degrees of freedom. \(\square\)

Remark. The identity (ii) is the conceptual content of the test. A nonzero intercept \(\alpha\) means the effect estimate \(\hat\theta_i\) varies systematically with its standard error \(s_i\): large, imprecise studies sit at a different effect level than small, precise ones, which is exactly funnel asymmetry. The weighted intercept \(\hat\beta\), the extrapolation of the effect to \(s_i = 0\) (infinite precision), is the “precision-effect test” estimate of the bias-adjusted effect. Egger’s test has low power for \(k < 10\) studies and conflates genuine small-study reporting effects with ordinary between-study heterogeneity, since heterogeneity also breaks the homoscedasticity assumed in part (i); trim-and-fill, the funnel plot itself, and the PET-PEESE family are common complements. We include the test for completeness; it is diagnostic, not confirmatory. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why this matters for ITC. An indirect comparison is only as trustworthy as the direct comparisons it is built from. If the \(A\)-versus-\(C\) evidence is distorted by small-study effects, the Bucher contrast \(\hat d_{AB} = \hat d_{AC} - \hat d_{BC}\) of Chapter 14 and the network estimates of Chapter 15 inherit that distortion. Screening each pairwise contrast for funnel asymmetry is therefore part of judging whether the evidence base is solid enough to compare treatments indirectly at all. The caution above applies with force: with few trials the test is underpowered and confounds reporting bias with ordinary heterogeneity, so it informs judgment rather than deciding it. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Egger’s test. Forming \(z_i = \hat\theta_i/s_i\) and \(p_i = 1/s_i\) and fitting Equation 13.17 by least squares gives intercept \(\hat\alpha = 2.634\) with standard error \(0.815\), so \(t = 2.634/0.815 = 3.23\) on \(k - 2 = 3\) degrees of freedom, two-sided \(p = 0.048\). The algebraic identity of Theorem 13.7 is visible in the companion weighted regression of \(\hat\theta_i\) on \(s_i\): its slope is \(2.634\) (equal to \(\hat\alpha\)) and its intercept is \(-0.116\) (equal to \(\hat\beta\)). Taken at face value the test flags asymmetry, but with \(k = 5\) it is severely underpowered and unstable; the “signal” is essentially the single precise low-effect study C, and no conclusion about reporting bias is warranted from five trials. The example shows both the mechanics and the fragility of the test.

Decision impact: which interval, and why. It is worth stating what a decision-maker would actually conclude from these five trials, because the three analyses tell noticeably different stories. The fixed-effect interval \(0.266 \pm 1.96(0.069) = (0.130, 0.402)\) is the narrowest, but it excludes no effect only because it assumes the five trials share one effect, which the \(I^2\) of \(30\%\) already questions. The random-effects Wald interval \((0.127, 0.481)\) widens to admit heterogeneity, yet still trusts a normal quantile and a plug-in \(\tau^2\). The Hartung-Knapp interval \((0.058, 0.550)\) is wider still and is the only one whose stated \(95\%\) coverage survives having just \(k = 5\) trials. All three keep the effect positive here, but the strength of that conclusion weakens as the interval widens; a cautious analyst reports the Hartung-Knapp interval and treats the fixed-effect interval as an optimistic bound. This is the pattern to expect whenever heterogeneity is present and trials are few, which is the normal situation in health technology assessment, and it is the same caution that will attend every indirect comparison built on these pooled inputs.

Exercise 13.1 (Which trial gets more weight? (warm-up)) Two trials estimate the same log odds ratio: trial \(P\) reports \(\hat\theta_P = 0.30\) with \(s_P^2 = 0.01\), and trial \(R\) reports \(\hat\theta_R = 0.10\) with \(s_R^2 = 0.04\). (a) Compute the inverse-variance weights \(w_P\) and \(w_R\), and state in words how many times more weight the more precise trial receives and why. (b) Compute the fixed-effect estimate \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{FE}}\) and check that it lies closer to the more precise trial. (c) Without recomputing from scratch, describe what happens to the two weights and to their ratio if a heterogeneity variance \(\tau^2 = 0.03\) is added to each \(s_i^2\), and connect this to the random-effects reweighting of Section Section 13.4.

Exercise 13.2 (Interpreting \(I^2\) (interpretation)) A meta-analysis of \(k = 8\) trials reports \(I^2 = 60\%\). For each statement, say whether it is true or false and why. (a) “\(60\%\) of the eight trials are biased.” (b) “There is a \(60\%\) probability that heterogeneity is present.” (c) “About \(60\%\) of the observed variability among the estimates exceeds what sampling error alone would produce.” (d) “The pooled effect is wrong by \(60\%\).” (e) “A fixed-effect analysis is therefore the safe choice.” Justify each answer from Definition 13.3 and Proposition 13.3.

Exercise 13.3 (Fixed-effect pipeline by hand (\(\star\))) Three trials report a mean difference: \(\hat\theta_1 = 1.2\) with \(s_1^2 = 0.16\), \(\hat\theta_2 = 0.8\) with \(s_2^2 = 0.09\), \(\hat\theta_3 = 1.5\) with \(s_3^2 = 0.25\). Exercise 13.4 (Optimality and the equality case) Prove from scratch, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, that any linear unbiased estimator \(\sum_i a_i \hat\theta_i\) of \(\theta\) under Equation 13.1 has variance at least \(1/\sum_i w_i\), and that equality holds if and only if \(a_i = w_i/\sum_j w_j\). Then show, without Lagrange multipliers, that the same conclusion follows by writing \(a_i = w_i/W + d_i\) with \(\sum_i d_i = 0\) and expanding the variance. Hint. For the first route, apply Cauchy-Schwarz to the vectors \((a_i s_i)_i\) and \((1/s_i)_i\) exactly as in the remark after Theorem 13.1. For the second, expand \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) = \sum_i a_i^2 s_i^2\) with \(a_i = w_i/W + d_i\); the cross term vanishes because \(\sum_i d_i = 0\), leaving \(1/W\) plus a nonnegative remainder \(\sum_i d_i^2 s_i^2\) that is zero only when every \(d_i = 0\).

(c) Explain why the truncation bias is largest when the true \(\tau^2\) is near zero and negligible when \(\tau^2\) is large.

Starting from the REML estimating equation Equation 13.13, show that \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}} = \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_i (\hat\theta_i - \bar{\hat\theta})^2 - s^2\) (truncated at \(0\)), where \(\bar{\hat\theta}\) is the unweighted mean.
