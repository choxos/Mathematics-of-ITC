Appendix B — Matrix Calculus

Several of the central derivations of this book are exercises in differentiating a function whose argument is a vector or a matrix. The normal equations of least squares come from setting the gradient of a quadratic form to zero (Definition 3.6 and the least-squares chapter); the score and Fisher information of a likelihood are a gradient and a negative expected Hessian (Definition 5.5); the restricted likelihood used for variance components in hierarchical models and meta-analysis is built from a log-determinant and an inverse-quadratic form (Chapters 8 and 13); and the calibration of matching weights and the variance propagation of population-adjusted estimators rest on Jacobians (Chapters 18 and 19). This appendix collects the matrix-calculus identities those chapters use and proves each one. It is reference material, but reference material with proofs: every formula below is derived from the definition of the derivative, so the reader can trust it and reconstruct it.

How to read this appendix. The identities here are tools, and different readers need different tools. If your goal is least squares and the normal equations, read the gradient and quadratic-form identities (Section B.4) and stop. If your goal is the delta method and variance propagation for MAIC and STC (Chapters 18 and 19), read the Jacobian convention (Definition B.2) and the chain rule (Theorem B.8). If your goal is restricted maximum likelihood and random-effects meta-analysis (Chapters 8 and 13), read the trace, inverse, and log-determinant identities (Section B.5) together with the worked covariance example (Section B.8). If your goal is efficient computation of a marginal likelihood in ML-NMR (Chapters 20 to 25), read the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity (Section B.6). A one-line map from each identity to the chapter that consumes it is the closing table of the appendix (Section B.10); a reader who wants that map first may jump there now and use it as a roadmap.

Two reading aids appear throughout. A short reader translation in italics follows each identity, saying in plain words what it computes and where the book uses it. A shape check in parentheses records the size of each factor: the single most common error in matrix calculus is a stray transpose or a nonconformable product, and a shape check catches it on sight.

The treatment is deliberately self-contained. We fix one set of layout conventions, compatible with the gradient and Jacobian already used in the foundations chapters, and we adopt the differential as the working tool. Differentials turn matrix differentiation into ordinary algebra: one expands a perturbation to first order, reads off a linear term, and identifies the derivative from it. This avoids the index gymnastics that make componentwise differentiation error-prone, while remaining completely rigorous. The prerequisites are the determinant, the adjugate and Cramer’s rule, and the characterization of invertibility, all developed in Strang (2016) and in Theorem 1.6.

Throughout, \(\|\cdot\|\) denotes any fixed norm on the relevant finite-dimensional space; because all norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent, the Landau symbols \(O(\cdot)\) and \(o(\cdot)\) do not depend on the choice. For matrices we will most often use the Frobenius norm \(\|A\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2}\). We write \(E_{ij}=\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^\top\) for the standard basis matrix with a single \(1\) in position \((i,j)\) and zeros elsewhere. The symbol \(X^{-\top}\) abbreviates \((X^{-1})^\top=(X^\top)^{-1}\). See Notation for the standing conventions on bold vectors and matrices.

A note on symbols used only here. Two small conventions differ from the rest of the book, and we flag them so the reader is not tripped up. First, matrix calculus is traditionally written with plain capital letters for generic matrices; accordingly, in this appendix \(A\), \(X\), \(U\), \(V\), \(C\) denote generic matrices, not random variables, not the design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) of the regression chapters, and not a covariance matrix, while bold letters such as \(\mathbf{x}\), \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{1}_n\) are reserved for vectors. This is why the matrices here are unbolded even though the book’s standing convention (see Notation) bolds matrices; where a plain capital could be confused with a book-wide symbol we say so explicitly. Second, two pieces of asymptotic shorthand recur. The symbol \(o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\) denotes a remainder that, divided by \(\|\mathbf{h}\|\), tends to \(0\) as \(\mathbf{h}\to\mathbf{0}\): it is strictly smaller than linear in the perturbation, so it disappears when we identify a derivative. The symbol \(O(\|\mathbf{h}\|^2)\) denotes a remainder bounded in absolute value by a constant times \(\|\mathbf{h}\|^2\), that is, at most quadratic; a quadratic remainder is in particular \(o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\). The phrase “\(U\) is open” may be read as “\(U\) contains a small ball around each of its points,” so that a small perturbation in any direction stays inside \(U\) and the function is defined there; a reader who has not met open sets may replace “open \(U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\)” by “a neighborhood of the point in question” without losing anything. Finally, \(f\) is called \(C^2\) if it has continuous second-order partial derivatives; this is the standard condition under which the mixed partials are equal, so the Hessian is symmetric.

B.1 A scalar warm-up: derivatives as first-order approximations

Before any matrices, we recall the one idea that all of matrix calculus generalizes. For a differentiable scalar function of one real variable, the derivative \(f'(x)\) is the number for which \[ f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)\,h+o(h)\qquad(h\to 0). \tag{B.1}\] Read this as: near \(x\), the function changes by approximately \(f'(x)\,h\), a quantity linear in the small step \(h\), plus an error \(o(h)\) too small to matter as \(h\to 0\). Every definition in this appendix is this one sentence with \(h\) replaced by a vector or a matrix perturbation and the ordinary product \(f'(x)\,h\) replaced by an inner product. Nothing more abstract is going on; matrix calculus is single-variable calculus with the slope promoted to a vector or a matrix.

The differential is simply a name for the linear part \(f'(x)\,h\). To see it in the smallest possible case, take \(f(x)=x^2\). Then \[ f(x+h)=(x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2, \] so the change \(f(x+h)-f(x)=2xh+h^2\) splits into a linear part \(2xh\) and a quadratic remainder \(h^2=o(h)\). We write \(df=2x\,dx\), meaning: the first-order change in \(f\) caused by a small change \(dx\) in \(x\) is \(2x\,dx\), and we read the derivative \(f'(x)=2x\) off the coefficient of \(dx\). The whole method of this appendix is exactly this three-step move, expand, discard everything smaller than linear, read off the coefficient, carried out for functions whose argument is a vector or a matrix. The single new feature is that matrices do not commute, so the coefficient may sit to the left or to the right of the perturbation \(dX\), and that order must be respected; getting it wrong is what produces a spurious transpose.

Geometrically, the derivative is the best linear approximation to the function at a point: a slope in one dimension, a direction of steepest ascent (the gradient) for a scalar field, a local linear map (the Jacobian) for a vector field, and a curvature (the Hessian) for the second order. Keeping those pictures in mind makes the algebra below easier to remember.

B.2 Conventions: gradient, Jacobian, and the matrix derivative

We first pin down what “the derivative” means for the three cases we need: a scalar function of a vector, a vector function of a vector, and a scalar function of a matrix. The conventions chosen here are exactly those already in force in the inference and regression chapters, so the formulas of this appendix can be dropped into those chapters without relabeling.

One choice deserves to be named, because it is the source of most apparent contradictions between textbooks. There are two competing traditions, called numerator layout and denominator layout, for arranging the partial derivatives of a function into an array. They differ by a transpose. We use denominator layout, meaning the derivative is given the same shape as the variable: the gradient of a scalar function of the vector \(\mathbf{x}\) is a column vector shaped like \(\mathbf{x}\), and the derivative of a scalar function of the matrix \(X\) is a matrix shaped like \(X\). This is the natural choice because it puts the gradient in the same space as the argument, so that a step \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto \mathbf{x}-\eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\) makes sense without a transpose. The practical warning is this: if you meet a formula in a paper that disagrees with ours by a single transpose, it is almost certainly a layout difference and not an error. Numerator layout would report the gradient as a row; we never do.

Definition B.1 (Gradient of a scalar function of a vector) Let \(U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\) be open and \(f:U\to\mathbb{R}\). We say \(f\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}\in U\) if there is a vector \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\), the gradient, with \[ f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})=f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top\mathbf{h}+o(\|\mathbf{h}\|) \qquad\text{as }\mathbf{h}\to\mathbf{0}. \tag{B.2}\] The gradient is a column vector of the same shape as \(\mathbf{x}\), with entries \([\nabla f(\mathbf{x})]_i=\partial f/\partial x_i\).

Reader translation. The gradient is the vector version of a slope: it collects the \(n\) partial derivatives into one column so that \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top\mathbf{h}\) gives the first-order change in \(f\) when we step from \(\mathbf{x}\) to \(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h}\). It is a column, not a row, precisely so that it lives in the same space as \(\mathbf{x}\); that is what lets a step \(\mathbf{x}-\eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\) (gradient descent, and Fisher scoring in later chapters) even be written down. Its direction is the direction of steepest ascent of \(f\). In this book the gradient is the score \(\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ell(\boldsymbol\theta)\) of a log-likelihood (Definition 5.5) and the gradient of the least-squares objective that yields the normal equations.

This is the convention under which the least-squares objective \(S(\mathbf{b})\) has gradient \(\nabla S(\mathbf{b})=-2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\), a column vector, and under which the score \(\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ell(\boldsymbol\theta)\) of Definition 5.5 is a column vector in the parameter space.

Definition B.2 (Jacobian of a vector function of a vector) Let \(U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\) be open and \(\mathbf{g}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\). We say \(\mathbf{g}\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}\in U\) if there is a matrix \(J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\), the Jacobian, with \[ \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})+J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{h} +o(\|\mathbf{h}\|). \tag{B.3}\] Its entries are \([J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})]_{ab}=\partial g_a/\partial x_b\): row \(a\) collects the partials of output \(g_a\), column \(b\) the partials with respect to input \(x_b\).

Reader translation. The Jacobian is the local linear map that best approximates \(\mathbf{g}\) near \(\mathbf{x}\): it is the matrix by which you multiply a small input change \(\mathbf{h}\) to get the resulting output change, \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\approx J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{h}\). This is the object the delta method uses to push the variance of an estimator through a smooth transformation, which is exactly how the variance of a population-adjusted effect is propagated in the MAIC and STC chapters (Chapters 18 and 19). A concrete \(2\times 2\) Jacobian is computed in Example B.2 below.

This is the \(q\times p\) Jacobian \(\mathbf{D}=[\partial g_a/\partial\theta_b]\) of the delta method (Theorem 5.3). The two conventions are consistent: a scalar function \(f\) is the special case \(m=1\), where the Jacobian is the \(1\times n\) row vector \(J_f(\mathbf{x})\), and comparing Equation B.2 with Equation B.3 gives the relationship \[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x})=J_f(\mathbf{x})^\top. \tag{B.4}\] This equation looks like a paradox on first reading, so it is worth stating carefully: the gradient and the Jacobian of a scalar function are not the same object. The gradient \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\) is a column vector (denominator layout), while the Jacobian \(J_f(\mathbf{x})\) of the same scalar \(f\) is a \(1\times n\) row vector; one is literally the transpose of the other. There is no contradiction because they are two different arrangements of the same \(n\) partial derivatives. Throughout the book we use the gradient (the column). Say it once and the confusion never returns.

The Hessian of a twice differentiable \(f\) is the Jacobian of the gradient map \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\), \[ \nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x})=J_{\nabla f}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, \qquad [\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x})]_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}, \] which is symmetric whenever \(f\) is \(C^2\), by the equality of mixed partials.

Reader translation. The Hessian is curvature: it is the matrix of second derivatives, and it records how fast the gradient itself is changing. Its sign pattern classifies a stationary point (positive definite Hessian means a strict local minimum, negative definite a maximum, indefinite a saddle), which is exactly how the least-squares and maximum-likelihood objectives of this book are shown to have genuine optima. Its role in inference is central: the observed information is the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood, and the Fisher information \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol\theta)\) is its negative expectation (Definition 5.5), so the Hessian is what turns a fitted model into a standard error. It is also the matrix inverted at each step of Newton and Fisher scoring.

For functions of a matrix we use the natural extension: the derivative has the same shape as the matrix argument.

Definition B.3 (Derivative of a scalar function of a matrix) Let \(U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) be open and \(f:U\to\mathbb{R}\) differentiable at \(X\in U\), meaning that there is a matrix \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) with \[ f(X+H)=f(X)+\Big\langle \tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X},\,H\Big\rangle_F+o(\|H\|), \qquad \langle A,B\rangle_F:=\operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}. \tag{B.5}\] The matrix \(\partial f/\partial X\) has entries \([\partial f/\partial X]_{ij}=\partial f/\partial X_{ij}\), and \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_F\) is the Frobenius inner product.

Reader translation. The Frobenius inner product \(\langle A,B\rangle_F=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}\) is not an arbitrary ceremony involving a trace: it is exactly the ordinary dot product of \(A\) and \(B\) after you flatten each matrix into a long vector of its entries. So Equation B.5 says the same thing as the gradient definition, only with the \(mn\) entries of \(X\) laid out as a rectangle instead of a column. That is also why the derivative with respect to a matrix is itself a matrix and not a giant unwieldy array: we keep the \(mn\) partial derivatives arranged in the same rectangle as \(X\) (denominator layout), so that \([\partial f/\partial X]_{ij}=\partial f/\partial X_{ij}\) sits in the slot of the entry it differentiates. The simplest instance is \(f(X)=\operatorname{tr}(X)\), whose derivative is the identity \(I\); it is worked in Example B.1 once we have the differential rules.

The bridge between the differential of a matrix function and its derivative is the following identification, the single most useful tool in this appendix.

Lemma B.1 (Identification of the derivative from the differential) Let \(f:U\to\mathbb{R}\) be differentiable at \(X\in U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\). If a matrix \(G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) satisfies \[ f(X+H)=f(X)+\operatorname{tr}(G^\top H)+o(\|H\|)\qquad\text{for all }H, \] then \(\partial f/\partial X=G\). Such a \(G\) is unique.

Proof. Setting \(H=tE_{ij}\) for a scalar \(t\to 0\) in the hypothesis gives, after dividing by \(t\), \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{ij}} =\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(X+tE_{ij})-f(X)}{t} =\operatorname{tr}(G^\top E_{ij})=G_{ij}, \] because \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top E_{ij})=\sum_{k}(G^\top)_{ik}(E_{ij})_{ki}=G_{ij}\). Hence \(G=\partial f/\partial X\) entrywise, which is Equation B.5. Uniqueness is the same computation: if \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top H)=\operatorname{tr}(\tilde G^\top H)\) for all \(H\), take \(H=E_{ij}\) to get \(G_{ij}=\tilde G_{ij}\) for every \(i,j\). \(\square\)

In words: expand \(f(X+H)\), keep only the part linear in \(H\), write it as \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top H)\), and read off \(\partial f/\partial X=G\). Everything quadratic or smaller in \(H\) is absorbed into \(o(\|H\|)\). The same principle, with \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top H)\) replaced by \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{h}\) or \(J\mathbf{h}\), identifies gradients and Jacobians in the vector case.

Why is this the most useful tool here? Because it splits a hard problem into an easy one. Computing a derivative entry by entry means differentiating \(mn\) scalar functions and keeping the bookkeeping straight, which is where sign and transpose errors creep in. Computing a differential, by contrast, is pure algebra: it obeys a product rule, a transpose rule, and a trace rule (developed next), so one manipulates whole matrices at once. The identification lemma is the hinge that connects the two. Once we have massaged the linear part of \(f(X+H)\) into the canonical form \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top\,dX)\), the derivative is \(G\) for free, with no indices. Essentially every identity below is proved this way.

B.3 The differential and its algebra

It is convenient to manipulate the linear term abstractly. For a map \(F\) between matrix spaces (a scalar, vector, or matrix being special cases), the differential at \(X\) is the linear map \(H\mapsto dF(X)[H]\) defined by \[ F(X+H)=F(X)+dF(X)[H]+o(\|H\|). \] When the base point is understood we write \(dF\) for \(dF(X)[H]\) and treat \(dX:=H\) as a free increment. The derivative is recovered from the differential by Lemma B.1 in the scalar case and by reading off the coefficient matrix in the vector case. The following rules let us compute differentials of composite expressions without ever returning to indices.

What \(dX\) and \(dF\) mean, in plain words. Think of \(dX\) as a bookkeeping symbol standing for a small, arbitrary change applied to every entry of \(X\) at once (it is the perturbation \(H\), renamed). Then \(dF\) is the resulting first-order change in \(F\), the part of \(F(X+dX)-F(X)\) that is linear in \(dX\), with the smaller-than-linear remainder thrown away. This is precisely the scalar picture \(df=f'(x)\,dx\) from Section B.1, promoted to matrices. The one caution: \(dX\) is not a number, so when a rule produces a factor on each side of \(dX\), as in \(A\,(dX)\,B\), the order cannot be rearranged; the whole art of the method is to shuffle those factors, legally, until \(dX\) stands alone at the end inside a trace.

Lemma B.2 (Rules of differentials) Let \(F,G\) be differentiable matrix-valued maps and \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\) constants. Wherever the shapes are conformable, \[ \begin{aligned} d(\alpha F+\beta G)&=\alpha\,dF+\beta\,dG, &&\text{(linearity)}\\ d(FG)&=(dF)\,G+F\,(dG), &&\text{(product rule)}\\ d(F^\top)&=(dF)^\top, &&\text{(transpose)}\\ d\operatorname{tr}(F)&=\operatorname{tr}(dF). &&\text{(trace)} \end{aligned} \] A constant matrix \(C\) has \(dC=0\).

Proof. Linearity is immediate from the definition, since the first-order term of a sum is the sum of the first-order terms, and \(d C=0\) holds because a constant satisfies \(C(X+H)=C(X)\) exactly.

Product rule. With \(F=F(X)\), \(G=G(X)\) and increments \(dF,dG\) of order \(\|H\|\), \[ F(X+H)\,G(X+H) =\bigl(F+dF+o\bigr)\bigl(G+dG+o\bigr) =FG+(dF)G+F(dG)+\underbrace{(dF)(dG)}_{O(\|H\|^2)}+o(\|H\|). \] The cross term \((dF)(dG)\) is \(O(\|H\|^2)=o(\|H\|)\) because each factor is \(O(\|H\|)\) and the Frobenius norm is submultiplicative, meaning \(\|AB\|_F\le\|A\|_F\,\|B\|_F\) (the size of a product is at most the product of the sizes; this standard inequality is proved from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the rows of \(A\) and columns of \(B\)). So the product of two first-order changes is a second-order quantity, the matrix analogue of the discarded \(h^2\) in \(f(x)=x^2\): two small things multiplied give something an order of magnitude smaller still, negligible at first order. Collecting the linear part gives \(d(FG)=(dF)G+F(dG)\).

Transpose and trace are linear operations with constant coefficients, so they commute with taking the first-order term: \((F+dF)^\top=F^\top+(dF)^\top\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(F+dF)=\operatorname{tr}F +\operatorname{tr}(dF)\). \(\square\)

We will also use, repeatedly, the three elementary properties of the trace that turn a differential into the canonical form \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top\,dX)\).

Lemma B.3 (Properties of the trace) For matrices of conformable shape and \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\): \[ \operatorname{tr}(\alpha A+\beta B)=\alpha\operatorname{tr}A+\beta\operatorname{tr}B,\qquad \operatorname{tr}(A^\top)=\operatorname{tr}(A),\qquad \operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA). \] Consequently \(\operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)=\operatorname{tr}(B^\top A)=\langle A,B\rangle_F\), and the Frobenius inner product is symmetric.

Proof. Linearity is entrywise. For the transpose, \(\operatorname{tr}(A^\top)=\sum_i (A^\top)_{ii}=\sum_i A_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}(A)\). For the cyclic property, with \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\) and \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}\), \[ \operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{q}A_{ik}B_{ki} =\sum_{k=1}^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{p}B_{ki}A_{ik}=\operatorname{tr}(BA). \] The last claim follows since \(\operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)=\operatorname{tr}((A^\top B)^\top) =\operatorname{tr}(B^\top A)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}\). \(\square\)

The canonical-form recipe. These trace rules exist to serve one workflow, the recipe that recovers a matrix derivative from a differential. It has four steps.

  1. Differentiate. Apply the differential rules of Lemma B.2 to \(f(X)\), treating \(dX\) as a small perturbation. Discard at once every term containing two factors of \(dX\) (those are \(o(\|dX\|)\)).
  2. Trace it. Since \(f\) is scalar, \(df\) already equals its own trace; write \(df=\operatorname{tr}(df)\) if it is not yet a trace.
  3. Isolate \(dX\). Use the cyclic property to rotate the factors until \(dX\) sits alone at the right end, producing the form \(\operatorname{tr}(M\,dX)\) for some matrix \(M\).
  4. Read off. Rewrite \(M=G^\top\); then Lemma B.1 gives \(\partial f/\partial X=G\).

One warning is essential here, because the whole recipe leans on step 3. The cyclic property \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\) extends to longer products as a rotation: \[ \operatorname{tr}(ABC)=\operatorname{tr}(BCA)=\operatorname{tr}(CAB). \] You may rotate the factors around the circle, keeping their circular order. You may not permute them freely: in general \(\operatorname{tr}(ABC)\ne\operatorname{tr}(ACB)\), because swapping two factors breaks the circular order. Rotate, never reshuffle. Confusing the two is the commonest mistake in this style of calculation.

As a mini example of step 3, suppose a differentiation has produced the term \(\operatorname{tr}(A\,dX\,B)\) with \(A,B\) constant. Rotate \(dX\) to the end: \[ \operatorname{tr}(A\,dX\,B)=\operatorname{tr}(B A\,dX) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl((A^\top B^\top)^\top dX\bigr), \] so the coefficient is \(G=A^\top B^\top\) and the derivative of that piece is \(A^\top B^\top\). Notice we rotated \(B\) to the front, we did not swap it past \(A\).

Example B.1 (The two simplest matrix derivatives, from scratch) Take \(f(X)=\operatorname{tr}(X)\). By the trace rule \(d\operatorname{tr}(X)=\operatorname{tr}(dX) =\operatorname{tr}(I^\top\,dX)\), so Lemma B.1 reads off \(\partial f/\partial X=I\): the derivative of the trace is the identity matrix. Now take \(f(X)=\operatorname{tr}(AX)\) with \(A\) constant. The product and trace rules give \(d\operatorname{tr}(AX)=\operatorname{tr}(A\,dX) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl((A^\top)^\top dX\bigr)\), so \(\partial f/\partial X=A^\top\). These are the two identities of Theorem B.4, obtained here in one line each purely by the recipe, with no indices.

Reader translation. This is the payoff promised in the introduction: differentiating whole matrices by the recipe is quick and mechanical, whereas doing the same entry by entry (as in Theorem B.4’s alternative) is where errors hide. We reuse exactly this recipe for every identity in the next two sections.

B.4 Linear, quadratic, and bilinear forms

These are the derivatives that appear in least squares and in the quadratic-form arithmetic of the normal theory of regression.

Theorem B.1 (Derivative of a linear form and of a linear map) Let \(\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) and \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) be constant.

  1. The linear form \(f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{a}\) has gradient \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\).
  2. The linear map \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}\) has Jacobian \(J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})=A\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\).

Proof. For (1), \(f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{h} =f(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{h}\), with no remainder; comparison with Equation B.2 gives \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}\). Equivalently \(\partial(\sum_k a_k x_k)/\partial x_i=a_i\). For (2), \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})=A\mathbf{x}+A\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})+A\mathbf{h}\), again with no remainder, so Equation B.3 gives \(J_{\mathbf{g}}=A\). Entrywise, \(g_a=\sum_b A_{ab}x_b\) has \(\partial g_a/\partial x_b=A_{ab}\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. A linear form \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{x}\) has a constant gradient, namely its own coefficient vector \(\mathbf{a}\) (shape check: \(\mathbf{a}\) is \(n\times 1\), matching \(\mathbf{x}\)); a linear map \(A\mathbf{x}\) has constant Jacobian \(A\) (shape check: \(A\) is \(m\times n\), mapping an \(n\)-vector step to an \(m\)-vector step). These are the “slope equals the coefficient” facts that make the linear predictor \(\boldsymbol\eta=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\) differentiate to the design matrix, which is the first step of every score computation in the regression and GLM chapters.

Example B.2 (A concrete \(2\times 2\) Jacobian) Let \(\mathbf{g}:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\) be the nonlinear map \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})=(x_1^2 x_2,\; x_1+x_2^2)\). Its Jacobian collects the four partials, output by input: \[ J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})= \begin{pmatrix} \partial g_1/\partial x_1 & \partial g_1/\partial x_2\\[2pt] \partial g_2/\partial x_1 & \partial g_2/\partial x_2 \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} 2x_1 x_2 & x_1^2\\ 1 & 2x_2 \end{pmatrix}. \] At the point \(\mathbf{x}=(1,2)\) this is the numerical matrix \(J_{\mathbf{g}}(1,2)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\1&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Reading it as a local linear approximation: a small input step \(\mathbf{h}=(h_1,h_2)\) produces the output step \(J_{\mathbf{g}}(1,2)\,\mathbf{h}=(4h_1+h_2,\;h_1+4h_2)\) to first order. This is the picture behind the delta method (Theorem 5.3): to move a variance through \(\mathbf{g}\), sandwich the input covariance with this matrix.

Theorem B.2 (Derivative of a quadratic form) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be constant, not necessarily symmetric, and let \(q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\). Then \[ \nabla q(\mathbf{x})=(A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}, \qquad \nabla^2 q(\mathbf{x})=A+A^\top . \tag{B.6}\] If \(A\) is symmetric, \(\nabla q(\mathbf{x})=2A\mathbf{x}\) and \(\nabla^2 q(\mathbf{x})=2A\).

Proof. Expand about \(\mathbf{x}\): \[ q(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h}) =(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})^\top A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h}) =\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x} +\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{h}^\top A\mathbf{x} +\mathbf{h}^\top A\mathbf{h}. \] The term \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{h}\) is a scalar, hence equal to its transpose \(\mathbf{h}^\top A^\top\mathbf{x}\), so the two middle terms combine to \(\mathbf{h}^\top(A^\top+A)\mathbf{x}=\bigl((A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}\bigr)^\top\mathbf{h}\). The final term satisfies \(|\mathbf{h}^\top A\mathbf{h}|\le\|A\|\,\|\mathbf{h}\|^2=o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\). Therefore \[ q(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})=q(\mathbf{x})+\bigl((A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}\bigr)^\top\mathbf{h}+o(\|\mathbf{h}\|), \] and Equation B.2 identifies \(\nabla q(\mathbf{x})=(A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}\). The gradient is the linear map \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto(A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}\), whose Jacobian is \(A+A^\top\) by Theorem B.1; that Jacobian is the Hessian. When \(A=A^\top\) these reduce to \(2A\mathbf{x}\) and \(2A\). \(\square\)

This is the identity behind the normal equations: the least-squares objective expands as \(S(\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}-2\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} +\mathbf{b}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\mathbf{b}\), a quadratic form with symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\), so Theorem B.2 and Theorem B.1 give \(\nabla S(\mathbf{b})=-2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\) and Hessian \(2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\), positive semidefinite by Definition 3.7.

Reader translation. The gradient of \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\) is \((A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}\), the symmetrized matrix times \(\mathbf{x}\) (shape check: \(A+A^\top\) is \(n\times n\), \(\mathbf{x}\) is \(n\times 1\), so the gradient is \(n\times 1\), the same shape as \(\mathbf{x}\), as denominator layout demands). Setting this to zero is what produces the normal equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\); the Hessian \(2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) being positive semidefinite is what certifies that solution is a minimum, not a saddle. This one identity is the entire analytic content of least squares.

Example B.3 (A quadratic gradient two ways) Let \(q(\mathbf{x})=3x_1^2+2x_1x_2+x_2^2\). Writing \(q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\) with the symmetric \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) checks out, since \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}=3x_1^2+2\cdot 1\cdot x_1x_2+x_2^2\). Entrywise, \(\partial q/\partial x_1 =6x_1+2x_2\) and \(\partial q/\partial x_2=2x_1+2x_2\), so \(\nabla q=(6x_1+2x_2,\;2x_1+2x_2)\). By the formula Equation B.6, \(\nabla q=2A\mathbf{x} =2\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mathbf{x} =(6x_1+2x_2,\;2x_1+2x_2)\), in agreement. At \(\mathbf{x}=(1,1)\) both give \(\nabla q=(8,4)\), and the Hessian is \(2A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}6&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). The theorem also tolerates a nonsymmetric representation: taking instead \(A'=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) (which also gives \(q\)) yields the same gradient because \(A'+A'^\top=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}6&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\); this is exactly why the symmetrization \(A+A^\top\) appears in Equation B.6.

Theorem B.3 (Derivative of a bilinear form) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) be constant and \(\beta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{y}\) for \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\), \(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\). Then \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\beta=A\mathbf{y}, \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\beta=A^\top\mathbf{x}. \] Regarded instead as a function of the matrix \(A\) with \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\) fixed, \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{y}\bigr)=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top . \]

Proof. Fix \(\mathbf{y}\). Then \(\beta(\cdot,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{y})=(A\mathbf{y})^\top\mathbf{x}\) is a linear form in \(\mathbf{x}\) with coefficient vector \(A\mathbf{y}\), so \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\beta=A\mathbf{y}\) by Theorem B.1(1). Fix \(\mathbf{x}\). Then \(\beta(\mathbf{x},\cdot)=(A^\top\mathbf{x})^\top\mathbf{y}\) is a linear form in \(\mathbf{y}\) with coefficient \(A^\top\mathbf{x}\), so \(\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\beta=A^\top\mathbf{x}\). For the derivative in \(A\), write \(\beta=\sum_{i,j}x_i A_{ij}y_j\); then \(\partial\beta/\partial A_{ij}=x_i y_j\), which is the \((i,j)\) entry of the outer product \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top\). Equivalently, by the differential, \(\beta(A+H)-\beta(A)=\mathbf{x}^\top H\mathbf{y}=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^\top H) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top)^\top H\bigr)\), and Lemma B.1 gives \(\partial\beta/\partial A=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. Read left to right, \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{y}\) is linear in each of its three arguments, so each gradient is just the “other” object: differentiating in \(\mathbf{x}\) leaves \(A\mathbf{y}\), in \(\mathbf{y}\) leaves \(A^\top\mathbf{x}\), and in the matrix \(A\) leaves the outer product \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top\) (shape check: \(\mathbf{x}\) is \(m\times 1\) and \(\mathbf{y}^\top\) is \(1\times n\), so \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top\) is \(m\times n\), the shape of \(A\)). The outer-product rule \(\partial(\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{y})/\partial A=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^\top\) is the seed from which the trace derivatives of the next section grow.

B.5 Trace, inverse, and log-determinant

These identities power the restricted-likelihood computations of Chapters 8 and 13, where one must differentiate \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) and \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) with respect to variance parameters that enter \(\mathbf{V}\).

Why these two expressions, and not others. It is worth seeing, before the algebra, exactly where \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) and the inverse-quadratic form come from, because they are not arbitrary. A multivariate normal model with mean \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\) and covariance \(\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol\vartheta)\) (the covariance depending on variance parameters \(\boldsymbol\vartheta\), for instance a between-study variance \(\tau^2\)) has log-likelihood \[ \ell(\boldsymbol\beta,\boldsymbol\vartheta) =-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol\vartheta) -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol\vartheta)^{-1}\mathbf{r} +\text{const}, \qquad \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta, \tag{B.7}\] where \(\mathbf{r}\) is the residual vector. This is the single formula that makes the whole section relevant: to fit the model one maximizes Equation B.7, and to maximize it one needs its derivative with respect to \(\boldsymbol\vartheta\), which by the chain rule requires the derivatives of exactly \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) and \(\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\). The restricted (REML) log-likelihood of Chapters 8 and 13 adds one more log-determinant term of the same kind. So the two identities below are precisely the pieces a health researcher needs to differentiate a random-effects meta-analysis or mixed model likelihood; we assemble the resulting score explicitly at the end of Section B.7.

Theorem B.4 (Derivative of the trace of a product) For constant \(A\) and a matrix variable \(X\) of conformable shape, \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\operatorname{tr}(AX)=A^\top,\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A)=A,\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X)=(A+A^\top)X, \] and in particular \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial X}\operatorname{tr}(X)=I\).

Proof. We use Lemma B.2 and Lemma B.3, then Lemma B.1.

For \(\operatorname{tr}(AX)\): \(d\operatorname{tr}(AX)=\operatorname{tr}(A\,dX) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl((A^\top)^\top dX\bigr)\), so the derivative is \(A^\top\).

For \(\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A)\): \(d\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl((dX)^\top A\bigr) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(((dX)^\top A)^\top\bigr)=\operatorname{tr}(A^\top\,dX)\), so the derivative is \(A\).

For \(\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X)\), the product and trace rules give \[ d\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl((dX)^\top A X\bigr)+\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X^\top A\,dX\bigr). \] The first term equals \(\operatorname{tr}\bigl((AX)^\top dX\bigr)\) by transposing inside the trace, since \(((dX)^\top AX)^\top=X^\top A^\top\,dX\) has the same trace and \(X^\top A^\top=(AX)^\top\). The second term is already \(\operatorname{tr}\bigl((A^\top X)^\top dX\bigr)\) because \((A^\top X)^\top=X^\top A\). Adding, \(d\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(((A+A^\top)X)^\top dX\bigr)\), so the derivative is \((A+A^\top)X\). Setting \(A=I\) in the first identity gives \(\partial\operatorname{tr}(X)/\partial X=I\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. These are the workhorse trace derivatives. Notice the last one, \(\partial\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X)/\partial X=(A+A^\top)X\), is the matrix twin of the quadratic-form gradient \((A+A^\top)\mathbf{x}\); the same symmetrization appears for the same reason. It is instructive to appreciate what the differential method saved us: done entrywise one writes \(\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X) =\sum_{i,j,k}X_{ji}A_{jk}X_{ki}\), differentiates this triple sum in a generic entry \(X_{ab}\) (two terms, by the product rule on the two factors of \(X\)), and reassembles the pieces into a matrix; the proof above reached \((A+A^\top)X\) in three clean lines with no indices at all. That contrast is the entire argument for adopting differentials.

To differentiate the determinant and the inverse we first record how the inverse responds to a perturbation. Two classical objects appear, and we define them here at first use rather than assume them. The minor \(M_{ij}\) of a square matrix \(X\) is the determinant of the submatrix obtained by striking out row \(i\) and column \(j\). The cofactor is the signed minor \(C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}\), and the adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}(X)\) is the transpose of the cofactor matrix, \(\operatorname{adj}(X)=C^\top\). The one fact we borrow (from Strang (2016)) is Cramer’s rule in the form \(X^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}(X)/\det(X)\), valid whenever \(\det(X)\ne 0\). The proof below needs that the entries of \(X^{-1}\) are differentiable functions of the entries of \(X\); Cramer’s rule exhibits each entry of \(X^{-1}\) as a ratio of polynomials in the entries of \(X\) whose denominator \(\det(X)\) does not vanish on the open set of invertible matrices, so each entry is differentiable there (Strang 2016).

Theorem B.5 (Derivative of the inverse) Let \(X\) range over invertible matrices. As a differential, \[ d\bigl(X^{-1}\bigr)=-X^{-1}\,(dX)\,X^{-1}. \tag{B.8}\] Componentwise, \(\dfrac{\partial (X^{-1})_{kl}}{\partial X_{ij}}=-(X^{-1})_{ki}\,(X^{-1})_{jl}\). If \(X(t)\) depends differentiably on a scalar \(t\), then \(\dfrac{d}{dt}X(t)^{-1}=-X(t)^{-1}\dot X(t)\,X(t)^{-1}\).

Proof. Differentiate the identity \(X X^{-1}=I\). The right side is constant, so \(d(X X^{-1})=0\); the product rule of Lemma B.2 gives \((dX)X^{-1}+X\,d(X^{-1})=0\). Left-multiplying by \(X^{-1}\) and solving, \[ d(X^{-1})=-X^{-1}(dX)X^{-1}, \] which is Equation B.8. For the componentwise form take \(dX=E_{ij}\); then \(d(X^{-1})=-X^{-1}E_{ij}X^{-1}\), and \[ \bigl(X^{-1}E_{ij}X^{-1}\bigr)_{kl} =\sum_{p,q}(X^{-1})_{kp}(E_{ij})_{pq}(X^{-1})_{ql} =(X^{-1})_{ki}(X^{-1})_{jl}, \] where the double sum collapses because \(E_{ij}=\mathbf{e}_i\mathbf{e}_j^\top\) has a single nonzero entry, \((E_{ij})_{pq}=1\) only when \(p=i\) and \(q=j\); every other term is zero. Hence \(\partial(X^{-1})_{kl}/\partial X_{ij}=-(X^{-1})_{ki}(X^{-1})_{jl}\). The scalar-parameter statement is the same computation with \(dX\) replaced by \(\dot X(t)\,dt\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. The rule \(d(X^{-1})=-X^{-1}(dX)X^{-1}\) is the matrix version of the scalar fact \(d(1/x)=-x^{-2}\,dx\); the two copies of \(X^{-1}\), one on each side of \(dX\), are the price of noncommutativity, and their order cannot be swapped. This is exactly the identity that produces the \(-\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\partial\mathbf{V}/\partial\vartheta)\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) term in a variance-component score: it says how the inverse covariance changes when a variance parameter nudges \(\mathbf{V}\). We use it in that form in Section B.7 and Chapters 8 and 13.

Next, the differential of the determinant, classically Jacobi’s formula. It rests on the cofactor (Laplace) expansion and the adjugate identity \(X\,\operatorname{adj}(X)=\det(X)\,I\), both standard (Strang 2016). A reader who only needs the log-determinant derivative below (which is what the likelihood of Equation B.7 requires) may accept this lemma on first reading and skip its proof; the cofactor machinery is used only to prove it, not to apply it.

Lemma B.4 (Jacobi’s formula for the determinant) For any square \(X\), writing \(C=\operatorname{cof}(X)\) for the matrix of cofactors \(C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}\) (with \(M_{ij}\) the minor deleting row \(i\) and column \(j\)), \[ \frac{\partial \det(X)}{\partial X_{ij}}=C_{ij}, \qquad d\det(X)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\operatorname{adj}(X)\,dX\bigr). \] If in addition \(X\) is invertible, then \(\operatorname{adj}(X)=\det(X)\,X^{-1}\) and \[ \frac{\partial \det(X)}{\partial X}=\det(X)\,X^{-\top}, \qquad d\det(X)=\det(X)\,\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X^{-1}\,dX\bigr). \tag{B.9}\]

Proof. Fix a row index \(i\). The Laplace expansion along row \(i\) reads \(\det(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{ik}\,C_{ik}\), and crucially each cofactor \(C_{ik}\) is built from the minor that deletes row \(i\), so \(C_{ik}\) does not depend on any entry of row \(i\). To see this last point plainly: the minor \(M_{ik}\) is by definition the determinant of the submatrix obtained by striking out row \(i\) and column \(k\), so not a single entry \(X_{i1},\dots,X_{in}\) of row \(i\) survives in it; therefore \(\partial C_{ik}/\partial X_{ij}=0\) for every \(j\). Differentiating \(\det(X)=\sum_k X_{ik}C_{ik}\) in \(X_{ij}\), only the explicit factor \(X_{ik}\) can contribute, and it does so only when \(k=j\), giving \(\partial\det(X)/\partial X_{ij}=C_{ij}\). Assembling the differential, \[ d\det(X)=\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}\,dX_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(C^\top\,dX\bigr) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\operatorname{adj}(X)\,dX\bigr), \] since the adjugate is the transpose of the cofactor matrix, \(\operatorname{adj}(X)=C^\top\). When \(X\) is invertible, the adjugate identity \(X\,\operatorname{adj}(X)=\det(X)\,I\) gives \(\operatorname{adj}(X)=\det(X)\,X^{-1}\); substituting yields \(d\det(X)=\det(X)\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX)\), and because \(\operatorname{adj}(X)=C^\top\) we get \(C=\det(X)(X^{-1})^\top\), that is \(\partial\det(X)/\partial X=\det(X)X^{-\top}\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. Jacobi’s formula says the determinant changes, to first order, in proportion to \(\det(X)\) times the inverse transpose: \(d\det(X)=\det(X)\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}\,dX)\). We almost never need the raw determinant derivative; we need its logarithm, obtained next by one application of the chain rule, because it is \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) (not \(\det\mathbf{V}\)) that appears in every Gaussian log-likelihood Equation B.7.

Theorem B.6 (Derivative of the log-determinant) Let \(X\) range over matrices with \(\det(X)>0\). Then \[ d\log\det(X)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X^{-1}\,dX\bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\log\det(X)=X^{-\top}=(X^{-1})^\top . \tag{B.10}\] If \(X\) is symmetric and invertible, \(\partial\log\det(X)/\partial X=X^{-1}\).

Proof. The map \(X\mapsto\log\det(X)\) is the composition of \(t\mapsto\log t\) with \(X\mapsto\det(X)\), both differentiable where \(\det(X)>0\). The ordinary chain rule for the outer scalar function gives \[ d\log\det(X)=\frac{1}{\det(X)}\,d\det(X) =\frac{1}{\det(X)}\,\det(X)\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X^{-1}\,dX\bigr) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X^{-1}\,dX\bigr), \] using Equation B.9. Writing this as \(\operatorname{tr}\bigl((X^{-\top})^\top dX\bigr)\) and applying Lemma B.1 gives \(\partial\log\det(X)/\partial X=X^{-\top}\). For symmetric invertible \(X\) the inverse is symmetric, so \(X^{-\top}=X^{-1}\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. This is one of the two identities the whole section was aiming at. In words: the derivative of \(\log\det\) is the inverse (transposed, but for a symmetric covariance matrix the transpose does nothing). Combined with the inverse-derivative rule for the \(\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\) term, it delivers the gradient of the Gaussian log-likelihood Equation B.7 with respect to a variance parameter, hence the restricted-likelihood score of random-effects meta-analysis. The next remark records a layout subtlety that matters precisely because covariance matrices are symmetric.

Remark. The symmetric case hides a layout subtlety worth stating, because covariance and precision matrices are symmetric. There are two different questions, and they have different answers.

Question one: all entries free. If one differentiates \(f(X)=\log\det X\) treating all \(n^2\) entries as independent, the answer is \(X^{-\top}\) as proved above. This is the derivative reported by the theorem.

Question two: only the free parameters of a symmetric matrix. If instead \(X\) is constrained symmetric and one differentiates with respect to the \(\binom{n+1}{2}\) genuinely free parameters (the diagonal and one triangle), then a diagonal parameter \(X_{ii}\) moves one entry, but an off-diagonal free parameter \(X_{ij}\) with \(i<j\) moves two entries at once, \(X_{ij}\) and \(X_{ji}\). By the chain rule the derivative with respect to that one parameter is \(\partial f/\partial X_{ij}+\partial f/\partial X_{ji} =2(X^{-1})_{ij}\): the off-diagonal derivatives are doubled.

A \(2\times 2\) example makes the doubling concrete. Let \(X=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\ b&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) with free parameters \(a,b,c\), so \(\det X=ac-b^2\) and \(X^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{ac-b^2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c&-b\\ -b&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Differentiating \(f=\log(ac-b^2)\) directly, \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial a}=\frac{c}{ac-b^2}=(X^{-1})_{11}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial c}=\frac{a}{ac-b^2}=(X^{-1})_{22}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial b}=\frac{-2b}{ac-b^2}=2\,(X^{-1})_{12}. \] The diagonal parameters reproduce single entries of \(X^{-1}\), while the off-diagonal parameter \(b\) picks up a factor \(2\) because it sits in both off-diagonal slots. Arranging these into a symmetric matrix gives \[ \begin{pmatrix}(X^{-1})_{11}&2(X^{-1})_{12}\\ 2(X^{-1})_{12}&(X^{-1})_{22}\end{pmatrix} =2X^{-1}-\operatorname{diag}(X^{-1}), \] the “symmetric gradient” with off-diagonal entries doubled. This is a genuine chain-rule consequence, not a trap, once one is clear about which variables are being held free.

In the restricted-likelihood applications of Chapters 8 and 13 this subtlety does not arise: one differentiates with respect to a scalar variance parameter \(\vartheta\) that enters \(\mathbf{V}(\vartheta)\), and the chain rule of Section B.7 gives the unambiguous \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}\log\det\mathbf{V}(\vartheta) =\operatorname{tr}\!\Bigl(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\vartheta}\Bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}\bigl(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\bigr) =-\,\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\vartheta}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}, \] the second equality from Theorem B.5. These are the two pieces of the restricted-likelihood score. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

B.6 The Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity

A recurring computational need is to invert a matrix that differs from an easily inverted one by a low-rank correction. In random-effects meta-analysis and in mixed models the marginal covariance has the form \(\mathbf{V}=A+UCV\) with \(A\) diagonal (so trivially inverted) and \(UCV\) of small rank; the Woodbury identity reduces the inversion of \(\mathbf{V}\) to that of a small matrix.

A warning about the letter \(V\). In the identity below, \(V\) (plain, unbolded) is a generic rectangular factor, one of the four matrices \(A,U,C,V\) that build the low-rank update. It is not the covariance matrix \(\mathbf{V}\) (bold) of the likelihood Equation B.7, and it is not a vector space. The clash is unfortunate but the notation \(A+UCV\) is universal, so we keep it and simply flag it here.

What the four matrices are, concretely. It helps to have a picture before the algebra. Think of a mixed model or a random-effects meta-analysis whose marginal covariance is \(\mathbf{V}=A+UCV\). Here \(A\) is the easy diagonal part (within-study or residual variances, one per observation); \(U\) is a tall thin matrix that maps a few random effects to the many observations they influence; \(C\) is the small covariance of those random effects (often just a single between-study variance \(\tau^2\)); and \(V=U^\top\) closes the low-rank correction. Inverting the big \(n\times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{V}\) directly is expensive, but Woodbury turns it into inverting the tiny \(k\times k\) capacitance matrix \(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\), where \(k\) is the number of random effects. The compound-symmetry example of Section B.8 is the smallest instance, with \(k=1\): a single shared random effect, \(U=V^\top=\mathbf{1}_n\) and \(C=[\tau^2]\). The same low-rank structure appears in the marginal likelihood of ML-NMR (Chapters 20 to 25), where integrating out individual-level variation leaves a diagonal-plus-low-rank covariance of exactly this kind, and Woodbury is what keeps the likelihood computable.

We build up from the rank-one case.

Lemma B.5 (Sherman-Morrison rank-one update) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be invertible and \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\). If \(1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\ne 0\), then \(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\) is invertible and \[ \bigl(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\bigr)^{-1} =A^{-1}-\frac{A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}} . \tag{B.11}\]

Proof. Write \(c=\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\) (a scalar) and \(s=1+c\ne 0\), and let \(B\) denote the proposed right-hand side of Equation B.11. Multiply on the left by \(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\): \[ \bigl(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\bigr)B =AB+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top B. \] Compute each piece. First \[ AB=A A^{-1}-\frac{A A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}}{s} =I-\frac{\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}}{s}. \] Second, using \(\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}=c\), \[ \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top B =\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1} -\frac{\mathbf{u}\,(\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}}{s} =\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{c}{s}\Bigr) =\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\,\frac{s-c}{s} =\frac{\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}}{s}, \] since \(s-c=1\). Adding the two pieces, the corrections cancel and \(\bigl(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\bigr)B=I\). A square matrix with a right inverse is invertible, with that right inverse equal to the inverse (Theorem 1.6), so \(\bigl(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\bigr)^{-1}=B\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. Sherman-Morrison says: when you add a rank-one bump \(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\) to a matrix you can already invert, the new inverse is the old inverse minus a rank-one correction, and the only new scalar you must compute is the denominator \(1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\). No new matrix is inverted at all. It is worth verifying once on numbers before trusting the general case.

Example B.4 (Sherman-Morrison on a \(2\times 2\) matrix) Take \(A=I_2\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{1}_2=(1,1)^\top\), so \(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top=I_2+\mathbf{1}_2\mathbf{1}_2^\top =\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\ 1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Here \(A^{-1}=I_2\) and the denominator is \(1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}=1+\mathbf{1}_2^\top\mathbf{1}_2=1+2=3\), so Equation B.11 gives \[ \bigl(I_2+\mathbf{1}_2\mathbf{1}_2^\top\bigr)^{-1} =I_2-\tfrac{1}{3}\mathbf{1}_2\mathbf{1}_2^\top =\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\ -1&2\end{pmatrix}. \] Direct check: \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\ 1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\cdot \tfrac{1}{3}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\ -1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) has first row \(\tfrac{1}{3}(2\cdot 2+1\cdot(-1),\ 2\cdot(-1)+1\cdot 2)=\tfrac{1}{3}(3,0)=(1,0)\), and similarly the second row is \((0,1)\), confirming the identity. This is the \(n=2\) case of the compound-symmetry inverse computed in full in Section B.8.

Theorem B.7 (Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity) Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and \(C\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\) be invertible, and let \(U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\), \(V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\). If the capacitance matrix \(S:=C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\) is invertible, then \(A+UCV\) is invertible and \[ \bigl(A+UCV\bigr)^{-1} =A^{-1}-A^{-1}U\bigl(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\bigr)^{-1}VA^{-1}. \tag{B.12}\] The rank-one identity Equation B.11 is the case \(k=1\), \(C=[1]\), \(U=\mathbf{u}\), \(V=\mathbf{v}^\top\).

Proof. Let \(B\) denote the right-hand side of Equation B.12 and let \(M:=VA^{-1}U\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\), so \(S=C^{-1}+M\). Multiply on the left by \(A+UCV\) and expand: \[ (A+UCV)B=AB+UCVB. \] The first term is \[ AB=I-US^{-1}VA^{-1}. \] For the second term, factor \(VA^{-1}\) on the right: \[ UCVB=UCVA^{-1}-UCVA^{-1}U S^{-1}VA^{-1} =UC\bigl(I-M S^{-1}\bigr)VA^{-1}. \] Adding and collecting the common right factor \(VA^{-1}\) and left factor \(U\), \[ (A+UCV)B =I+U\Bigl\{-S^{-1}+C-CM S^{-1}\Bigr\}VA^{-1}. \] The braced \(k\times k\) matrix vanishes: \[ -S^{-1}+C-CM S^{-1} =C-(I+CM)S^{-1} =C-C\bigl(C^{-1}+M\bigr)S^{-1} =C-C S S^{-1} =C-C=0, \] using \(I+CM=C(C^{-1}+M)=CS\). Hence \((A+UCV)B=I\). A square matrix with a right inverse is invertible (Theorem 1.6), so \((A+UCV)^{-1}=B\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. Woodbury is Sherman-Morrison for a rank-\(k\) (rather than rank-one) correction: the \(n\times n\) inversion of \(A+UCV\) collapses to the \(k\times k\) inversion of the capacitance matrix \(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\), a huge saving when \(k\ll n\). The name “capacitance” is borrowed from electrical network theory, where this Schur-complement matrix arises; in this book it carries no electrical meaning and is simply the small matrix you end up inverting. This is the identity that makes the marginal likelihood of a mixed model, a random-effects meta-analysis, or ML-NMR computable at scale.

A companion identity handles the determinant, which is what the log-determinant term of a restricted likelihood requires. We first record the Schur-complement determinant formula, then specialize.

Lemma B.6 (Block determinant via the Schur complement) Let \(P=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\ C&D\end{pmatrix}\) be a square block matrix with \(A\) square and invertible. Then \[ \det P=\det(A)\,\det\bigl(D-CA^{-1}B\bigr). \]

Proof. Verify the block factorization \[ \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\ C&D\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\ CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\ 0&D-CA^{-1}B\end{pmatrix}, \] by multiplying the right side block by block. All four blocks check out: the \((1,1)\) block is \(I\cdot A+0\cdot 0=A\); the \((1,2)\) block is \(I\cdot B+0\cdot(D-CA^{-1}B)=B\); the \((2,1)\) block is \(CA^{-1}\cdot A+I\cdot 0=C\); and the \((2,2)\) block is \(CA^{-1}B+I\cdot(D-CA^{-1}B)=D\). So the factorization holds exactly. The first factor is block lower triangular with identity diagonal blocks, so its determinant is \(1\); the second is block upper triangular, so its determinant is the product \(\det(A)\det(D-CA^{-1}B)\) of the diagonal blocks (Strang 2016). Multiplicativity of the determinant gives the claim. \(\square\)

Corollary B.1 (Matrix determinant lemma) With \(A,C,U,V\) as in Theorem B.7 and \(A,C\) invertible, \[ \det\bigl(A+UCV\bigr)=\det(A)\,\det(C)\,\det\bigl(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\bigr). \tag{B.13}\] In particular, for the rank-one update, \(\det\bigl(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\bigr)=\det(A)\,\bigl(1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\bigr)\).

Proof. Apply Lemma B.6 to the block matrix \(P=\begin{pmatrix}A&U\\ -V&C^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\) in two ways. This particular block matrix is not pulled from nowhere: it is built so that eliminating one diagonal block produces the small capacitance matrix \(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\), while eliminating the other produces the large matrix \(A+UCV\). Computing \(\det P\) both ways and equating the results is what links the two determinants. Taking first the Schur complement of the invertible top-left block \(A\), \[ \det P=\det(A)\,\det\bigl(C^{-1}-(-V)A^{-1}U\bigr)=\det(A)\,\det\bigl(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U\bigr). \] Taking instead the Schur complement of the invertible bottom-right block \(C^{-1}\) (the same formula applied to the block matrix after swapping the roles of the two diagonal blocks), \[ \det P=\det\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)\,\det\bigl(A-U\,(C^{-1})^{-1}(-V)\bigr) =\det\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)\,\det\bigl(A+UCV\bigr). \] Equating the two expressions and using \(\det(C^{-1})=1/\det(C)\) gives Equation B.13. The rank-one case is \(k=1\), \(C=[1]\), where \(\det(C)=1\) and \(C^{-1}+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u} =1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\). \(\square\)

Reader translation. The matrix determinant lemma is Woodbury for determinants: the determinant of the big matrix \(A+UCV\) factors through the small capacitance determinant, so the \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) term of a likelihood (Equation B.7) is computed from a \(k\times k\) determinant instead of an \(n\times n\) one. The rank-one case \(\det(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top)=\det(A)(1+\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u})\) is the one used in the compound-symmetry determinant of Section B.8.

B.7 The chain rule for matrix functions

Finally, the rule that lets all of the above be composed. We state it for vector compositions, where it is the workhorse of the delta method (Theorem 5.3), and for a scalar parameter passing through a matrix, which is the form used for likelihood scores.

Theorem B.8 (Chain rule)  

  1. (Vector composition.) Let \(\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\) be differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{g}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}\) differentiable at \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\). Then \(\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}\) with \[ J_{\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}) =J_{\mathbf{g}}\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\,J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}). \tag{B.14}\] For a scalar outer function \(\phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(\nabla(\phi\circ\mathbf{f})(\mathbf{x})=J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})^\top\, \nabla\phi\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\).
  2. (Scalar parameter through a matrix.) Let \(X:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) be differentiable at \(t\) and \(f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}\) differentiable at \(X(t)\). Then \(\phi(t)=f(X(t))\) is differentiable at \(t\) with \[ \phi'(t)=\Big\langle \tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X}\big(X(t)\big),\,\dot X(t)\Big\rangle_F =\operatorname{tr}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X}\big(X(t)\big)^{\!\top}\dot X(t)\Bigr). \tag{B.15}\]

Proof. (1). Differentiability of \(\mathbf{f}\) gives \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{k}\) with \(\mathbf{k}=J_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{h}+o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\), so \(\|\mathbf{k}\|=O(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\) and \(\mathbf{k}\to\mathbf{0}\) as \(\mathbf{h}\to\mathbf{0}\). Differentiability of \(\mathbf{g}\) at \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\) gives \[ \mathbf{g}\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{k}\bigr) =\mathbf{g}\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)+J_{\mathbf{g}}\,\mathbf{k}+o(\|\mathbf{k}\|) =\mathbf{g}\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\bigr) +J_{\mathbf{g}}J_{\mathbf{f}}\,\mathbf{h} +\underbrace{J_{\mathbf{g}}\,o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)+o(\|\mathbf{k}\|)}_{o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)}. \] The error is \(o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\) because \(J_{\mathbf{g}}\,o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)=o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\) and \(o(\|\mathbf{k}\|)=o(O(\|\mathbf{h}\|))=o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)\). By Equation B.3 the Jacobian of the composition is \(J_{\mathbf{g}}J_{\mathbf{f}}\). The scalar case is Equation B.4 applied to \(\phi\circ\mathbf{f}\): \(\nabla(\phi\circ\mathbf{f})=J_{\phi\circ\mathbf{f}}^\top =(J_\phi J_{\mathbf{f}})^\top=J_{\mathbf{f}}^\top\,\nabla\phi\).

(2). This is part (1) after flattening the matrix into a vector: identify \(\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) with \(\mathbb{R}^{mn}\) by stacking columns, under which the Frobenius inner product becomes the Euclidean inner product. The choice of stacking order does not matter: the conclusion Equation B.15 is written invariantly as a Frobenius inner product (equivalently a trace), and a trace is unchanged by any consistent relabeling of the entries, so every flattening gives the same scalar \(\phi'(t)\). Concretely, \(X(t+\delta)=X(t)+\delta\,\dot X(t)+o(\delta)\), so by Equation B.5 \[ \phi(t+\delta)=f\bigl(X(t)+\delta\dot X(t)+o(\delta)\bigr) =f(X(t))+\Big\langle\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X},\,\delta\dot X(t)\Big\rangle_F+o(\delta), \] and dividing by \(\delta\) and letting \(\delta\to0\) gives Equation B.15. \(\square\)

Reader translation. The chain rule is what lets all the identities above be composed. Part (1) is the familiar “multiply the Jacobians” rule that powers the delta method (Theorem 5.3): to move a variance through a chain of transformations, multiply the local linear maps. Part (2) is the version used for likelihood scores: when a scalar parameter \(t\) (a variance component, say) enters a matrix, the derivative of any scalar function of that matrix is a single trace, \(\operatorname{tr}((\partial f/\partial X)^\top\dot X)\). The next remark applies it to exactly the two functions the Gaussian log-likelihood needs.

Remark. Two specializations of Equation B.15 were used in Section B.5. With \(f(X)=\log\det X\) and \(\partial f/\partial X=X^{-\top}\), Equation B.15 gives \(\frac{d}{dt}\log\det X(t)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl((X^{-\top})^\top\dot X\bigr) =\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}\dot X)\). With \(f(X)=\mathbf{a}^\top X^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) one combines Theorem B.5 with the chain rule to get \(\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mathbf{a}^\top X^{-1}\mathbf{a}\bigr) =-\mathbf{a}^\top X^{-1}\dot X X^{-1}\mathbf{a}\). These are exactly the building blocks of the restricted-likelihood score in Chapters 8 and 13. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Rather than leave those building blocks unassembled, we now build the score, since assembling it is the single computation this whole section exists to enable.

Example B.5 (Assembling a variance-component score) Consider the Gaussian log-likelihood Equation B.7 with residual \(\mathbf{r}\) held fixed and a covariance \(\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}(\vartheta)\) depending on a single variance parameter \(\vartheta\) (for instance the between-study variance \(\tau^2\)): \[ \ell(\vartheta)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\mathbf{V}(\vartheta) -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}(\vartheta)^{-1}\mathbf{r}+\text{const}. \] Write \(\mathbf{V}_\vartheta:=\partial\mathbf{V}/\partial\vartheta\). Differentiate term by term. The first term uses Theorem B.6 through the chain rule Equation B.15, \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\mathbf{V}\Bigr) =-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta\bigr). \] The second term uses the inverse-derivative rule Theorem B.5, which supplies \(\partial\mathbf{V}^{-1}/\partial\vartheta=-\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta\mathbf{V}^{-1}\), so \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\Bigr) =+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}. \] Adding the two pieces gives the score for the variance parameter, \[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\vartheta} =-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta\bigr) +\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}, \tag{B.16}\] and setting it to zero yields the estimating equation \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta) =\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_\vartheta\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\). The restricted (REML) log-likelihood adds a further term \(-\tfrac12\log\det(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X})\), whose derivative is again a trace of the same shape by Theorem B.6 and Theorem B.5; the mechanics are identical.

Reader translation. Equation Equation B.16 is the payoff of the appendix for meta-analysis: it is the exact gradient that a random-effects model solves to estimate heterogeneity. Every symbol in it came from an identity proved above, the log-determinant derivative and the inverse derivative, glued by the chain rule. In the next section we evaluate every piece of it in closed form for the compound-symmetry covariance, where \(\mathbf{V}_\vartheta=\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top\).

B.8 A worked example: the compound-symmetry covariance

We assemble the rank-one inverse, the matrix determinant lemma, and the log-determinant derivative on the covariance matrix that governs the balanced one-way random-effects model and the random-effects meta-analysis of Chapter 13. Let \[ \mathbf{V}=\sigma^2 I_n+\tau^2\,\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top, \] the compound-symmetry (equicorrelation) matrix, with \(\sigma^2>0\) and \(\tau^2\ge 0\). We compute its inverse, its determinant, and the derivative of \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) with respect to the between-study variance \(\tau^2\), all in closed form, and then verify the formulas numerically.

What each symbol means for a meta-analysis. This covariance is not an abstract choice; it is the exact marginal covariance of the estimates from \(n\) studies in a random-effects meta-analysis, so it is worth translating every symbol. Here \(n\) is the number of studies; \(\sigma^2\) is the within-study (residual, sampling) variance, assumed common across studies in this balanced illustration; and \(\tau^2\) is the between-study variance, the heterogeneity, the extra variability because the true effect differs from study to study. The vector \(\mathbf{1}_n=(1,\dots,1)^\top\) is the \(n\)-vector of ones, and the matrix \(\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top\) (a matrix of all ones) encodes a single shared random effect: it says every study’s estimate is shifted by the same common random deviation, so all pairs of studies share the \(\tau^2\) component of variance. That matrix has rank one, because it is the outer product of one vector with itself, and the rank-one structure is exactly what lets Sherman-Morrison and the matrix determinant lemma reduce an \(n\times n\) inverse and determinant to scalars. The reason we care about the derivative with respect to \(\tau^2\) is that estimating heterogeneity means solving the variance-component score Equation B.16, and that score is built from precisely \(\partial\log\det\mathbf{V}/\partial\tau^2\) and the inverse-quadratic derivative computed below.

Inverse. Apply Lemma B.5 with \(A=\sigma^2 I_n\), \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{1}_n\), and \(\mathbf{v}=\tau^2\mathbf{1}_n\), so that \(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top=\tau^2\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top\). Here \(A^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}I_n\) and \(\mathbf{v}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{u}=\tau^2\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{1}_n^\top\mathbf{1}_n=n\tau^2/\sigma^2\), so Equation B.11 gives \[ \mathbf{V}^{-1} =\frac{1}{\sigma^2}I_n-\frac{\tau^2/\sigma^4}{1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2}\,\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top =\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\Bigl(I_n-\frac{\tau^2/\sigma^2}{1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2}\,\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top\Bigr). \tag{B.17}\]

Determinant. The rank-one case of Corollary B.1 gives \[ \det\mathbf{V}=\det(\sigma^2 I_n)\,\bigl(1+\tau^2\,\mathbf{1}_n^\top(\sigma^2 I_n)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_n\bigr) =\sigma^{2n}\Bigl(1+\frac{n\tau^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr). \tag{B.18}\]

Derivative of the log-determinant. From Equation B.18, \(\log\det\mathbf{V}=n\log\sigma^2+\log\!\bigl(1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2\bigr)\), so directly \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau^2}\log\det\mathbf{V}=\frac{n/\sigma^2}{1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2}. \] The chain rule Equation B.15 with Theorem B.6 must agree. Since \(\partial\mathbf{V}/\partial\tau^2=\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top\), \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau^2}\log\det\mathbf{V} =\operatorname{tr}\!\Bigl(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial\tau^2}\Bigr) =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{1}_n^\top\bigr) =\mathbf{1}_n^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_n. \] Using Equation B.17 and \(\mathbf{1}_n^\top\mathbf{1}_n=n\), \[ \mathbf{1}_n^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_n =\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\Bigl(n-\frac{\tau^2/\sigma^2}{1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2}\,n^2\Bigr) =\frac{n}{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{1}{1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2} =\frac{n/\sigma^2}{1+n\tau^2/\sigma^2}, \] in agreement.

Numerical check. Take \(n=3\), \(\sigma^2=1\), \(\tau^2=2\), so \(\mathbf{V}=I_3+2\,\mathbf{1}_3\mathbf{1}_3^\top=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&2&2\\2&3&2\\2&2&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Formula Equation B.17 gives \(\mathbf{V}^{-1}=I_3-\tfrac{2}{7}\mathbf{1}_3\mathbf{1}_3^\top\), that is \[ \mathbf{V}^{-1}=\frac{1}{7}\begin{pmatrix}5&-2&-2\\-2&5&-2\\-2&-2&5\end{pmatrix}. \] Direct multiplication confirms it: the first row of \(\mathbf{V}\) dotted with the first column of \(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\) is \((3\cdot5+2\cdot(-2)+2\cdot(-2))/7=(15-4-4)/7=1\), and with the second column is \((3\cdot(-2)+2\cdot5+2\cdot(-2))/7=(-6+10-4)/7=0\). Formula Equation B.18 gives \(\det\mathbf{V}=1^3(1+3\cdot2)=7\), matching the eigenvalue count: \(\mathbf{1}_3\mathbf{1}_3^\top\) has eigenvalues \(3,0,0\), so \(\mathbf{V}=I_3+2\,\mathbf{1}_3\mathbf{1}_3^\top\) has eigenvalues \(7,1,1\) with product \(7\). Finally \(\partial\log\det\mathbf{V}/\partial\tau^2=\frac{3}{1+6}=\frac{3}{7}\), which equals \(\mathbf{1}_3^\top\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_3=\) (sum of all entries of \(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\)) \(=(3\cdot5+6\cdot(-2))/7=(15-12)/7=\frac{3}{7}\). All three routes agree.

This is precisely the arithmetic that makes the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimating equations for \(\tau^2\) tractable in closed form for balanced designs, as used in Chapters 8 and 13: the Woodbury inverse and the matrix determinant lemma reduce an \(n\times n\) problem to scalars, and the log-determinant and inverse derivatives produce the score.

B.9 A second worked example: the score of a logistic log-likelihood

The previous example served the variance-component side of the book. This one serves the side the target reader most often needs: the likelihood derivatives behind logistic regression, which underpin the binary-outcome likelihood of ML-NMR (Chapters 20 to 25) and the outcome model of an anchored indirect comparison. The phrase “the MAIC, STC, and ML-NMR derivations just follow from differentiating a likelihood” means, concretely, the computation below.

Let \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) be binary outcomes for \(i=1,\dots,n\), with covariate rows \(\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^p\) stacked into the design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) (rows \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\)). Logistic regression sets the linear predictor \(\eta_i=\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) and the success probability \(p_i=\operatorname{expit}(\eta_i)=1/(1+e^{-\eta_i})\). The log-likelihood is \[ \ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\bigr] =\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[y_i\,\eta_i-\log(1+e^{\eta_i})\bigr]. \]

Score (gradient). Differentiate term by term in \(\boldsymbol\beta\). By the linear-form derivative Theorem B.1, \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}\eta_i=\mathbf{x}_i\); and by the chain rule (Theorem B.8), \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}\log(1+e^{\eta_i}) =\dfrac{e^{\eta_i}}{1+e^{\eta_i}}\,\mathbf{x}_i=p_i\,\mathbf{x}_i\). Hence \[ \mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol\beta)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ell =\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i=\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}), \qquad \mathbf{p}=(p_1,\dots,p_n)^\top. \tag{B.19}\] The score is the design matrix transpose times the residual vector \(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}\) (shape check: \(\mathbf{X}^\top\) is \(p\times n\), \(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}\) is \(n\times 1\), so the score is \(p\times 1\), matching \(\boldsymbol\beta\)).

Hessian (curvature) and Fisher information. The score is a sum of \(-p_i\mathbf{x}_i\) plus constants; using \(\partial p_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta^\top=p_i(1-p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i^\top\) (chain rule again, since \(\operatorname{expit}'=\operatorname{expit}(1-\operatorname{expit})\)), \[ \nabla^2_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ell =-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i(1-p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top =-\mathbf{X}^\top W\mathbf{X}, \qquad W=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(p_i(1-p_i)\bigr). \tag{B.20}\] Because \(W\succeq 0\), the Hessian is negative semidefinite, so \(\ell\) is concave and its stationary point is the maximum likelihood estimator. The Fisher information is the negative Hessian, \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf{X}^\top W\mathbf{X}\), exactly the matrix inverted at each iteration of iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) in the GLM chapter; the weights \(W\) are why it is reweighted.

Numerical instance. Take \(n=2\), \(p=2\), design rows \(\mathbf{x}_1=(1,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{x}_2=(1,1)^\top\) (an intercept and one covariate), outcomes \(\mathbf{y}=(1,0)^\top\), evaluated at \(\boldsymbol\beta=(0,0)^\top\). Then \(\eta_1=\eta_2=0\) and \(p_1=p_2=\tfrac12\), so the residual is \(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)^\top\) and Equation B.19 gives \[ \mathbf{s}=\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}) =\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12\\ -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0\\ -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}. \] The weight matrix is \(W=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac14,\tfrac14)\), so Equation B.20 gives the Fisher information \[ \mathcal{I}=\mathbf{X}^\top W\mathbf{X} =\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac14&0\\ 0&\tfrac14\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&\tfrac14\\ \tfrac14&\tfrac14\end{pmatrix}, \] and the Hessian is \(-\mathcal{I}\). One Newton (Fisher scoring) step from \(\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf{0}\) is \(\boldsymbol\beta\mapsto\boldsymbol\beta+\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\), the same update that drives the maximum likelihood fits in the GLM, MAIC, and ML-NMR chapters. This is the derivative computation the target reader came to this appendix for; it uses only Theorem B.1 and Theorem B.8.

B.11 Notes and references

The conventions and identities of this appendix are standard matrix calculus. The differential method, which we adopted as the organizing principle, isolates the derivative as the unique matrix appearing in the canonical form \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top\,dX)\) (Lemma B.1); it is the systematic development of Magnus and Neudecker, Matrix Differential Calculus with Applications in Statistics and Econometrics, and the same identities are cataloged, without proof, in Petersen and Pedersen, The Matrix Cookbook, and in Harville, Matrix Algebra from a Statistician’s Perspective. The linear-algebra prerequisites we invoked, namely the determinant, the cofactor expansion, the adjugate and Cramer’s rule, the multiplicativity of the determinant, the block-triangular determinant, and the characterization of invertibility, are developed in Strang (2016) and, in this book, in Theorem 1.6, Definition 3.6, and Definition 3.7.

The Sherman-Morrison formula (Lemma B.5) and its generalization (Theorem B.7) are named for Sherman and Morrison (1950) and for Woodbury (1950); the matrix determinant lemma (Corollary B.1) is sometimes attributed to Sylvester. The proofs given here are by direct verification, which is the most transparent route and is self-checking. The compound-symmetry worked example of Section B.8 is the elementary case underlying the closed-form restricted maximum likelihood estimators of the balanced one-way random-effects model; the general restricted likelihood, built from \(\log\det\mathbf{V}\) and \(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\), appears in Dias et al. (2018) and is differentiated using exactly Theorem B.6 and Theorem B.5.

These identities have no separate counterpart in the companion ITC_Coq development, whose formalized results sit at the statistical and causal layer rather than at the level of matrix differentiation; accordingly, none carries a machine-checked tag. The downstream uses are pervasive: the gradient and Hessian of the least-squares objective (Theorem B.2) drive the normal equations and the Gauss-Markov theory of the regression chapter; the Jacobian and the chain rule (Theorem B.8) are the analytic content of the delta method (Theorem 5.3) and of the variance propagation in the population-adjustment chapters; and the trace, inverse, and log-determinant derivatives are the engine of the restricted-likelihood score equations in the hierarchical and meta-analytic models.
