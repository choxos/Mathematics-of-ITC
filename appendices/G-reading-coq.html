Appendix G — Reading the Coq Proofs

Chapter 30 (Section 30.1) tells the story of the companion ITC_Coq development: what a proof assistant guarantees, the proof-status vocabulary (Definition 30.1), the three active axioms (Definition 30.2), and the map from the book’s tagged theorems to the Coq lemmas that machine-check them (Section 30.10). This appendix is the operational counterpart: a hands-on guide for a reader who wants to obtain the development, install the toolchain, compile every file, read the proof scripts, and query the kernel for the axioms a theorem uses. Nothing here re-argues the mathematics; the chapters do that. What follows is precise enough to be typed at a shell and a Coq prompt. Symbols follow Notation; in particular the abstract expectation operator written E in the Coq source plays the role of \(\mathbb{E}\).

The development is fifteen Coq source files in a directory theories/, built against the Mathematical Components and mathcomp-analysis libraries, compiling with zero project axioms and zero admitted proofs. It is released under the GPL-3 license and lives in the repository https://github.com/choxos/ITC_Coq, whose theories/AXIOMS.md is the canonical assumption inventory and whose manuscript/16_coq.md is the file-by-file walk-through on which Chapter 30 is based.

G.1 Why read the Coq development, and what success looks like

If you run meta-analyses in R or Stan, a fair first question is why you would spend an afternoon installing a proof assistant to read files you will never execute in an analysis. The payoff is specific to indirect comparison. Every method in this book rests on assumptions that are easy to state in prose and easy to lose in prose: constancy of relative effects for a Bucher comparison (Definition 14.3), positivity and conditional exchangeability for weighting (Definition 9.8, Definition 9.7), the absence of effect modification on the scale that would otherwise bias an unanchored comparison (Definition 11.3). In a machine-checked proof these assumptions cannot be smuggled into a sentence: each one appears as a named Hypothesis that the final theorem carries in its statement, and the proof will not close if the algebra leans on an assumption that was never declared. Three concrete guarantees follow.

  • Assumptions are auditable. You can read exactly which premises a result needs, in one place, and you can query the proof mechanically for the foundational axioms it rests on (Section G.11).
  • The algebra cannot be fudged. The subtraction that cancels the common comparator in Bucher (Theorem 14.1), the exponential tilt defining the MAIC weights (Theorem 18.1), and the effective-sample-size bound (Theorem 18.6) are each checked symbol by symbol by a small trusted program, so a sign error or a dropped term cannot slip through.
  • The certificate is reproducible. Anyone can rebuild the development and re-verify every tagged theorem on their own machine; the check is a command you run, not a claim you accept.

What the machine does not do matters just as much, and a health researcher should hold onto it: Coq verifies that the conclusions follow from the stated premises by valid logic and algebra. It does not verify that those premises hold in your trials. Whether relative effects really are constant across the \(AC\) and \(BC\) populations, whether positivity holds in your data, whether you have measured the effect modifiers: those are scientific questions the machine leaves to you. Machine-checking moves the argument from “trust the author’s algebra and the author’s assumption bookkeeping” to “trust the author’s science”; it does not remove the science. For a health technology assessment that shift is worth having, because a reviewer can see the exact assumption ledger behind a comparative-effect estimate.

Reader goal. You do not need to become a Coq developer, and this appendix does not try to teach you to write proofs. A realistic goal is narrower: install the toolchain, rebuild the development so you have watched the kernel accept every theorem, open one file, read the statement of a theorem the book tags as machine-checked, identify the assumptions written into that statement, and run one command that reports the foundational axioms the proof uses. If at the end you can do that for bucher_unbiased, you can do it for any tagged theorem in the book, and you have gained the ability to audit a machine-checked claim rather than take it on trust.

G.2 The minimal path

Before the step-by-step detail, here is the whole journey in a handful of commands, so you can see its shape. The sections that follow expand each step, and Section G.6 says what to do when one fails. First, at an ordinary shell:

git clone https://github.com/choxos/ITC_Coq.git       # 1. get the source
cd ITC_Coq
opam install 'coq>=9.1' 'coq-mathcomp-analysis>=1.9'   # 2. install the toolchain (full list below)
make                                                   # 3. compile and kernel-check every file

Then, at a Coq prompt started with the project’s load path (coqtop -R theories ITC, explained in Section G.11):

Require Import ITC.Bucher.          (* 4. load the compiled Bucher module *)
Check bucher_unbiased.              (* 5. print the theorem's statement, assumptions included *)
Print Assumptions bucher_unbiased.  (* 6. report the foundational axioms the proof uses *)

What success looks like. Step 3 ends without an error and leaves fifteen .vo files in theories/, one per source file; that clean exit is itself the verification that the kernel accepted every theorem (Section G.5). Step 5 prints the premises and the conclusion, so you can read the theorem’s assumptions (Section G.10). Step 6 prints exactly three axioms and nothing else (Section G.11). The companion “nothing was left unproved” check is one more shell command, grep -nE "Admitted|^Axiom|admit\." theories/*.v, which should print nothing at all. If every one of these signals appears, you have reproduced the book’s central machine-checked claim yourself; the rest of this appendix explains what each step means and how to read what it prints.

G.3 Getting the development

Clone the repository and enter it:

git clone https://github.com/choxos/ITC_Coq.git
cd ITC_Coq

The pieces relevant to this appendix are the build files _CoqProject and Makefile at the root, the fifteen .v source files in theories/, and theories/AXIOMS.md. The sibling directories proofs/ (the Markdown proof catalogue, Parts A through O) and manuscript/ (a self-contained pedagogical text) are reference prose and are not part of the Coq build.

G.4 Installing the toolchain

The development is built and checked by Coq, the proof assistant renamed the Rocq Prover in 2025, together with three libraries: Mathematical Components (mathcomp, the SSReflect proof language plus the algebra hierarchy), mathcomp-analysis (classical real analysis, measure theory, Lebesgue integration, probability measures, and transition kernels), and Hierarchy Builder (the tool that generates mathcomp’s structure hierarchy). The required versions, as declared in _CoqProject and the project README, are collected in Table G.1.

These libraries form a stack, each layer resting on the one below. mathcomp-ssreflect is the base: the SSReflect proof language and the library of finite objects. mathcomp-algebra adds the algebraic hierarchy on top of it, and mathcomp-analysis adds real analysis, measure theory, and probability on top of that. Hierarchy Builder is not a mathematical layer but the tool that generates the hierarchy itself, the layered tower of algebraic structures (from monoid up to ring up to field) that lets a theorem proved for rings apply automatically to the real numbers. For reading Bucher.v you need only the base and the algebra layer; the analysis layer matters for the measure-theoretic files.

The supported way to obtain these is opam, the OCaml package manager. Install opam from your operating system’s package manager, then create an isolated switch (an opam switch is a self-contained OCaml compiler together with its own installed packages, analogous to a Python virtual environment or a separate R library tree, but covering the whole toolchain rather than one language’s add-on packages), add the Coq released-packages repository, and install the libraries; opam resolves the prover and the remaining mathcomp packages as dependencies:

opam init --bare                                   # first-time opam setup
opam switch create itc-coq 4.14.2                  # a fresh switch with an OCaml compiler
eval $(opam env)                                   # point the shell at the new switch

opam repo add coq-released https://coq.inria.fr/opam/released

opam install 'coq>=9.1' 'coq-mathcomp-ssreflect>=2.0' coq-mathcomp-algebra \
             'coq-mathcomp-analysis>=1.9' 'coq-hierarchy-builder>=1.6'

opam will select a mutually compatible set of versions satisfying the constraints; installing coq-mathcomp-analysis alone would pull in coq-mathcomp-ssreflect, coq-mathcomp-algebra, and coq-hierarchy-builder transitively, but listing them documents the intent and lets opam report a clear error if any constraint cannot be met.

One practical point the commands above hide: eval $(opam env) updates only the current shell so that coqc, make, and the libraries resolve to the new switch. It is not permanent. Open a fresh terminal, or reboot, and you must run eval $(opam env) again before building, or the shell will not find the switch’s tools (Section G.6). Some opam installations add a startup hook that does this for you; when in doubt, run it.

Remark. The Coq to Rocq rename. At version 9.x the prover’s opam package may appear as rocq-prover, with coq retained as a transitional alias, and the libraries may also be published under rocq-mathcomp-*. If the coq package is unavailable on your opam repository snapshot, substitute rocq-prover for coq in the install command; the source files and build are unaffected, since they refer to the libraries by their logical module names, not their opam package names.

In practice, try the coq-named commands first; if your shell reports that coqc or coqtop is not found, the Rocq 9 equivalents are rocq (for example rocq --version in place of coqc --version) and rocqtop in place of coqtop. The two naming families drive the same prover, read the same source files, and take the same -R theories ITC load-path flag, so anywhere this appendix writes coqc or coqtop you may read rocq or rocqtop with no other change. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Verify the installation before building:

coqc --version                 # or: rocq --version
opam list | grep mathcomp      # should list ssreflect, algebra, analysis

To read and step through the proofs interactively, any of the standard front ends works: VsCoq (the Visual Studio Code extension), Proof General (the Emacs mode), or the bundled graphical interface CoqIDE, called RocqIDE from version 9. Each lets you place the cursor mid-proof and inspect the goal and context after every tactic, which is the natural way to read a Coq proof for the first time.

G.5 The _CoqProject file and the build

_CoqProject is the single source of build configuration. It declares one logical mapping and lists the source files in dependency order:

-R theories ITC

theories/Axioms.v
theories/BasicTypes.v
...
theories/EffectModifiers.v

The line -R theories ITC maps the physical directory theories/ to the logical module prefix ITC (recursively); a logical name here is simply the name Coq uses to locate a compiled module, independent of where the file sits on disk, so that the module compiled from theories/Bucher.v is addressed as ITC.Bucher, theories/MAIC.v as ITC.MAIC, and so on. This is the path you will use in Require Import (Section G.11).

Building is driven by make from the repository root:

make

The top-level Makefile is a thin wrapper: if Makefile.coq is missing or out of date it regenerates it from _CoqProject with coq_makefile, namely

coq_makefile -f _CoqProject -o Makefile.coq        # rocq makefile -f ... on Rocq 9

and then delegates the actual compilation to that generated makefile. To compile a file here means more than to translate it. coqc runs the file’s proof scripts to build a proof term for each theorem and submits every one to the kernel, Coq’s small trusted checking core (the kernel is explained in Section G.8). The resulting theories/X.vo is the compiled, kernel-checked file: an artifact that exists only because the kernel accepted every proof in X.v. So a run that ends without error, producing fifteen .vo files, is not merely a successful build; it is the verification that every theorem in the tree is accepted by the kernel. A clean rebuild is

make clean && make

which deletes the .vo files and the generated Makefile.coq and recompiles from scratch.

The inventory claims of Chapter 30 are reproducible at the shell. That the development declares no axioms of its own and admits no proof is confirmed by a search that returns nothing across the fifteen files:

grep -nE "Admitted|^Axiom|admit\." theories/*.v        # no output

Two flaws would undermine the claim that the tree is fully proved, and this one command looks for both. An Admitted proof is a hole the kernel is told to accept with no proof term, so the theorem above it is not actually checked; an Axiom (or an admit. inside a proof) is an extra unproved assumption that every result downstream silently inherits. Fewer of both means more of the mathematics is genuinely verified, which is why “zero admits, zero project axioms” is a guarantee, not a boast. The grep prints one line per match; finding none, it prints nothing and returns you to the prompt. Had it printed a line, that line would name the file and the offending Admitted or Axiom, and the tree would not be fully proved. The search deliberately scans only the project’s own theories/*.v: the three classical axioms the proofs rely on live in the mathcomp-analysis library, not in these files, so they never appear here. That is exactly why the complementary per-theorem check below, Print Assumptions, exists; it reports the inherited library axioms that grep cannot see.

Per-theorem axiom closure is queried with Print Assumptions, described next.

G.6 Troubleshooting the build

When the minimal path of Section G.2 stops at an error, the cause is almost always one of a short list. The fixes are in plain terms.

  • opam install cannot find a compatible set of versions. Refresh opam’s index with opam update, confirm the released-packages repository is present (opam repo list should show coq-released), and re-run the install. On an older switch, opam upgrade may be needed first.
  • coqc --version reports a version below 9.1, or an old mathcomp. The shell is using a system-wide Coq rather than the switch you built. Run eval $(opam env) in that terminal and check again; opam switch list shows which switch is active.
  • coqc: command not found. Same cause: the switch is not active in this shell. Run eval $(opam env); if you have only just installed opam, open a new terminal so its startup hook runs.
  • make reports coq_makefile: command not found. Coq itself is not on the path, so the switch is not active, or the libraries installed but the prover did not. Activate the switch and confirm coqc --version works before running make. On Rocq 9 the tool is rocq makefile, which make invokes automatically once the switch is active.
  • Require Import ITC.Bucher fails with the module not found. The load path is missing. Interactive sessions and scratch files need the -R theories ITC flag (coqtop -R theories ITC), the same mapping that turns theories/Bucher.v into the name ITC.Bucher (Section G.5). Inside the build, make supplies it from _CoqProject; outside the build you must pass it yourself.
  • A scratch file breaks make. If you place a scratch .v file inside theories/ or list it in _CoqProject, make will try to compile it. Keep scratch files at the repository root and out of _CoqProject, and run them directly with coqc -R theories ITC scratch.v (Section G.11).
  • You are on Windows without a Unix-like shell. opam and the sh/make workflow assume a Unix-like environment. The Windows Subsystem for Linux (WSL) provides one: install opam inside WSL and follow the instructions unchanged.

G.7 Dependency order of the theories

The fifteen files are grouped into five phases in _CoqProject, each phase building only on the ones before it. The grouping is pedagogical; the binding constraint is the set of Require Import lines at the top of each file, which the build resolves into a topological order.

  • Phase 1, foundations: Axioms.v, BasicTypes.v, PotentialOutcomes.v, CausalAssumptions.v.
  • Phase 2, conditional independence and balancing scores: ConditionalIndep.v, BalancingScore.v, PropensityScore.v.
  • Phase 3, estimators: IPWEstimator.v, OutcomeRegression.v, DoublyRobust.v.
  • Phase 4, indirect-comparison methods: MAIC.v, STC.v, Comparison.v.
  • Phase 5, indirect-comparison algebra: Bucher.v, EffectModifiers.v.

The actual import edges are tighter than the linear phase order suggests. Axioms.v imports only the mathcomp libraries; BasicTypes.v imports Axioms; PotentialOutcomes imports Axioms BasicTypes; CausalAssumptions adds PotentialOutcomes; ConditionalIndep adds CausalAssumptions. The balancing and estimator layers chain on top: PropensityScore imports through ConditionalIndep and BalancingScore; IPWEstimator, OutcomeRegression, and DoublyRobust import PropensityScore (and, for DoublyRobust, the two estimator files); Comparison imports all of MAIC, STC, and the estimator layer.

Table G.2 gives the literal dependency: the modules named on each file’s Require Import line(s) at its head, read from the source. This is the ground truth the paraphrase above summarizes; a reader tracing what a file needs should trust this table, since “imports” and “adds” in the prose are shorthand for these exact lines.

Two files in the last phase are deliberately lightweight: Bucher.v and EffectModifiers.v import only Axioms and BasicTypes. They sit at the end of _CoqProject for narrative reasons (they are the headline indirect-comparison results), but they depend on nothing from Phases 2 through 4 and could be compiled immediately after BasicTypes.v. A reader new to the tree can therefore start at Bucher.v without having read the kernel-level conditional-independence machinery, which is the technical heart of the development but is not needed for the indirect-comparison algebra. This is why the worked example below uses Bucher.v.

G.8 What a proof assistant is: terms, types, goals, and the kernel

The sections above were about setup; from here on we read Coq. This short primer supplies the ideas that make a .v file legible. Chapter 30 gives the conceptual account in full (Section 30.1); the point here is only to install enough vocabulary that the annotated Bucher example reads smoothly.

Terms, types, and propositions as types. Coq is a typed language: every expression, called a term, has a type. Familiar types are nat (the natural numbers) and bool (the two-element type of the values true and false). The idea that turns this into a proof assistant is that a proposition is also a type, and a proof of that proposition is a term of that type. To prove the proposition \(P\) is therefore the very same task as to construct a term whose type is \(P\). This is why “Coq checks types” and “Coq checks proofs” are one sentence: when the system confirms that a term has type \(P\), it has confirmed that the term is a valid proof of \(P\). The type whose members are propositions is called Prop; so the line Definition transitivity : Prop := d_AC = d_AB + d_BC reads “transitivity is a proposition, namely the equation \(d_{AC} = d_{AB} + d_{BC}\).”

The kernel. Coq splits into a large convenient outer layer (the tactic language, the libraries, the user interface) and a small kernel: a compact, heavily scrutinized program whose only job is to check that a proof term really has the type it claims. Everything outside the kernel is untrusted machinery that produces candidate proof terms; the kernel is the trusted core that checks them. “Machine-checked” means checked by this kernel. The practical consequence is reassuring: you need not trust the tactics, the libraries, or the author, only the kernel, because whatever route produced a proof term, the kernel re-checks the finished term from scratch.

Proof script versus proof term. You almost never write a proof term by hand. Instead you write a proof script: a recipe, between the keywords Proof. and Qed., of instructions that build the term for you. Each instruction is a tactic. When the script finishes, Qed. hands the constructed term to the kernel; if the kernel accepts it, the theorem is recorded. The distinction matters when reading: the statement of a theorem is what is proved, the script is only how, so a beginner should read the statement first and treat the script as secondary.

Goal and context: the state a tactic transforms. While a proof script runs, Coq maintains a proof state with two parts. The context is the list of things currently assumed or already established: the variables in scope and the named hypotheses, each with a name and a type. The goal is the single proposition you are trying to prove right now. A front end displays them stacked, context above a line, goal below:

  d_AB, d_AC, d_BC : R          <- context: three real numbers in scope
  H : d_AC = d_AB + d_BC        <- context: a named hypothesis
  ============================
  d_AB = d_AC - d_BC            <- goal: what remains to prove

A tactic is an instruction that transforms this state: it may move something from the goal into the context, rewrite the goal with an equation, or close the goal outright. Reading a proof interactively means placing the cursor after each tactic and watching the context and goal change; this appendix reproduces those snapshots in the Bucher example (Section G.10) so you can follow without a running Coq.

Two languages in one file: Gallina and the tactic language. A .v file is written in two languages, and telling them apart removes most early confusion. Definitions and statements are written in Gallina, Coq’s term language: Definition ... := ..., Lemma name : <proposition>, and the propositions themselves (\(d_{AC} = d_{AB} + d_{BC}\), forall x, ...). Proof scripts are written in a tactic language: Coq’s built-in one is called Ltac, and this development uses SSReflect (short for small-scale reflection), the tactic dialect that ships with Mathematical Components and favors very short, composable steps. So in the Bucher lemma below, the line transitivity -> d_AB = d_AC - d_BC is Gallina (the claim), while rewrite /transitivity => H is SSReflect (a step of the proof). When you find yourself trying to read an equation as if it were an instruction, or the reverse, this is usually the confusion to check first. Table G.3 collects the vocabulary; the mathcomp-specific notation is in Table G.5.

A hello-world proof. Before the real material, here is a complete four-line proof over Coq’s built-in natural numbers, small enough to watch every state change. It uses the same three tactics that carry the Bucher proof: move=> (move a hypothesis from the goal into the context), rewrite (rewrite the goal with an equation), and by [] (finish a goal that now holds by computation).

Lemma warmup (n : nat) : n = 2 -> n + 1 = 3.
Proof.
  move=> H.
  rewrite H.
  by [].
Qed.

The state evolves as follows. After Proof. the context holds the number n and the goal is the whole implication:

  n : nat
  ============================
  n = 2 -> n + 1 = 3

move=> H moves the antecedent n = 2 out of the goal and into the context under the name H, leaving the consequent as the goal:

  n : nat
  H : n = 2
  ============================
  n + 1 = 3

rewrite H replaces n by 2 in the goal, using H left to right:

  n : nat
  H : n = 2
  ============================
  2 + 1 = 3

by [] observes that 2 + 1 computes to 3, so the goal 3 = 3 holds, and closes it; Qed. then submits the finished term to the kernel. With this picture of state, goal, context, and the three tactics, the Bucher example uses nothing new in kind, only the same moves over real numbers instead of natural numbers.

G.9 How to read a Coq definition, lemma, and theorem

The development is written in the SSReflect proof language, the dialect of Coq tactics used by Mathematical Components. Three syntactic forms carry the content: Definition (an object or a proposition), Lemma/Theorem/Corollary (a claim together with a proof), and the Section mechanism that scopes shared parameters.

A Section opens with Section Name. and closes with End Name.. Inside it, Variable (x : T) introduces a parameter every later result may use, Hypothesis H : P introduces an assumed proposition, and Context introduces several at once. When the section closes, each parameter and hypothesis that a definition or lemma actually used is added to that object’s statement as an explicit argument or premise. This is precisely the device that makes the development’s modeling assumptions visible: a probabilistic fact such as linearity of expectation enters as a Hypothesis and reappears in the final theorem’s statement, never as a hidden global axiom (Section 30.9).

To see the discharge concretely, take the opening section of Bucher.v (shown with two of its results). Inside the section, d_AB, d_AC, d_BC are section variables:

Section RelativeEffects.
Variable (R : realType).
Variable (d_AB d_AC d_BC : R).

Definition transitivity : Prop := d_AC = d_AB + d_BC.

Lemma transitivity_subtract :
  transitivity -> d_AB = d_AC - d_BC.
Proof. rewrite /transitivity => H. by rewrite H GRing.addrK. Qed.

End RelativeEffects.

Inside the section, transitivity_subtract appears to mention no d’s in its binders; they are supplied by the section. The moment End RelativeEffects. closes the section, Coq discharges the used variables, turning each into an explicit argument of every result that used it. The statement of transitivity_subtract becomes

forall (R : realType) (d_AB d_AC d_BC : R),
  transitivity d_AB d_AC d_BC -> d_AB = d_AC - d_BC

and transitivity itself becomes a function forall (R : realType), R -> R -> R -> Prop. The three population effects, invisible in the body, are now visible arguments. (With Set Implicit Arguments, as in the actual file, Coq may hide the arguments it can infer when it prints these; the underlying statements are as shown.) This is the mechanism referenced above: any assumption you place in a section as a Variable or Hypothesis cannot vanish, it resurfaces in the discharged statement, which is why reading a theorem’s final statement tells you its full set of premises (Section 30.9).

A Definition binds a name to a term or a proposition, optionally with arguments:

Definition bucher_estimate : R := d_AC_hat - d_BC_hat.
Definition transitivity : Prop := d_AC = d_AB + d_BC.

A Lemma or Theorem states a type (the proposition) and is followed by Proof., a sequence of tactics, and Qed.. The keyword chosen (Lemma, Theorem, Corollary, Proposition) carries no logical difference; it is documentation. Qed. submits the constructed proof term to the kernel and records the result; a proof that does not fully close the goal cannot reach Qed..

The tactics that do the work are listed in Table G.4, and the notation and scopes needed to read the statements are in Table G.5. Both are reference cards, not reading assignments. You met three of these tactics in action in the warm-up of Section G.8, and the Bucher example needs only five tactic families in all, flagged in the third column of Table G.4: on a first pass read only those rows (move=>, rewrite, have, apply:, by) and leave the rest for the larger files. Three small notational habits recur and are worth naming once: the => in move=> H reads “and then introduce the name H”; the leading - in rewrite -E is a direction flag meaning “rewrite right to left,” not a subtraction; and by t is a discipline device that runs t and fails unless the goal is completely closed, so writing by asserts “this step finishes the goal.” With these habits and the two tables, the annotated example of Section G.10 can be read line by line.

G.10 A fully annotated example: Bucher’s theorem

We read theories/Bucher.v from its definitions to its headline theorem, the unbiasedness of the Bucher estimator, which is Theorem 14.1 in Chapter 14. The file imports only Axioms and BasicTypes and opens ring_scope, so every symbol below is in Table G.4 or Table G.5.

Before the code, fix the clinical picture, because the Coq symbols name exactly its pieces. Two trials share a common comparator \(C\): an \(AC\) trial compares treatment \(A\) against \(C\), and a \(BC\) trial compares \(B\) against \(C\). No trial compared \(A\) against \(B\) directly, yet \(A\) versus \(B\) is the target of the health technology assessment. Bucher’s indirect comparison estimates that target by subtracting the two observed contrasts, which is valid when the relative effects are constant across the two trial populations (transitivity). With the picture fixed, we read the file from its definitions to its headline theorem.

Example G.1 (The assumption as a definition) The constancy-of-relative-effects assumption (Definition 14.2) is encoded as a named proposition, the formal counterpart of Definition 14.3:

Definition transitivity : Prop := d_AC = d_AB + d_BC.

Here d_AB, d_AC, d_BC : R are section variables standing for the three population relative effects. The definition names the equation \(d_{AC} = d_{AB} + d_{BC}\); nothing is asserted, the name simply abbreviates the proposition so later results can take it as a hypothesis. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example G.2 (A lemma and its proof) The equivalent subtractive form (Proposition 14.2) is a one-line lemma:

Lemma transitivity_subtract :
  transitivity -> d_AB = d_AC - d_BC.
Proof.
  rewrite /transitivity => H.
  by rewrite H GRing.addrK.
Qed.

The statement reads: if transitivity holds, then \(d_{AB} = d_{AC} - d_{BC}\). The proof has two steps. rewrite /transitivity unfolds the definition, so the antecedent to be introduced becomes the explicit equation \(d_{AC} = d_{AB} + d_{BC}\), and => H introduces it into the context under the name H. Then by rewrite H GRing.addrK rewrites the goal’s \(d_{AC}\) to \(d_{AB} + d_{BC}\) using H, after which GRing.addrK (the cancellation law \(x + y - y = x\)) collapses \((d_{AB} + d_{BC}) - d_{BC}\) to \(d_{AB}\); by certifies that the goal is now closed.

Watching the state makes this concrete. The proof opens with the implication as the goal:

  R : realType
  d_AB, d_AC, d_BC : R
  ============================
  transitivity -> d_AB = d_AC - d_BC

rewrite /transitivity unfolds the name, and => H moves the (now explicit) antecedent into the context:

  H : d_AC = d_AB + d_BC
  ============================
  d_AB = d_AC - d_BC

rewrite H replaces d_AC by d_AB + d_BC in the goal, and GRing.addrK cancels the trailing - d_BC, reducing the goal to d_AB = d_AB, which by closes:

  after rewrite H:      d_AB = d_AB + d_BC - d_BC
  after GRing.addrK:    d_AB = d_AB            (closed by reflexivity)

\(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example G.3 (The abstract expectation operator) Unbiasedness is stated over an abstract expectation. The trial estimators are random variables on a common probability space, and the expectation is a section variable whose only assumed property is linearity, supplied as a hypothesis:

Variable (E : (Omega -> R) -> R).

Hypothesis E_linearity :
  forall (X Y : Omega -> R) (a b : R),
  E (fun omega => a * X omega + b * Y omega) = a * E X + b * E Y.

Read the type slowly. A value of type Omega -> R is a function from the sample space Omega to the reals, which is exactly a random variable: it assigns a number to each possible world omega. So E : (Omega -> R) -> R is a function that eats a random variable and returns a real number, the role of the expectation \(\mathbb{E}\) in the Notation. The hypothesis’s opening forall (X Y : Omega -> R) (a b : R), ... reads “for all random variables X, Y and all reals a, b,” and its body is the linearity identity \(\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\). E_linearity is the formal counterpart of linearity of expectation (A.4 in the proof catalogue); making it a Hypothesis rather than building a specific integral is the “abstract-operator” pattern (Section 30.8) that lets the algebra of estimands be machine-checked without committing to an integration theory. This is not merely a convenience for the formalizer: in population adjustment we manipulate expectations and conditional expectations constantly without ever specifying the underlying measure, and the unbiasedness proofs for Bucher, MAIC, and STC all turn on linearity alone. Assuming exactly linearity, and nothing more, is therefore faithful to how these arguments actually run on paper. From it the file derives the difference rule:

Lemma E_sub (X Y : Omega -> R) :
  E (fun omega => X omega - Y omega) = E X - E Y.
Proof.
  have -> : (fun omega => X omega - Y omega)
         = (fun omega => 1 * X omega + (-1) * Y omega).
    apply: fun_ext => omega.
    by rewrite GRing.mul1r GRing.mulN1r.
  rewrite E_linearity GRing.mul1r GRing.mulN1r.
  by [].
Qed.

This proof introduces two constructs worth slowing down on. have -> : A = B does two things at once: it opens a subgoal to prove the equation A = B, and once that subgoal is closed it immediately rewrites the main goal with it. Here A is the integrand fun omega => X omega - Y omega and B is fun omega => 1 * X omega + (-1) * Y omega, so the first task is to prove these two functions equal:

  ============================
  (fun omega => X omega - Y omega) = (fun omega => 1 * X omega + (-1) * Y omega)

Two functions are equal when they agree on every input; that principle is functional extensionality, and in this development it is the lemma fun_ext from Axioms.v. apply: fun_ext reduces the function-equality subgoal to a pointwise one, and => omega names the input, leaving an ordinary ring equation:

  omega : Omega
  ============================
  X omega - Y omega = 1 * X omega + (-1) * Y omega

The ring rewrites \(1\cdot x = x\) (GRing.mul1r) and \((-1)\cdot x = -x\) (GRing.mulN1r) close it. Back in the main goal, the integrand is now the linear combination, so rewrite E_linearity applies linearity with \(a = 1\), \(b = -1\); the same two ring rewrites simplify the coefficients, leaving \(E\,X - E\,Y\), and by [] closes. The lemma is exactly linearity of expectation specialized to a difference, and it is the only fact about E that Bucher’s theorem will need. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example G.4 (The theorem) The headline result, the formal Theorem 14.1, is now three lines:

Theorem bucher_unbiased (d_AB d_AC d_BC : R) :
  E d_AC_hat = d_AC ->
  E d_BC_hat = d_BC ->
  d_AB = d_AC - d_BC ->
  E (fun omega => d_AC_hat omega - d_BC_hat omega) = d_AB.
Proof.
  move=> H_AC H_BC H_trans.
  rewrite E_sub H_AC H_BC.
  by rewrite H_trans.
Qed.

Read the statement top to bottom. The three premises are: E d_AC_hat = d_AC, that the \(AC\) trial estimator is unbiased for \(d_{AC}\); E d_BC_hat = d_BC, likewise for the \(BC\) trial (these two are the formal content of Proposition 14.1); and d_AB = d_AC - d_BC, the constancy assumption in subtractive form (Example G.1). The conclusion is that the expectation of the difference of the two estimators equals the target \(d_{AB}\), that is, the Bucher estimator is unbiased.

The proof introduces the three premises as H_AC, H_BC, H_trans with move=>. Then rewrite E_sub turns the goal’s left side \(E[d_{AC}\text{-hat} - d_{BC}\text{-hat}]\) into \(E\,d_{AC}\text{-hat} - E\,d_{BC}\text{-hat}\), and the same rewrite continues with H_AC and H_BC to replace those two expectations by \(d_{AC}\) and \(d_{BC}\), leaving the goal \(d_{AC} - d_{BC} = d_{AB}\). Finally by rewrite H_trans rewrites \(d_{AB}\) to \(d_{AC} - d_{BC}\) and closes.

In state snapshots, after move=> H_AC H_BC H_trans the three premises sit in the context and the goal is the bare conclusion:

  H_AC : E d_AC_hat = d_AC
  H_BC : E d_BC_hat = d_BC
  H_trans : d_AB = d_AC - d_BC
  ============================
  E (fun omega => d_AC_hat omega - d_BC_hat omega) = d_AB

The chained rewrite E_sub H_AC H_BC applies three equations left to right in turn, and the goal shrinks at each step:

  after E_sub:   E d_AC_hat - E d_BC_hat = d_AB
  after H_AC:    d_AC - E d_BC_hat = d_AB
  after H_BC:    d_AC - d_BC = d_AB

One point of rewrite direction is worth naming, since it is a common first-time snag. rewrite H_trans uses H_trans : d_AB = d_AC - d_BC left to right: it looks for the left side d_AB in the goal and replaces it with the right side d_AC - d_BC. The goal is d_AC - d_BC = d_AB, whose only d_AB is on the right, so it becomes d_AC - d_BC = d_AC - d_BC, which closes by reflexivity. To rewrite the other way, replacing d_AC - d_BC by d_AB, one would write rewrite -H_trans with the leading minus, the SSReflect direction flag (not a subtraction).

The only structural property of E used anywhere is linearity, through E_sub; this is why the abstract operator suffices. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The same file continues with bucher_variance, the additivity of variances under zero covariance (Theorem 14.2), proved by the identical pattern over abstract Var and Cov operators with the variance-of-a-difference rule supplied as a hypothesis.

G.11 Inspecting the active axioms

The decisive question a proof assistant can answer that a paper proof cannot is: which axioms does this finished theorem actually use? The command is Print Assumptions. It traverses the kernel proof term and reports every axiom and unproved constant the theorem transitively depends on. To run it, compile the tree (Section G.5), then load the relevant module by its logical name and query the theorem.

Remark. Two kinds of assumption, before you read the output. A theorem depends on two different things, and Print Assumptions reports only the second. The first kind is its ordinary premises: the modeling assumptions written into the statement, such as unbiasedness of an estimator or constancy of relative effects. You read those by reading the statement (Check prints it). The second kind is the foundational axioms: logical principles the proof term rests on, below the level of any particular theorem. Print Assumptions prints the second kind only. So the question “what does this proof rest on” has two halves: read the statement for the scientific premises, then run Print Assumptions for the logical axioms. Keep both in view; neither alone is the full picture (Definition 30.1). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Interactively, start a Coq top level with the project’s load path and issue the commands:

coqtop -R theories ITC          # or: rocqtop -R theories ITC
Require Import ITC.MAIC.
Print Assumptions ESS_upper_bound.

Equivalently, in batch mode, place a scratch file at the repository root (anywhere outside the _CoqProject file list, so make ignores it) and run it through the compiler with the same load path. The scratch file holds exactly the two commands you would otherwise type interactively:

(* scratch.v *)
Require Import ITC.MAIC.
Print Assumptions ESS_upper_bound.
coqc -R theories ITC scratch.v        # prints the assumptions report to the terminal

For the effective-sample-size bound the output is exactly the three classical axioms inherited from mathcomp-analysis and nothing else:

Axioms:
boolp.propositional_extensionality : forall P Q : Prop, P <-> Q -> P = Q
boolp.functional_extensionality_dep :
  forall (A : Type) (B : A -> Type) (f g : forall x : A, B x),
  (forall x : A, f x = g x) -> f = g
boolp.constructive_indefinite_description :
  forall (A : Type) (P : A -> Prop), (exists x : A, P x) -> {x : A | P x}

These are the three active axioms catalogued in Definition 30.2; for what they mean and why they are uncontroversial, see Section 30.7.

Remark. Reading a theorem’s premises versus its axioms. Print Assumptions reports only the foundational axioms, not the named section hypotheses. A theorem such as mu1_IPW_unbiased carries the tower-property premise Hipw1 as an explicit argument in its statement (the boundary policy of Section 30.9); that premise is visible by reading the statement, while Print Assumptions confirms that, beyond it, the proof rests on nothing but the three classical axioms. The Bucher theorem you just queried makes the same point concretely. Its statement, printed by Check bucher_unbiased, lists three premises: E d_AC_hat = d_AC, E d_BC_hat = d_BC, and d_AB = d_AC - d_BC. Those are the scientific assumptions, the unbiasedness of each trial estimator and the constancy of relative effects, and they are what a health technology assessment must defend. Its Print Assumptions output, shown above, lists three classical axioms and nothing more. The first triple tells you what the theorem assumes about the world; the second tells you what it assumes about logic; the theorem is unconditionally true given the first, resting on nothing but the second.

The two readings together are what Definition 30.1 calls the two-level reading. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Four further commands are useful while reading. Check t prints the type of a term or the statement of a lemma; About name prints a lemma’s statement together with its arguments and how they are inferred; Print name prints the body of a definition; Search pat finds lemmas whose statement matches a pattern, for example Search _ (_ + _ - _) to find cancellation lemmas; and Locate "++" resolves a notation to the constant it denotes. Stepping through a proof in VsCoq, Proof General, or RocqIDE and watching the goal change after each tactic is, in practice, the fastest way to understand a script.

As a reading aid, Check prints a theorem’s statement without opening the file. For bucher_unbiased its output begins with the section parameters it discharges (the space Omega, the estimators, the operator E, and the linearity hypothesis) and ends with the operative part:

  ... ->
  forall d_AB d_AC d_BC : R,
    E d_AC_hat = d_AC ->
    E d_BC_hat = d_BC ->
    d_AB = d_AC - d_BC ->
    E (fun omega => d_AC_hat omega - d_BC_hat omega) = d_AB

The premises and conclusion appear exactly as in the source, so Check alone is often enough to audit what a tagged theorem assumes.
