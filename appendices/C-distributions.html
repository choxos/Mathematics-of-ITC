Appendix C — Probability Distributions

This appendix is a reference catalogue of the probability distributions used in the book. For each family we record the support, the probability mass or density function, the mean, the variance, and the moment generating function where it exists. The discrete families (Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson binomial, Poisson) and the normal, multivariate normal, and standard normal are defined and their moments derived in Section 4.11 and Section 4.12 of Chapter 4; this appendix collects those results and adds the further families (exponential, gamma, beta, Weibull, Dirichlet) that appear later as survival baselines in Chapters 25 and 26 and as conjugate priors in Chapter 8. Nothing here is proved that is not proved in the chapters or that does not follow by a one-line calculation; the value of the appendix is a single place to look up a defining formula. For the symbols used throughout, see Notation.

C.1 How to read this appendix

The fastest way to use this catalogue is to first name the modeling role you are in, then look up the family. Every distribution in the book plays one of six roles:

  1. an outcome likelihood, the model for the observed data on one arm of a trial (a binomial for responders, a Poisson for event counts, a normal for a continuous endpoint);
  2. a prior, the distribution placed on an unknown parameter before seeing data (a beta on a response probability, a gamma on a rate, a normal on a treatment effect);
  3. a covariate model, the distribution of baseline patient characteristics (a multivariate normal for correlated covariates);
  4. a discrete aggregation device, used to turn individual-level risks into an aggregate event count (the Poisson binomial and its Poisson approximation in Part V);
  5. a survival baseline, the model for a time-to-event outcome (exponential, Weibull); or
  6. a numerical integration device, used to average an individual likelihood over a covariate distribution (the standard normal and the Gaussian copula in the ML-NMR integration of Chapter 22).

Table C.1 maps each family to the clinical object it typically models and to the chapters that use it. A reader browsing this appendix should start there, identify the role, and then read only the relevant catalogue entry.

C.2 Conventions

Three plain-language reminders precede the formulas, because the tables below mix objects of different kinds. The support of a distribution is the set of values the random variable can actually take, that is, the values where the mass or density is positive; everything outside the support has probability zero (for a binomial the support is \(\{0,1,\dots,n\}\), for a Weibull survival time it is \((0,\infty)\)). A mass function \(P(X=k)\) gives a genuine probability at each point of a discrete support and never exceeds \(1\). A density \(f(x)\) does not: it is a rate of probability per unit length, and an actual probability is recovered only by integrating \(f\) over an interval, so a density can exceed \(1\) (a tightly concentrated normal has a peak above \(1\)) even though no probability ever does. This is why a density value is not itself a probability.

The moment generating function (MGF) of a random vector \(X\) is \(M_X(\mathbf{t})=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{t}^{\top}X}]\), written \(M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\) in the scalar case (Definition 4.7). We say the MGF exists when it is finite for all \(\mathbf{t}\) in an open neighborhood of the origin; in that case it determines all moments by differentiation at the origin (Theorem 4.10) and determines the law uniquely (Theorem 4.11). Intuitively the MGF is a bookkeeping device: because \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX}=X e^{tX}\), each derivative evaluated at \(t=0\) pulls down one extra factor of \(X\), so \(M_X^{(r)}(0)=\mathbb{E}[X^{r}]\) and the entire sequence of moments is stored in one function. That is the only reason the MGF earns a column in the tables below: given the MGF, the mean and variance are two derivatives away. For some families with a finite MGF the function has no elementary closed form; for the beta and Weibull families this is noted in the catalogue, and for the Dirichlet the joint MGF on the ambient space is degenerate.

(Advanced; skip on a first pass.) “Degenerate on the ambient space” means the following. When the support lies in a hyperplane, as it does for the Dirichlet whose components satisfy \(\sum_k x_k=1\), one coordinate is fixed by the others, namely \(x_K=1-\sum_{k<K}x_k\). The joint MGF on \(\mathbb{R}^{K}\) is then really a function of only \(K-1\) free arguments and cannot characterize the law in the usual full-rank way, so we describe such a family by its moments instead of by an MGF. This is why the Dirichlet row lists moments rather than an MGF.

Two special functions recur. The gamma function is \[ \Gamma(s)=\int_0^{\infty} u^{s-1}e^{-u}\,du,\qquad s>0, \tag{C.1}\] which satisfies \(\Gamma(s+1)=s\,\Gamma(s)\) and \(\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!\) for positive integers \(n\); it is the continuous interpolation of the factorial and appears in the normalizing constants of the gamma, beta, Weibull, and Dirichlet densities. The beta function is \[ B(a,b)=\int_0^1 u^{a-1}(1-u)^{b-1}\,du=\frac{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)},\qquad a,b>0, \tag{C.2}\] and its multivariate form, used by the Dirichlet density, is \(B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\prod_{k=1}^K\Gamma(\alpha_k)\big/\Gamma\!\big(\sum_{k=1}^K\alpha_k\big)\); the scalar \(B(a,b)\) is the \(K=2\) case of the vector \(B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\), so the two share a symbol on purpose. The numerical evaluation of these functions, together with the error function below, is treated in Appendix D.

We fix two parametrization conventions that are sources of confusion across the literature. The gamma and exponential families are parametrized by a rate, so that \(\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)\) has shape \(\alpha\), rate \(\beta\), and mean \(\alpha/\beta\); this matches the convention of Proposition 8.1 in Chapter 8. The Weibull family is parametrized by a shape and a scale, related to the proportional-hazards parametrization of Chapter 25 as recorded below. A rate has units of events per unit of the variable, so a larger rate means shorter times; a scale has the units of the variable itself, so a larger scale means longer times. Because closely related survival families are written with a rate, a scale, or a hazard depending on the chapter, Table C.2 gathers the three side by side.

C.3 Discrete distributions

The four discrete families are defined in Definition 4.8, Definition 4.9, Definition 4.11, and Definition 4.10. Their moment generating functions are finite for every \(t\in\mathbb{R}\).

Each family models a distinct kind of health data.

Bernoulli. A single yes/no outcome with success probability \(p\): one patient, responder (\(X=1\)) or non-responder (\(X=0\)). It is the atom from which the binary-outcome likelihoods of the book are built.

Binomial. The number of successes in \(n\) independent, identical yes/no trials, each with the same probability \(p\): the count of responders out of \(n\) randomized patients. This is the workhorse likelihood for aggregate binary endpoints in meta-analysis (Chapter 13) and network meta-analysis, where each arm reports “\(r\) responders out of \(n\).”

Poisson binomial. The number of successes in \(n\) independent trials whose success probabilities \(p_1,\dots,p_n\) are allowed to differ. The mass function looks forbidding but says something simple: to get exactly \(k\) successes, sum over every way of choosing which \(k\) of the \(n\) trials succeed, and for each such choice multiply the success probabilities of the chosen trials by the failure probabilities of the rest. It is the natural law for an aggregate event count when a model assigns each patient a different predicted risk, which is exactly the situation in covariate-adjusted binary outcomes; it drives the discrete aggregation results of Section 21.5 in Chapter 21.

Poisson. The count of events when events arrive independently at a constant average, for example the number of relapses in a fixed follow-up window. In practice the Poisson mean is a rate times an exposure: if events occur at rate \(\rho\) per person-year and a patient is followed for time \(E\), then the event count is \(\mathrm{Poi}(\rho E)\), so \(\lambda=\rho E\). This is how Poisson models describe events per person-time rather than bare counts.

The families nest. A Bernoulli variable is \(\mathrm{Bin}(1,p)\); a binomial is the Poisson binomial with all \(p_i\) equal to a common \(p\), in which case every entry of the Poisson binomial row reduces to its binomial counterpart. The Poisson is the law of rare events: if \(X_n\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\lambda/n)\) then \(X_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\) (Theorem 4.13), so a Poisson is the limit of a binomial with many trials each individually rare; a quantitative total-variation form of this approximation drives the discrete aggregation results of Part V. Sums of independent Poisson variables are Poisson, with rates adding, by the product rule for moment generating functions (Theorem 4.12).

Remark. For a fixed mean \(\sum_i p_i\), the Poisson binomial variance \(\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)=\sum_i p_i-\sum_i p_i^2\) is largest when the \(p_i\) are equal, by convexity of \(p\mapsto p^2\). (Convexity means \(\sum_i p_i^2\) is smallest when the \(p_i\) are all equal, hence the variance \(\sum_i p_i-\sum_i p_i^2\) is largest there; pulling the risks apart only lowers the aggregate variance.) Heterogeneous individual risks therefore produce a less dispersed aggregate count than a binomial with the same expected number of events, a fact used when an aggregate event count is modeled under covariate-adjusted risks. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example C.1 (Aggregating three unequal risks) Suppose three patients have predicted event probabilities \(p_1=0.1\), \(p_2=0.3\), \(p_3=0.6\), and we want the distribution of the total number of events \(X\sim\mathrm{PoBin}(0.1,0.3,0.6)\). Reading the Poisson binomial mass function one value of \(k\) at a time, \[ \begin{aligned} P(X=0)&=(0.9)(0.7)(0.4)=0.252,\\ P(X=1)&=(0.1)(0.7)(0.4)+(0.9)(0.3)(0.4)+(0.9)(0.7)(0.6)=0.514,\\ P(X=2)&=(0.1)(0.3)(0.4)+(0.1)(0.7)(0.6)+(0.9)(0.3)(0.6)=0.216,\\ P(X=3)&=(0.1)(0.3)(0.6)=0.018, \end{aligned} \] which sum to \(1\). The mean is \(\mathbb{E}[X]=0.1+0.3+0.6=1.0\) and the variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=0.1(0.9)+0.3(0.7)+0.6(0.4)=0.54\). Compare a binomial with the same mean, namely \(\mathrm{Bin}(3,\tfrac13)\), whose variance is \(3\cdot\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac23\approx0.667\). The heterogeneous risks give the smaller variance \(0.54<0.667\), exactly the effect of the remark above: when predicted risks differ across patients the aggregate event count is less dispersed than a binomial with the same expected number of events. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

When there are more than two response categories, the binomial generalizes to the multinomial, which the appendix uses only as the likelihood conjugate to the Dirichlet of Section C.6.

Definition C.1 (Multinomial distribution) Fix \(N\in\{1,2,\dots\}\) and a probability vector \(\mathbf{p}=(p_1,\dots,p_K)\) with \(p_k\ge 0\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k=1\). The count vector \(\mathbf{n}=(n_1,\dots,n_K)\) of the outcomes of \(N\) independent trials, each landing in category \(k\) with probability \(p_k\), has the multinomial distribution \(\mathrm{Multinomial}(N,\mathbf{p})\), with mass function \[ P(\mathbf{n})=\binom{N}{n_1,\dots,n_K}\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_k^{\,n_k} =\frac{N!}{n_1!\cdots n_K!}\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_k^{\,n_k}, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K}n_k=N, \tag{C.3}\] on the set of nonnegative integer vectors summing to \(N\).

The multinomial is the count of how many of \(N\) patients fall into each of \(K\) response categories, for example none, partial, or complete response. It records the joint distribution of category counts, so a single component \(n_k\) is \(\mathrm{Bin}(N,p_k)\) and at \(K=2\) the whole family reduces to \(\mathrm{Bin}(N,p_1)\). Its first two moments are \[ \mathbb{E}[n_k]=N p_k,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(n_k)=N p_k(1-p_k),\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(n_j,n_k)=-N p_j p_k\ (j\neq k), \] the negative covariance reflecting that the counts must sum to \(N\), so a surplus in one category forces a deficit in another. This is the likelihood in the Dirichlet-multinomial conjugate pair of Table C.7.

C.4 Continuous univariate distributions

The normal density and its moments are Definition 4.12. The remaining four families are first used here; the gamma and exponential appear as the conjugate prior and count-data baseline of Chapter 8 and as survival baselines in Chapters 25 and 26, the Weibull is the power-law-hazard survival model of Section 25.3 in Chapter 25, and the beta is the conjugate prior of Theorem 8.4. The gamma function \(\Gamma\) and beta function \(B\) are those of Equation C.1 and Equation C.2.

Several entries deserve comment. In modeling terms the four non-normal families split cleanly by role: the exponential and Weibull model time-to-event outcomes (overall or progression-free survival), the gamma is a prior on a positive rate or precision, and the beta is a prior on a probability; the normal models a continuous endpoint and, on the log scale, an estimated relative effect.

Exponential and gamma. The exponential is the gamma of unit shape, \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)= \mathrm{Gamma}(1,\lambda)\), as the densities and moment generating functions show. It is the simplest time-to-event model, the one with a constant hazard: the instantaneous event rate does not change over time, so the exponential is memoryless, meaning a patient who has survived to time \(s\) has the same remaining-life distribution as a fresh patient (\(P(X>s+u\mid X>s)=P(X>u)\)). Because independent moment generating functions multiply (Theorem 4.12), a sum of \(n\) independent \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) variables has MGF \((1-t/\lambda)^{-n}\), hence is \(\mathrm{Gamma}(n,\lambda)\); more generally gammas with a common rate add in shape. The chi-square law with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom is \(\mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2,\,1/2)\). The gamma is the book’s default prior on a positive quantity such as a Poisson rate (Proposition 8.1) or a precision; its shape parameter \(\alpha\) sets the shape of the density, with \(\alpha<1\) giving a density that diverges at the origin and decreases, \(\alpha=1\) the exponential, and \(\alpha>1\) a unimodal density that starts at zero, rises to a peak, and decays. The MGF \((1-t/\beta)^{-\alpha}\) is finite precisely for \(t<\beta\) and diverges as \(t\uparrow\beta\), the right tail being too heavy to integrate against \(e^{tx}\) beyond that point (for \(t\ge\beta\) the defining integral \(\int_0^\infty e^{tx}f(x)\,dx\) diverges because \(e^{tx}e^{-\beta x}\) no longer decays); all moments are nonetheless finite, with \(\mathbb{E}[X^{r}]=\Gamma(\alpha+r)/\big(\beta^{r}\Gamma(\alpha)\big)\) for \(r>-\alpha\).

Beta. The single most useful fact for a health researcher is that the beta puts a flexible distribution on a probability: because its support is \((0,1)\), it is the natural prior on a response rate, and it is the conjugate prior for the binomial (Theorem 8.4). Its two parameters read as pseudo-counts: \(a\) behaves like a number of prior successes and \(b\) like a number of prior failures, so the prior mean \(a/(a+b)\) is the prior success fraction and larger \(a+b\) means a more concentrated, more confident prior. As a shape guide: \(a=b=1\) is the uniform law on \((0,1)\) (no prior information); \(a=b>1\) is symmetric and unimodal at \(\tfrac12\); \(a>b\) leans toward \(1\) and \(a<b\) toward \(0\); and \(a,b<1\) is U-shaped, piling mass near the two endpoints. The density is bounded with compact support, so the MGF is finite for every \(t\), but it has no elementary closed form. (Advanced; you will not use the beta MGF in practice, since the moments below are elementary and are what the book uses.) The MGF equals the confluent hypergeometric function \[ M_X(t)={}_1F_1(a;a+b;t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_n}{(a+b)_n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},\qquad (c)_n:=c(c+1)\cdots(c+n-1), \tag{C.4}\] where \((c)_n\) is the rising factorial (Pochhammer symbol). Moments are elementary even though the MGF is not: \(\mathbb{E}[X^{r}]=B(a+r,b)/B(a,b)=\Gamma(a+r)\Gamma(a+b)\big/\big(\Gamma(a)\Gamma(a+b+r)\big)\) for \(r>-a\), which gives the mean and variance in Table C.4. The case \(a=b=1\) is the uniform law on \((0,1)\).

Weibull. The survival function is \(S(x)=\exp(-(x/\lambda)^{k})\) and the hazard is \(h(x)=\frac{k}{\lambda}(x/\lambda)^{k-1}\), a power law in \(x\). The shape parameter \(k\) controls whether risk rises or falls over time, which is exactly what a clinician chooses a survival model to capture: \(k<1\) gives a decreasing hazard (early high risk that wanes, the “infant mortality” pattern), \(k=1\) gives a constant hazard (the exponential), and \(k>1\) gives an increasing hazard (risk that grows with time, the “aging” or wear-out pattern). The \(r\)-th moment is \(\mathbb{E}[X^{r}]=\lambda^{r}\Gamma(1+r/k)\), from which the tabulated mean and variance follow. With \(k=1\) the hazard is constant and the Weibull is the exponential with rate \(1/\lambda\). The moment generating function has no elementary closed form and, more sharply, is finite on a neighborhood of the origin only when \(k\ge 1\): for \(k<1\) the tail \(\exp(-(x/\lambda)^{k})\) decays slower than any exponential, so \(\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]=\infty\) for every \(t>0\) and the MGF does not exist, though all polynomial moments remain finite. (The reason \(k<1\) makes the tail slow is that the exponent \((x/\lambda)^{k}\) then grows sub-linearly in \(x\), so \(e^{-(x/\lambda)^{k}}\) falls off more slowly than \(e^{-cx}\) for any \(c>0\).)

Example C.2 (How the Weibull shape bends the hazard) Fix the scale \(\lambda=1\) and read the hazard \(h(x)=k\,x^{k-1}\) at three follow-up times for three shapes. The table gives \(h(x)\) at \(x=0.25,\ 1,\ 4\).

Shape \(k\) \(h(0.25)\) \(h(1)\) \(h(4)\) Hazard over time
\(0.5\) \(1.00\) \(0.50\) \(0.25\) decreasing (early risk wanes)
\(1\) \(1.00\) \(1.00\) \(1.00\) constant (exponential, memoryless)
\(2\) \(0.50\) \(2.00\) \(8.00\) increasing (risk grows with time)

With \(k=0.5\) the event rate is highest just after baseline and falls; with \(k=1\) it never changes; with \(k=2\) it climbs steadily. All three curves pass through \(h=1\) at \(x=\lambda=1\), so the scale sets the time at which the hazard equals the reference level \(1/\lambda\) and the shape sets the tilt. This is the single most important reading of a Weibull survival model: the shape \(k\) is chosen to match whether a clinical hazard rises or falls with time. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Hazard parametrization. Chapter 25 writes the Weibull through its hazard, \(h(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{x})\,\nu\,t^{\nu-1}\), giving the survival function \(S(t\mid\mathbf{x})=\exp(-\lambda(\mathbf{x})\,t^{\nu})\) (see Section 25.3). This is the shape-scale form above with shape \(k=\nu\) and scale \(\lambda=\lambda(\mathbf{x})^{-1/\nu}\), equivalently \(\lambda(\mathbf{x})=\lambda^{-\nu}\). The two parametrizations describe the same family; the hazard form places the covariate structure on the rate-type coefficient \(\lambda(\mathbf{x})\), which is convenient for proportional-hazards regression. See Table C.2 for the three survival parametrizations side by side. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

C.5 The multivariate normal

The multivariate normal \(\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) is defined in Definition 4.13 through its moment generating function and is developed in full in Section 4.12 of Chapter 4. We restate the core formulas and point to the chapter for proofs. In this book the family plays four roles: it models a vector of correlated baseline covariates in a trial population (Chapters 12 and 20); it is the prior for a vector of random effects in a hierarchical model (Chapter 8); it is the approximate sampling distribution of a vector of estimated log-effects fed into network meta-analysis (Chapters 13 and 15); and, through the Gaussian copula, it is the engine that ties marginal covariate distributions together for the ML-NMR numerical integration of Chapter 22 (Section 22.9). Throughout, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ 0\) means \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is positive definite, which is the condition under which the covariance is invertible and a density exists.

The family is closed under the operations that drive normal-theory inference, proved in Chapter 4.

  • Affine maps. If \(\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) and \(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{b}+B\mathbf{X}\), then \(\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}_q(\mathbf{b}+B\boldsymbol{\mu},\,B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}B^{\top})\) (Theorem 4.14). In particular every subvector is multivariate normal and every linear combination \(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is univariate normal.
  • Uncorrelated implies independent. Jointly normal blocks with zero cross-covariance are independent (Lemma 4.3), a property special to the normal family.
  • Conditioning. With \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\succ 0\), \[ \mathbf{X}_1\mid\mathbf{X}_2=\mathbf{x}_2\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\Big(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2),\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\Big), \] the conditional mean affine in \(\mathbf{x}_2\) and the conditional covariance, the Schur complement of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\), free of \(\mathbf{x}_2\) (Theorem 4.15, Equation 4.15).

The conditioning formula is best read as a linear regression of \(\mathbf{X}_1\) on \(\mathbf{X}_2\): the conditional mean is the prediction \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\) with regression-coefficient matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\), and the conditional covariance (the term some readers know as the Schur complement, which you may take as a name on a first pass) does not depend on the observed value \(\mathbf{x}_2\), so observing \(\mathbf{X}_2\) shifts the center of \(\mathbf{X}_1\) but does not change its spread. This is precisely what one needs to impute an unreported baseline covariate from reported ones in a population-adjustment problem.

Example C.3 (Predicting a missing covariate from an observed one) Let \((X_1,X_2)\) be bivariate normal with means \(\mu_1=\mu_2=0\), unit variances, and correlation \(\rho=0.6\), so \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=\rho=0.6\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}=1\). Suppose \(X_1\) (say a baseline covariate not reported by a study) is unknown but the correlated covariate \(X_2=2\) is observed. The conditioning formula gives \[ X_1\mid X_2=2\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\big(\rho\cdot 2,\ 1-\rho^{2}\big)=\mathcal{N}(1.2,\ 0.64). \] The predicted mean \(1.2\) is the regression of \(X_1\) on \(X_2\) with slope \(\rho=0.6\), and the conditional variance \(0.64\) is smaller than the marginal variance \(1\): knowing the correlated covariate sharpens the prediction. The conditional variance \(1-\rho^{2}\) would be the same whatever value of \(X_2\) we had observed. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The univariate normal is the case \(p=1\), and the standard normal \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), with density \(\phi\) and distribution function \(\Phi\), is treated in Section C.7.

C.6 The Dirichlet distribution

The Dirichlet is the distribution of a random probability vector and is the multivariate generalization of the beta. In one line: it is a prior for a list of probabilities that must add up to one, such as the probabilities of the response categories none, partial, and complete. It is the natural conjugate prior for the cell probabilities of a categorical or multinomial outcome (Definition C.1), generalizing the beta-binomial pair of Theorem 8.4.

Definition C.2 (Dirichlet distribution) Let \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_K)\) with each \(\alpha_k>0\), and write \(\alpha_0=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_k\). A random vector \(\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\dots,X_K)\) supported on the open simplex \(\Delta^{K-1}=\{\mathbf{x}:x_k>0,\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}x_k=1\}\) has the Dirichlet distribution \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\) if it has density \[ f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\prod_{k=1}^{K}x_k^{\alpha_k-1}, \qquad B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Gamma(\alpha_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}, \tag{C.5}\] with respect to Lebesgue measure on the \((K-1)\)-dimensional simplex, taking \(x_1,\dots,x_{K-1}\) as free coordinates and \(x_K=1-\sum_{k<K}x_k\).

The simplex \(\Delta^{K-1}\) is just the set of valid probability vectors: \(K\) nonnegative numbers that add to one. (Advanced; skip on a first pass.) The phrase “density with respect to Lebesgue measure on the \((K-1)\)-dimensional simplex, taking \(x_1,\dots,x_{K-1}\) as free coordinates” means only that we treat \(x_1,\dots,x_{K-1}\) as the free variables, with the last coordinate forced to be \(x_K=1-\sum_{k<K}x_k\), and integrate over those \(K-1\) free variables. The normalizing constant \(B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\) is the multivariate beta function of Equation C.2, the product-of-gammas generalization of the scalar \(B(a,b)\).

The simplex constraint \(\sum_k X_k=1\) forces the components to be negatively associated and makes the joint moment generating function on \(\mathbb{R}^{K}\) degenerate, so the Dirichlet is described by its moments rather than by an MGF (this is the hyperplane degeneracy explained in Section C.2). The mixed moments are \[ \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\prod_{k=1}^{K}X_k^{r_k}\Big] =\frac{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mathbf{r})}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})} =\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0+\textstyle\sum_k r_k)}\prod_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_k+r_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)}, \tag{C.6}\] from which the first two moments follow.

With \(K=2\) and \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(a,b)\) the first coordinate \(X_1\) is \(\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)\), so the beta entries of Table C.4 are the \(K=2\) case of Table C.6. The parameters read as pseudo-counts, exactly as for the beta: \(\alpha_k\) is a prior count for category \(k\), and the total \(\alpha_0=\sum_k\alpha_k\) is a concentration. The mean is \(\alpha_k/\alpha_0\) regardless of \(\alpha_0\), while the variances and covariances shrink as \(\alpha_0\) grows. The picture: a small \(\alpha_0\) spreads the random probability vector out toward the corners of the simplex (nearly all mass on one category, but which one varies), while a large \(\alpha_0\) clusters it tightly around the mean vector \((\alpha_1/\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_K/\alpha_0)\). Concentration is thus the multivariate reading of “how confident is the prior,” the same role \(a+b\) plays for the beta.

C.7 The standard normal function and density

The standard normal \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) is the case \(\mu=0,\ \sigma^{2}=1\) of Definition 4.12. Its density and distribution function carry the dedicated symbols \(\phi\) and \(\Phi\): \[ \phi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-z^{2}/2},\qquad \Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(u)\,du,\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}. \tag{C.7}\] The density \(\phi\) is even and satisfies the differential identities \[ \phi'(z)=-z\,\phi(z),\qquad \phi''(z)=(z^{2}-1)\,\phi(z),\qquad \Phi'(z)=\phi(z), \tag{C.8}\] and the distribution function satisfies \(\Phi(0)=\tfrac12\), the reflection identity \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\), and the limits \(\Phi(z)\to 1\) as \(z\to\infty\) and \(\Phi(z)\to 0\) as \(z\to-\infty\). The mean and variance are \(\mathbb{E}[Z]=0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Z)=1\), the moment generating function is \(M_Z(t)=e^{t^{2}/2}\), finite for all \(t\), and the moments are \[ \mathbb{E}[Z^{2m-1}]=0,\qquad \mathbb{E}[Z^{2m}]=\frac{(2m)!}{2^{m}m!}=(2m-1)!!,\qquad m=1,2,\dots, \tag{C.9}\] read off as the Taylor coefficients of \(e^{t^{2}/2}=\sum_{m\ge 0}t^{2m}/(2^{m}m!)\) through Theorem 4.10.

There is no elementary closed form for \(\Phi\). It is expressed through the error function \(\mathrm{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^{x}e^{-u^{2}}\,du\) and its complement \(\mathrm{erfc}=1-\mathrm{erf}\) by \[ \Phi(z)=\tfrac12\Big[1+\mathrm{erf}\!\big(z/\sqrt{2}\big)\Big],\qquad 1-\Phi(z)=\tfrac12\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\big(z/\sqrt{2}\big), \tag{C.10}\] whose numerical evaluation is treated in Appendix D. (Advanced; optional on a first pass.) For the upper tail the Mills ratio gives the two-sided bound, valid for \(z>0\), \[ \Big(\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{z^{3}}\Big)\phi(z)\ <\ 1-\Phi(z)\ <\ \frac{\phi(z)}{z}, \tag{C.11}\] so that \(1-\Phi(z)\sim \phi(z)/z\) as \(z\to\infty\). This bound matters because \(1-\Phi(z)\) is the tail probability of a standardized effect, so it controls how fast \(p\)-values and normal-approximation tail probabilities for large standardized effects decay; a novice can note the asymptotic \(1-\Phi(z)\sim\phi(z)/z\) and move on.

The function \(\Phi\) also feeds two constructions the book uses later. It is the inverse link of the probit regression model (Chapter 7), the model in which a binary probability is written \(P(Y=1)=\Phi(\eta)\) for a linear predictor \(\eta\). And its inverse \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the normal quantile transform at the heart of the Gaussian copula \(C_\Omega(\mathbf{u})=\Phi_\Omega\big(\Phi^{-1}(u_1),\dots,\Phi^{-1}(u_d)\big)\) used in ML-NMR numerical integration: given a uniform variable \(u\in(0,1)\), the value \(\Phi^{-1}(u)\) is the standard-normal score with that lower-tail probability, so \(\Phi^{-1}\) turns the uniform points of a quasi-Monte Carlo rule into correlated normal covariate draws over which an individual likelihood is averaged (Section 22.7, Section 22.9). See Notation for the copula symbol.

C.8 Conjugate pairs

A prior family is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior stays in the family with updated hyperparameters. Conjugacy is an algebraic convenience, not a requirement of Bayesian modeling: it makes the update a closed-form parameter change instead of a numerical integration, but a valid Bayesian analysis may use any proper prior (and the book’s hierarchical models in Chapter 8 and Chapter 24 mostly use non-conjugate priors sampled by Markov chain Monte Carlo). Chapter 8 proves the three pairs of Section 8.4 in full and uses them to build the shrinkage estimator and the Gibbs updates of its samplers. Table C.7 collects those pairs, together with the Dirichlet-multinomial pair that generalizes the beta-binomial; throughout, the gamma prior uses the shape-rate parametrization, so \(\mathrm{Gamma}(a,b)\) has mean \(a/b\).

In each row the posterior hyperparameters combine prior information with data additively. For the normal-normal pair precisions (the reciprocals of the variances) add, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of prior mean and data (Equation 8.5): if the prior says the effect is about \(\mu_0\) and the data say about \(y\), the posterior mean sits between them, pulled toward whichever is more precise. For the beta-binomial and Dirichlet-multinomial pairs the prior hyperparameters act as pseudo-counts that are incremented by the observed counts (Equation 8.6); and for the gamma-Poisson pair the shape is incremented by the total count and the rate by the number of observations. The posterior mean is in every case a convex combination of the prior mean and the maximum likelihood estimate, the analytic form of shrinkage that Chapter 8 develops.

Example C.4 (A beta-binomial update with numbers) Put a uniform prior \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)\) on a response probability \(p\) (the flat prior: every value in \((0,1)\) equally likely, prior mean \(\tfrac12\)). Observe \(y=7\) responders out of \(n=10\) patients. By the beta-binomial row of Table C.7 the posterior is \[ p\mid y\ \sim\ \mathrm{Beta}(1+7,\ 1+10-7)=\mathrm{Beta}(8,4), \] with posterior mean \(8/(8+4)=8/12=2/3\approx0.667\). The two prior pseudo-counts \(a=b=1\) have been incremented by the \(7\) observed successes and \(3\) observed failures. Contrast the maximum likelihood estimate \(\hat p=7/10=0.7\) and the prior mean \(0.5\): the posterior mean \(0.667\) lies between them, and is in fact their convex combination \[ \frac{a+b}{a+b+n}\cdot\frac12+\frac{n}{a+b+n}\cdot\frac{y}{n} =\frac{2}{12}\cdot 0.5+\frac{10}{12}\cdot 0.7=0.667, \] weighted toward the data because the flat prior carries only \(a+b=2\) pseudo-observations against \(n=10\) real ones. This is the shrinkage reading of the closing paragraph, made concrete. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why the inverse-gamma is not cataloged. The classical conjugate prior for the variance \(\sigma^{2}\) of a normal likelihood with known mean is the inverse-gamma (a gamma law on the precision \(1/\sigma^{2}\)). This book does not use it: for the between-study variance of a hierarchical model the text follows modern practice and places a half-normal or half-\(t\) prior on the between-study standard deviation (Chapter 8; Dias et al. (2018)), which behaves better when the variance may be near zero, where an inverse-gamma prior is unstable. The inverse-gamma is therefore mentioned here only so a reader does not search the tables for it. \(\blacktriangleleft\)
