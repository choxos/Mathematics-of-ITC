Appendix D — Special Functions for Numerical Integration

The numerical machinery of multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) rests on a small library of special functions, and the analytic shortcuts that occasionally rescue a closed form rest on the same library. This appendix is the proved reference for those functions. It collects, in one place and with full derivations, the four objects that Part V calls on repeatedly: the moment generating function of the normal, which closes the log-link aggregation integral of Theorem 20.4; the error function, the analyst’s name for the normal distribution function and the gateway to its series and tail expansions; Owen’s \(T\) function, which evaluates bivariate normal probabilities and, through them, the expected squared probit needed for probit-link aggregation; and the radical-inverse function, the arithmetic atom of the Sobol’ sequences that drive the quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) integration of Theorem 22.6. Two further functions that arise in this circle of ideas, the one-probit and two-probit approximations to the logistic function, are derived here because they turn the otherwise non-elementary logit aggregation integral of Theorem 20.5 into an explicit, fast, and surprisingly accurate closed form.

Everything is proved or derived. Where a result is established in the main text we cite it by slug and, when it sharpens the reference to restate the derivation, reprove it self-contained. Symbols follow Notation: \(\Phi\) and \(\phi\) are the standard normal distribution and density functions, \(\mathrm{expit}(z)=e^{z}/(1+e^{z})\) is the inverse logit (also called the logistic sigmoid), \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the normal quantile function, and \(M_X(\mathbf{t})=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{t}^\top X}]\) is the moment generating function of Definition 4.7. Two standard facts of real analysis are used without proof: the Gaussian normalization \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt=\sqrt{\pi}\) (equivalently \(\int\phi=1\)), proved by the polar- coordinates evaluation of its square, and differentiation under the integral sign, justified by dominated convergence (Theorem 4.2) whenever a dominating integrable envelope is exhibited, which we do at each use.

D.1 How to use this appendix

Scope, and what is deliberately left out. This appendix is the proved reference for the five objects that the Part V integration machinery calls on: the normal moment generating function, the error function, Owen’s \(T\) function, the one- and two-probit approximations to the logistic, and the radical inverse behind the Sobol’ point set. It is not a general catalogue of the special functions of statistics. The gamma function, its logarithm, the digamma function, and the beta function do appear in Part V, but only as the fixed normalizing constants of standard likelihoods, for example the log-gamma terms in the Poisson and binomial log-likelihoods of Chapters 21 and 25; they are classical, are not special to indirect comparison, and are not reproved here. Everything below, by contrast, is tied to a concrete ML-NMR integration task and is proved in full.

A concrete task to keep in mind. Picture the setting the whole appendix serves. An aggregate-data (AgD) study arm reports an event count and a summary of its patients, say a mean and variance of age, but no individual records. The model has a link function \(g\), and to predict this arm’s event probability the model must average the individual probability \(g^{-1}(\eta)\) over the arm’s covariate distribution. The three regimes below are exactly the three ways that average can be computed, and each is the job of one group of functions in this appendix.

Function or object Section Feeds (chapter, result) What it does for the average
Normal MGF Section D.2 Chapter 20, Theorem 20.4 (catalogue H.6) gives the log-link average in exact closed form
Error function \(\operatorname{erf}\), \(\operatorname{erfc}\) Section D.3 Chapter 22, Theorem 22.6 and Theorem 22.4 how software actually computes \(\Phi\) and \(\Phi^{-1}\) for every Sobol’ node
Owen’s \(T\) Section D.4 Chapter 20 probit aggregation Proposition 20.1 supplies the probit second moment \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)^2]\)
One- and two-probit approximation Section D.5 Chapter 22 logit aggregation Theorem 20.5 a fast explicit approximation to the logit average
Radical inverse, Sobol’ construction Section D.6 Chapters 22 and 25, Theorem 22.6 builds the low-discrepancy points for the general numerical average

Read the table as a decision tree for the averaging task: the log link is a closed form (the MGF); the probit link has a closed form for the first moment and an Owen’s \(T\) closed form for the second; the logit link has no closed form but an accurate explicit probit approximation; and every remaining case, including the survival and ordinal likelihood kernels of Chapter 25, is handled by Sobol’ quasi-Monte Carlo, whose points are built from the radical inverse and pushed onto the covariate scale through \(\Phi^{-1}\), that is, through \(\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\). The sections are ordered from the easiest regime to the most general, and they rise in difficulty: the MGF section is short and gentle, the error-function section is moderate, Owen’s \(T\) is the hardest section and carries the Plackett partial differential equation, and the Sobol’ section is moderate but dense with quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) vocabulary. A reader who wants only the applied picture can take the closed forms and the worked examples from each section and leave the digital-net algebra of Section D.6 to software.

D.2 The moment generating function of the normal

The moment generating function (MGF) of Definition 4.7 turns the average of an exponential into an algebraic expression. Because the log link sends a linear predictor \(\eta\) to the mean \(e^{\eta}\), and because a linear predictor in jointly normal covariates is itself normal, the aggregate mean on the log scale is the expectation of an exponential of a normal, that is, a normal MGF evaluated at \(t=1\). This is the one nonlinear link for which ML-NMR has an exact closed form, and the MGF is the reason.

The name “moment generating” comes from a fact we do not actually use but which explains the terminology: differentiating \(M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\) a total of \(k\) times and setting \(t=0\) returns the \(k\)-th moment \(\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]\), because each derivative brings down one factor of \(X\). We exploit the MGF for a different reason. The log link sets the mean equal to \(e^{\eta}\), so an aggregate mean on the log scale is literally \(\mathbb{E}[e^{W}]\) for the (normal) linear predictor \(W\); that is the MGF evaluated at \(t=1\), not a derivative at \(t=0\). This is why the log link, alone among the common nonlinear links, yields a closed form: its inverse link is the exponential, and the exponential of a normal is exactly what the normal MGF integrates.

Theorem D.1 (Moment generating function of the normal) Let \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\) be univariate normal. Then the MGF of \(X\) is finite for every \(t\in\mathbb{R}\) and \[ M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]=\exp\!\Big(\mu t+\tfrac12\sigma^{2}t^{2}\Big). \tag{D.1}\] More generally, let \(\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) be \(p\)-variate normal with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succeq 0\) (positive semidefinite). Then for every \(\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\), \[ M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mathbf{t}^\top\mathbf{X}}\big] =\exp\!\Big(\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}+\tfrac12\,\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{t}\Big). \tag{D.2}\]

Proof. Univariate case. Fix \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). Writing the density of \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\) and collecting the exponent, \[ \mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big] =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\,dx =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}tx}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\,dx . \] Complete the square in \(x\). The numerator of the exponent is \[ (x-\mu)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}tx =\big(x-(\mu+\sigma^{2}t)\big)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}t\mu-\sigma^{4}t^{2}, \] which one verifies by expanding the completed square, \(\big(x-(\mu+\sigma^{2}t)\big)^{2}=x^{2}-2\mu x-2\sigma^{2}tx+\mu^{2}+2\mu\sigma^{2}t+\sigma^{4}t^{2}\): then subtracting the two constants \(2\sigma^{2}t\mu\) and \(\sigma^{4}t^{2}\) cancels the final two terms and leaves \(x^{2}-2\mu x-2\sigma^{2}tx+\mu^{2}=(x-\mu)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}tx\), the left side. Substituting and factoring out the constant terms, \[ \mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big] =e^{\mu t+\frac12\sigma^{2}t^{2}}\cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{(x-(\mu+\sigma^{2}t))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\,dx =e^{\mu t+\frac12\sigma^{2}t^{2}}, \] the remaining integral being that of a \(\mathcal{N}(\mu+\sigma^{2}t,\sigma^{2})\) density, which equals \(1\). The expectation is finite for every \(t\) because the integrand is a positive constant times a Gaussian density. This is Equation D.1.

Multivariate case. The law \(\mathcal{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) is defined in Definition 4.13 through exactly the MGF Equation D.2, so the statement is the consistency of that definition, which we reprove for reference. Let \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) be the symmetric positive semidefinite square root of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) (Theorem 3.2), let \(\mathbf{Z}=(Z_1,\dots,Z_p)^\top\) have independent standard normal entries, and set \(\mathbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Z}\), which has mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and, since \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) is symmetric with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}I_p\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). By independence and the univariate MGF \(\mathbb{E}[e^{sZ_i}]=e^{s^{2}/2}\) from Equation D.1, \[ \mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mathbf{t}^\top\mathbf{X}}\big] =e^{\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t})_i Z_i}\big] =e^{\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}}\exp\!\Big(\tfrac12\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t}\big\|_2^{2}\Big) =\exp\!\Big(\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}+\tfrac12\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}\Big), \] using \(\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t}\|_2^{2}=\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}\). This is Equation D.2, identical to Equation 4.13. \(\square\)

Reader’s translation. Equation D.1 says the average of \(e^{tX}\) over a normal \(X\) is itself an exponential, with the variance entering through the quadratic \(\tfrac12\sigma^{2}t^{2}\). That single formula is the entire reason the log-link aggregate mean has a closed form; the next corollary reads it off at \(t=1\).

The aggregation payoff is immediate. An aggregate mean on the log scale is the expectation of an exponential of a linear predictor; when the covariates are jointly normal the linear predictor is normal, and Equation D.1 evaluates the expectation at \(t=1\).

Corollary D.1 (Closed-form log-link aggregation) Let the inverse link be \(g^{-1}(z)=e^{z}\) (the log link) and let the covariates in study \(j\) be jointly normal, \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)\). Write \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_1+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}\) for the total slope on arm \(k\) and \(W=\eta_{jk}(X)=\mu_j+\gamma_k+X^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}\) for the linear predictor. Then \(W\sim\mathcal{N}(m,v)\) with \[ m=\mu_j+\gamma_k+\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}, \qquad v=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}, \] and the aggregate mean is \[ \theta_{\bullet jk}=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{W}\big]=M_W(1)=\exp\!\Big(m+\tfrac12 v\Big) =\theta^{\mathrm{naive}}_{\bullet jk}\cdot e^{\,v/2}, \qquad \theta^{\mathrm{naive}}_{\bullet jk}=e^{m}. \tag{D.3}\] This is the closed form Equation 20.12 of Theorem 20.4; the multiplicative correction \(e^{v/2}\ge 1\) is the exact downward bias of the plug-in mean, with equality if and only if \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}=\mathbf{0}\).

Proof. The linear predictor \(W\) is an affine function of the normal vector \(X\), hence univariate normal by the affine-image theorem Theorem 4.14, with the stated mean \(m\) and variance \(v=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}\). The aggregate mean of Definition 20.2 is \(\theta_{\bullet jk}=\mathbb{E}[g^{-1}(W)]=\mathbb{E}[e^{W}]\), which is \(M_W(1)\) by Definition 4.7. Evaluating Equation D.1 at \(t=1\) gives \(M_W(1)=\exp(m+\tfrac12 v)\). Finally \(e^{m+v/2}=e^{m}e^{v/2}=\theta^{\mathrm{naive}}_{\bullet jk}e^{v/2}\), and \(v\ge 0\) because \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\succeq 0\) (Theorem 4.9), with \(v=0\) exactly when \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}\) lies in the null space of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\). \(\square\)

Example D.1 (A multivariate log-link aggregate by hand) Take two covariates, \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)\) with \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j=(2,1)^\top\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), total slope \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}=(0.4,0.3)^\top\), and intercept-plus-treatment \(\mu_j+\gamma_k=-1\). The mean linear predictor is \(m=-1+\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}=-1+(2)(0.4)+(1)(0.3)=0.1\). The variance is the quadratic form \[ v=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k} =(0.4,0.3)\begin{pmatrix}0.55\\0.80\end{pmatrix}=(0.4)(0.55)+(0.3)(0.80)=0.46, \] where \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{tot},k}=(0.55,0.80)^\top\). By Equation D.3 the exact aggregate rate is \(\theta_{\bullet jk}=e^{0.1+0.23}=e^{0.33}=1.3910\), against the plug-in \(\theta^{\mathrm{naive}}_{\bullet jk}=e^{0.1}=1.1052\). The covariance off-diagonal enters only through \(v\): the same margins with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\) diagonal would give \(v=(0.16)(1)+(0.09)(2)=0.34\) and \(\theta_{\bullet jk}=e^{0.27}=1.3100\), so the positive covariance inflates the aggregate by the factor \(e^{(0.46-0.34)/2}=e^{0.06}=1.0618\). The MGF makes the dependence on the full covariance matrix exact and explicit. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

D.3 The error function

The normal distribution function has no elementary antiderivative, so numerical analysis works with a rescaled, standardized version of it called the error function. Software computes \(\Phi\) and \(\Phi^{-1}\) through \(\operatorname{erf}\) and \(\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\), and the series and asymptotic expansions of the error function are the practical tools for evaluating tail probabilities and normal quantiles.

This is not decorative. In the Chapter 22 integration pipeline every Sobol’ node is a point \(u\in(0,1)\) that must be pushed onto the covariate scale through the normal quantile \(\Phi^{-1}(u)\) (the inverse-CDF transform of Theorem 22.4), and \(\Phi^{-1}\) is computed as \(\sqrt{2}\,\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(2u-1)\). Every normal probability the fitted model reports, including the small tail probabilities that arise for rare events, is computed as an \(\operatorname{erf}\) or \(\operatorname{erfc}\) value. Stable evaluation of these functions is therefore part of what makes the integration numerically reliable, which is why they earn a section of their own rather than hiding inside a call to \(\Phi\).

Definition D.1 (Error function and complementary error function) The error function is \[ \operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}, \tag{D.4}\] and the complementary error function is \(\operatorname{erfc}(x)=1-\operatorname{erf}(x)=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader’s translation. The error function is the normal distribution function in disguise: it integrates the same Gaussian bump \(e^{-t^{2}}\), but centered at \(0\), scaled so that \(\operatorname{erf}(\pm\infty)=\pm1\), and measured from the center outward. The complement \(\operatorname{erfc}=1-\operatorname{erf}\) is what you use when the interesting probability is a small tail, because it is computed directly instead of by subtracting two nearly equal numbers.

Proposition D.1 (The error function and the normal distribution function) The error function is odd, \(\operatorname{erf}(-x)=-\operatorname{erf}(x)\), with \(\operatorname{erf}(0)=0\) and \(\operatorname{erf}(\pm\infty)=\pm 1\). It is related to the standard normal distribution function by \[ \Phi(x)=\frac12\Big(1+\operatorname{erf}\!\big(x/\sqrt{2}\big)\Big) =\frac12\operatorname{erfc}\!\big(-x/\sqrt{2}\big), \qquad \operatorname{erf}(x)=2\Phi\!\big(x\sqrt{2}\big)-1, \tag{D.5}\] and the quantile functions are related by \[ \Phi^{-1}(p)=\sqrt{2}\,\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(2p-1),\qquad p\in(0,1). \tag{D.6}\] The densities satisfy \(\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\) and \(\operatorname{erf}'(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-x^{2}}\), consistent with Equation D.5 by the chain rule.

Proof. Oddness is immediate from Equation D.4, since \(t\mapsto e^{-t^{2}}\) is even, so reversing the sign of the upper limit reverses the sign of the integral; \(\operatorname{erf}(0)=0\) is the empty integral, and \(\operatorname{erf}(+\infty)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2}\,dt=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}=1\) by the Gaussian normalization, with \(\operatorname{erf}(-\infty)=-1\) by oddness.

For Equation D.5, start from \(\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(u)\,du=\frac12+\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du\), the constant \(\frac12\) being \(\Phi(0)\) by the symmetry of \(\phi\). Substitute \(u=\sqrt{2}\,t\), so \(du=\sqrt{2}\,dt\) and the upper limit becomes \(x/\sqrt{2}\): \[ \Phi(x)=\frac12+\int_{0}^{x/\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}}\sqrt{2}\,dt =\frac12+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x/\sqrt{2}}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt =\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{erf}\!\big(x/\sqrt{2}\big), \] using \(\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}=\frac12\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\) to recognize Equation D.4. This is the first equality of Equation D.5; the second is \(\operatorname{erf}=1-\operatorname{erfc}\) together with oddness, and inverting \(p=\frac12(1+\operatorname{erf}(x/\sqrt2))\) for \(x\) gives Equation D.6. The density identity \(\operatorname{erf}'(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-x^{2}}\) is the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to Equation D.4, and differentiating Equation D.5 reproduces \(\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\cdot\frac12\operatorname{erf}'(x/\sqrt2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\). \(\square\)

Remark. What the error function does in health statistics. Every normal probability a health technology assessment (HTA) reports is an error-function value in disguise. Three tasks recur.

Health-statistics task Function used
a normal \(p\)-value or a confidence-limit tail \(\Phi\), \(\operatorname{erfc}\)
turning a Sobol’ uniform \(u\) into a normal covariate \(\Phi^{-1}\), hence \(\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\)
avoiding numerical underflow in an extreme tail \(\operatorname{erfc}\) or \(\operatorname{erfcx}\)

For a concrete instance, the familiar two-sided \(95\%\) cutoff \(1.96\) satisfies, by Equation D.5, \(\Phi(1.96)=\tfrac12\big(1+\operatorname{erf}(1.96/\sqrt2)\big)=\tfrac12\big(1+\operatorname{erf}(1.3859)\big)=\tfrac12(1+0.9500)=0.9750\), and the upper tail is \(1-\Phi(1.96)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(1.3859)=\tfrac12(0.0500)=0.0250\). The tail form earns its keep far out: \(1-\Phi(10)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(10/\sqrt2)\approx 7.6\times10^{-24}\) is returned accurately by \(\operatorname{erfc}\), whereas forming \(\Phi(10)\approx 1\) first and subtracting would return \(0\) by cancellation. This is the sense in which the tail-safe functions are part of the pipeline, not an ornament. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

For evaluation one needs a convergent series near the origin and an asymptotic expansion in the tail. Both follow from Equation D.4 by elementary calculus.

Proposition D.2 (Series and tail expansions) The error function has the everywhere-convergent Maclaurin series \[ \operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}x^{2n+1}}{n!\,(2n+1)} =\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(x-\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\frac{x^{5}}{10}-\frac{x^{7}}{42}+\cdots\Big), \tag{D.7}\] and the complementary error function has the asymptotic expansion, as \(x\to+\infty\), \[ \operatorname{erfc}(x)=\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{x\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}+\frac{3}{4x^{4}}-\cdots\Big), \tag{D.8}\] which translates, through Equation D.5, into the normal-tail statement (the Mills ratio is the tail-to-density ratio \((1-\Phi(x))/\phi(x)\)) \(1-\Phi(x)=\phi(x)\big(\tfrac{1}{x}-\tfrac{1}{x^{3}}+\tfrac{3}{x^{5}}-\cdots\big)\) as \(x\to+\infty\), so in particular \(1-\Phi(x)\sim\phi(x)/x\), where the symbol \(\sim\) means the ratio of the two sides tends to \(1\).

Proof. The series Equation D.7 is the termwise integration of the everywhere-convergent power series \(e^{-t^{2}}=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^{n}t^{2n}/n!\), legitimate because the series converges uniformly on every bounded interval; integrating \(t^{2n}\) from \(0\) to \(x\) gives \(x^{2n+1}/(2n+1)\) and the factor \(\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\) from Equation D.4.

For Equation D.8, integrate by parts in \(\operatorname{erfc}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-t^{2}}\,dt\), writing \(e^{-t^{2}}=\frac{1}{-2t}\,\frac{d}{dt}e^{-t^{2}}\): \[ \int_x^\infty e^{-t^{2}}\,dt =\Big[-\frac{e^{-t^{2}}}{2t}\Big]_x^\infty-\int_x^\infty\frac{e^{-t^{2}}}{2t^{2}}\,dt =\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{2x}-\frac12\int_x^\infty\frac{e^{-t^{2}}}{t^{2}}\,dt . \] Iterating the same step on the remainder integral generates the alternating series in inverse powers of \(x\); the expansion is asymptotic rather than convergent because the integrated-by-parts remainder, after \(N\) steps, is bounded by a constant times \(e^{-x^{2}}x^{-(2N+1)}\), which is smaller than the last retained term but does not tend to zero as \(N\to\infty\) at fixed \(x\). Multiplying by \(\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\) gives Equation D.8. The Mills ratio statement is Equation D.8 rewritten through \(1-\Phi(x)=\frac12\operatorname{erfc}(x/\sqrt2)\) and \(\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\): substituting \(x\mapsto x/\sqrt2\) and simplifying \(\frac12\cdot\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{(x/\sqrt2)\sqrt\pi}=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}=\phi(x)/x\) identifies the leading term, and the subsequent coefficients follow likewise. \(\square\)

Remark. Convergent versus asymptotic, and why the tail series is still useful. The two expansions of Proposition D.2 behave oppositely. The Maclaurin series Equation D.7 is convergent: fix \(x\), keep adding terms, and the partial sums approach \(\operatorname{erf}(x)\) without limit, so more terms always mean more accuracy. The tail expansion Equation D.8 is asymptotic: for a fixed large \(x\) the terms first shrink, reach a smallest term, and then grow, so the series diverges if pushed too far. One uses it by stopping at the smallest term, which for large \(x\) already gives extraordinary accuracy. The practical rule is: near the origin use the convergent series, in the tail truncate the asymptotic series at its smallest term. This is exactly how library routines for \(\Phi\) and \(\operatorname{erfc}\) switch regimes. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Why software standardizes on \(\operatorname{erf}\). The change of variable \(u=\sqrt2\,t\) that produced Equation D.5 is the only difference between \(\Phi\) and \(\operatorname{erf}\): they are the same function up to an affine rescaling of the argument and the range. Numerical libraries implement \(\operatorname{erf}\), \(\operatorname{erfc}\), and \(\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\) as primitives, often with the scaled complement \(\operatorname{erfcx}(x)=e^{x^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(x)\) to avoid overflow in the tail, and obtain \(\Phi\), \(1-\Phi\), and \(\Phi^{-1}\) by Equation D.5 and Equation D.6. Every appearance of \(\Phi\) or \(\Phi^{-1}\) in the ML-NMR pipeline of Theorem 22.6, in the Gaussian copula Equation 22.16, and in the inverse-CDF transform Theorem 22.4, is computed this way. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

D.4 Owen’s \(T\) function

The error function evaluates one-dimensional normal integrals. The two-dimensional analogue, the bivariate normal probability, has no reduction to a single error function; the irreducible new ingredient is Owen’s \(T\) function (Owen (1956)). We define it, give its probabilistic meaning, prove the bivariate normal probability identity that motivated Owen’s tables, and then prove the identity for the expected squared normal distribution function that probit-link aggregation needs.

Before the formal definition, here is the picture and the purpose, because both are easy to lose once the integral appears.

The picture. Owen’s \(T\) measures the probability that a random point lands in a wedge. Take two independent standard normal draws \(X\) and \(Y\) and plot the point \((X,Y)\) in the plane. Owen’s \(T(h,a)\), for \(h\ge 0\) and \(a\ge 0\), is the chance that this point falls into the wedge bounded on the left by the vertical line at \(h\) and above by the ray \(Y=aX\) through the origin, the region \(\{X>h,\ 0<Y<aX\}\). So \(h\) sets a vertical cutoff for the first variable and \(a\) sets the slope of the sloped edge for the second: larger \(h\) pushes the wedge to the right and shrinks it, larger \(a\) tilts the sloped edge upward and enlarges it. The integral in the definition below is simply the analytic formula for the normal-probability content of that wedge.

The purpose. We need this wedge because of probit aggregation. When the link is the probit, the aggregate binomial likelihood of an AgD arm depends on the response through both its mean and its variance, and a Bernoulli response with success probability \(\Phi(\eta)\) has variance \(\Phi(\eta)(1-\Phi(\eta))\). Averaging over the covariate distribution, the aggregate needs both the first moment \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)]\), which Proposition 20.1 gives as a single \(\Phi\), and the second moment \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)^{2}]\). That second moment turns out to be a bivariate normal probability, and Owen’s \(T\) is precisely the function that reduces a bivariate normal probability to a one-dimensional integral. This is why the error function, which handled one-dimensional normal integrals, cannot finish the job alone.

One analytic signpost. Several proofs below differentiate under the integral sign, meaning they move a derivative inside an integral. This is a genuine theorem, not a formality: it is licensed whenever the integrand’s derivative is bounded by a fixed integrable function (a dominating envelope), which we display at each use. A reader can treat each such step as permitted once the envelope is shown.

Definition D.2 (Owen’s \(T\) function) For \(h\in\mathbb{R}\) and \(a\in\mathbb{R}\), Owen’s \(T\) function is \[ T(h,a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 h^{2}(1+x^{2})\big)}{1+x^{2}}\,dx . \tag{D.9}\] \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Three structural facts are read directly off Equation D.9: \(T(h,0)=0\) for all \(h\); \(T(h,a)=T(-h,a)\), because the integrand depends on \(h\) only through \(h^{2}\), so \(T(h,a)=T(|h|,a)\); and \(T(h,-a)=-T(h,a)\) by reversing the limits. The next lemma gives the probabilistic content.

Lemma D.1 (Integral and probabilistic representation) For all \(h\in\mathbb{R}\) and \(a\ge 0\), \[ T(h,a)=\int_{h}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,\Big(\Phi(ax)-\tfrac12\Big)\,dx . \tag{D.10}\] If moreover \(h\ge 0\), then \(T(h,a)\) is the probability that two independent standard normals \(X,Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) fall in the wedge \(\{X>h,\ 0<Y<aX\}\): \[ T(h,a)=P\big(X>h,\ 0<Y<aX\big),\qquad h\ge 0,\ a\ge 0 . \tag{D.11}\]

Proof. Both sides of Equation D.10 vanish at \(a=0\): the left by \(T(h,0)=0\), the right because \(\Phi(0)-\frac12=0\). It therefore suffices to show their derivatives in \(a\) agree for \(a>0\). The left side, by the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to the upper limit of Equation D.9, \[ \partial_a T(h,a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{e^{-\frac12 h^{2}(1+a^{2})}}{1+a^{2}} . \tag{D.12}\] For the right side, differentiate under the integral (the integrand’s \(a\)-derivative \(\phi(x)\,x\,\phi(ax)\) is dominated by the integrable \(|x|\phi(x)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\)): \[ \partial_a\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\big(\Phi(ax)-\tfrac12\big)\,dx =\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\,x\,\phi(ax)\,dx =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_h^\infty x\,e^{-\frac12 x^{2}(1+a^{2})}\,dx . \] The last integral is elementary: an antiderivative of \(x\,e^{-\frac12 x^{2}(1+a^{2})}\) is \(-\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}e^{-\frac12 x^{2}(1+a^{2})}\), which vanishes at \(+\infty\) and equals \(-\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}e^{-\frac12 h^{2}(1+a^{2})}\) at \(x=h\), for every real \(h\). Hence the integral equals \(\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}e^{-\frac12 h^{2}(1+a^{2})}\), and the derivative of the right side is \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-\frac12 h^{2}(1+a^{2})}}{1+a^{2}}\), matching Equation D.12. The two sides of Equation D.10 agree at \(a=0\) and have equal derivatives, so they are equal for all \(a\ge 0\).

For Equation D.11 with \(h\ge 0\), observe that for \(x>h\ge 0\) the inner probability \(P(0<Y<ax)=\Phi(ax)-\Phi(0)=\Phi(ax)-\frac12\) is the genuine probability of the interval \((0,ax)\), so \[ P(X>h,\ 0<Y<aX)=\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\,P(0<Y<ax)\,dx=\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\big(\Phi(ax)-\tfrac12\big)\,dx =T(h,a) \] by Equation D.10. (For \(h<0\) the same integral Equation D.10 remains valid as an analytic identity, but its integrand is negative on \((h,0)\) and it is no longer the probability of a wedge.) \(\square\)

Two special values follow at once and will be needed below.

Lemma D.2 (Special values of Owen’s \(T\)) For every \(h\in\mathbb{R}\), \[ T(h,1)=\tfrac12\,\Phi(h)\big(1-\Phi(h)\big), \qquad \lim_{a\to\infty}T(h,a)=\tfrac12\big(1-\Phi(|h|)\big). \tag{D.13}\]

Proof. Set \(a=1\) in Equation D.10: \(T(h,1)=\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\big(\Phi(x)-\tfrac12\big)\,dx =\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\Phi(x)\,dx-\tfrac12\int_h^\infty\phi(x)\,dx\). In the first integral, \(\frac{d}{dx}\big[\tfrac12\Phi(x)^{2}\big]=\Phi(x)\phi(x)\), so \(\int_h^\infty\phi\Phi=\tfrac12\Phi(x)^{2}\big|_h^\infty=\tfrac12\big(1-\Phi(h)^{2}\big)\); the second is \(\tfrac12\big(1-\Phi(h)\big)\). Subtracting, \(T(h,1)=\tfrac12(1-\Phi(h)^{2})-\tfrac12(1-\Phi(h))=\tfrac12\Phi(h)\big(1-\Phi(h)\big)\). For the limit, use \(T(h,a)=T(|h|,a)\) and Equation D.11 with \(|h|\ge 0\): as \(a\to\infty\) the wedge \(\{X>|h|,0<Y<aX\}\) increases to \(\{X>|h|,Y>0\}\), whose probability is \(P(X>|h|)P(Y>0)=\big(1-\Phi(|h|)\big)\cdot\tfrac12\) by independence. \(\square\)

The bridge from Owen’s \(T\) to bivariate normal probabilities is a classical partial differential equation for the bivariate normal density, due to Plackett: the density’s derivative in the correlation equals its mixed second derivative in the two coordinates.

Why differentiate in the correlation \(\rho\)? Because it lets us build the bivariate probability we want out of a case we already know. At \(\rho=0\) the two coordinates are independent and the joint probability factorizes into \(\Phi(\lambda)^{2}\); at the target \(\rho\) it is the quantity we are after. Sliding \(\rho\) continuously from \(0\) to its target and accumulating the change reduces the unknown probability to an integral of its \(\rho\)-derivative. Plackett’s identity Equation D.14 is what makes this tractable: it says that \(\rho\)-derivative is nothing but the corner value of the density, a quantity we can write down explicitly. The lemma is the engine; the theorem after it turns the crank.

Lemma D.3 (The correlation heat equation for the bivariate normal) Let \(f(x,y;\rho)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{x^{2}-2\rho xy+y^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\Big)\) be the standard bivariate normal density with correlation \(\rho\in(-1,1)\). Then \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}=\frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x\,\partial y}. \tag{D.14}\]

Proof. Write \(D=1-\rho^{2}\), \(Q=x^{2}-2\rho xy+y^{2}\), and \(g=e^{-Q/(2D)}\), so \(f=(2\pi)^{-1}D^{-1/2}g\). For the right side, \(\partial_x g=g\cdot\big(-(x-\rho y)/D\big)\) and then \[ \partial_y\partial_x g =g\Big(-\tfrac{y-\rho x}{D}\Big)\Big(-\tfrac{x-\rho y}{D}\Big)+g\cdot\tfrac{\rho}{D} =g\Big[\tfrac{(x-\rho y)(y-\rho x)}{D^{2}}+\tfrac{\rho}{D}\Big], \] so \(\partial_x\partial_y f=(2\pi)^{-1}D^{-1/2}g\big[(x-\rho y)(y-\rho x)/D^{2}+\rho/D\big]\). For the left side, \(\partial_\rho D^{-1/2}=\rho D^{-3/2}\) and, since \(\partial_\rho Q=-2xy\), \(\partial_\rho g=g\,\partial_\rho(-Q/2D)=g\big(xy/D-\rho Q/D^{2}\big)\), whence \(\partial_\rho f=(2\pi)^{-1}D^{-1/2}g\big[\rho/D+xy/D-\rho Q/D^{2}\big]\). The two expressions agree if and only if \(xyD-\rho Q=(x-\rho y)(y-\rho x)\). Expanding both sides, \[ (x-\rho y)(y-\rho x)=(1+\rho^{2})xy-\rho(x^{2}+y^{2}),\qquad xyD-\rho Q=(1-\rho^{2})xy-\rho(x^{2}-2\rho xy+y^{2})=(1+\rho^{2})xy-\rho(x^{2}+y^{2}), \] which are equal. \(\square\)

We can now prove the bivariate normal probability identity on the diagonal, which is exactly the case probit aggregation requires.

Theorem D.2 (Bivariate normal probability via Owen’s \(T\)) Let \((V_1,V_2)\) be standard bivariate normal with correlation \(\rho\in(-1,1)\), and let \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\). Then \[ P\big(V_1\le\lambda,\ V_2\le\lambda\big) =\Phi(\lambda)-2\,T\!\Big(\lambda,\ \sqrt{\tfrac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}\Big). \tag{D.15}\]

Roadmap for the proof. The strategy is the deformation just described. Both sides are checked to agree at \(\rho=0\) (the independent base case, where the probability is \(\Phi(\lambda)^{2}\)), and then both sides are shown to have the same derivative in \(\rho\). Two functions that start equal and change at the same rate stay equal, so they coincide for every \(\rho\). The one technical point is a change of variable \(a(\rho)=\sqrt{(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\) that carries \(\rho\) into the second argument of \(T\); the checkpoint \(1+a(\rho)^{2}=2/(1+\rho)\) is what makes the algebra collapse.

Proof. Write \(\Psi(\rho)=P(V_1\le\lambda,V_2\le\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}f(x,y;\rho)\,dx\,dy\) for fixed \(\lambda\). Differentiating in \(\rho\) and using Equation D.14, \[ \Psi'(\rho)=\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}\partial_\rho f\,dx\,dy =\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}\partial_x\partial_y f\,dx\,dy =f(\lambda,\lambda;\rho), \] the double integral of the mixed derivative collapsing by the fundamental theorem of calculus in each variable, since \(f\) and its first derivatives vanish as either argument tends to \(-\infty\). Evaluating the density on the diagonal, with \(x^{2}-2\rho xy+y^{2}=2\lambda^{2}(1-\rho)\) at \(x=y=\lambda\) and \(1-\rho^{2}=(1-\rho)(1+\rho)\), \[ f(\lambda,\lambda;\rho)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2\lambda^{2}(1-\rho)}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\Big) =\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,e^{-\lambda^{2}/(1+\rho)} . \tag{D.16}\] At \(\rho=0\) the coordinates are independent, so \(\Psi(0)=\Phi(\lambda)^{2}\), and by Equation D.13 the base case can be written \(\Psi(0)=\Phi(\lambda)-2T(\lambda,1)\), since \(2T(\lambda,1)=\Phi(\lambda)(1-\Phi(\lambda))\) gives \(\Phi(\lambda)-2T(\lambda,1)=\Phi(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)=\Phi(\lambda)^2\). Define \(a(\rho)=\sqrt{(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\), a decreasing bijection from \((-1,1)\) onto \((0,\infty)\) with \(a(0)=1\). We claim \(G(\rho):=\Phi(\lambda)-2T(\lambda,a(\rho))\) has the same derivative as \(\Psi\) and the same value at \(\rho=0\), hence equals \(\Psi\) throughout.

The value at \(\rho=0\) matches by construction. For the derivative, by the chain rule and Equation D.12, \[ G'(\rho)=-2\,\partial_a T(\lambda,a)\big|_{a=a(\rho)}\,a'(\rho) =-2\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-\frac12\lambda^{2}(1+a(\rho)^{2})}}{1+a(\rho)^{2}}\cdot a'(\rho). \] Now \(1+a^{2}=1+\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}=\frac{2}{1+\rho}\), so \(\frac12\lambda^{2}(1+a^{2})=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\rho}\) and \(\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}=\frac{1+\rho}{2}\). Differentiating \(a(\rho)^{2}=(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)\) gives \(2a\,a'=\frac{-(1+\rho)-(1-\rho)}{(1+\rho)^{2}}=\frac{-2}{(1+\rho)^{2}}\), so \(a'(\rho)=\frac{-1}{a(1+\rho)^{2}}\) with \(a=\sqrt{(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\), that is \(a'(\rho)=\frac{-1}{(1+\rho)^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}}=\frac{-1}{(1+\rho)^{3/2}(1-\rho)^{1/2}}=\frac{-1}{(1+\rho)\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\). Substituting, \[ G'(\rho)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,e^{-\lambda^{2}/(1+\rho)}\cdot\frac{1+\rho}{2}\cdot\frac{-1}{(1+\rho)\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}} =\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,e^{-\lambda^{2}/(1+\rho)}=f(\lambda,\lambda;\rho)=\Psi'(\rho), \] matching Equation D.16. Since \(G\) and \(\Psi\) agree at \(\rho=0\) and have equal derivatives on \((-1,1)\), they are equal, which is Equation D.15. \(\square\)

Remark. Where this identity is used, and where it is not. Equation D.15 is the workhorse behind the probit second moment of the next theorem, and it is the right tool whenever one needs an analytic bivariate normal probability, for instance to check a probit-link fit by hand. It is not needed to generate the quasi-Monte Carlo covariate nodes of Chapter 22: sampling from a Gaussian copula requires only the marginal quantile \(\Phi^{-1}\), not the bivariate distribution function, so ML-NMR fitting never evaluates the full copula CDF. The identity explains and checks the bivariate object; it does not sit on the sampling path. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. The general Owen reduction. Off the diagonal, the same differentiate-in-\(\rho\) argument yields Owen’s general formula for the standard bivariate normal distribution function \(\Phi_2(h,k;\rho)=P(V_1\le h,V_2\le k)\), \[ \Phi_2(h,k;\rho)=\tfrac12\Phi(h)+\tfrac12\Phi(k) -T\!\Big(h,\tfrac{k-\rho h}{h\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\Big) -T\!\Big(k,\tfrac{h-\rho k}{k\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\Big)-\varepsilon, \tag{D.17}\] where \(\varepsilon=\tfrac12\) when exactly one of \(h,k\) is negative and \(\varepsilon=0\) otherwise; this is the identity that Owen’s tables (Owen (1956)) tabulate, and Equation D.15 is its specialization to \(h=k=\lambda\), where the two \(T\)-terms coincide and \(\varepsilon=0\). We use only the diagonal case, which we have proved in full; the general formula is stated for reference. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The probit-link aggregate likelihood needs not only the first moment of a probit response, the closed form \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(a+bZ)]=\Phi(a/\sqrt{1+b^{2}})\) of Proposition 20.1, but also its second moment, which Owen’s \(T\) supplies.

Theorem D.3 (Expected squared normal distribution function) Let \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and let \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\). With \(\lambda=a/\sqrt{1+b^{2}}\), \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(a+bZ)^{2}\big] =\Phi(\lambda)-2\,T\!\Big(\lambda,\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2b^{2}}}\Big). \tag{D.18}\] Consequently the variance contribution \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(a+bZ)(1-\Phi(a+bZ))]\), the response-scale Bernoulli variance averaged over the linear predictor, equals \(\Phi(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}[\Phi(a+bZ)^{2}]=2\,T\big(\lambda,1/\sqrt{1+2b^{2}}\big)\).

Roadmap for the proof. The key move is to read a squared probability as a joint probability. Since \(\Phi(a+bZ)\) is itself the probability that a fresh standard normal \(U\) falls below \(a+bZ\), its square is the probability that two independent such draws \(U_1,U_2\) both fall below \(a+bZ\). Both comparisons share the same random threshold \(a+bZ\), so once we average over \(Z\) the two draws become correlated through their common dependence on \(Z\). That turns \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(a+bZ)^{2}]\) into a bivariate normal probability, which Theorem D.2 has already evaluated. In three words: square, condition, correlate.

Proof. Let \(U_1,U_2,Z\) be independent standard normals. For fixed \(Z\), the definition of \(\Phi\) gives \(\Phi(a+bZ)=P(U_i\le a+bZ\mid Z)\) for each \(i\), and by conditional independence of \(U_1,U_2\), \[ \Phi(a+bZ)^{2}=P\big(U_1\le a+bZ,\ U_2\le a+bZ\mid Z\big). \] Taking expectations over \(Z\) and rearranging the inequalities, \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(a+bZ)^{2}\big]=P\big(U_1-bZ\le a,\ U_2-bZ\le a\big). \] The pair \((W_1,W_2)=(U_1-bZ,U_2-bZ)\) is bivariate normal with mean \(\mathbf{0}\), equal variances \(\mathrm{Var}(W_i)=1+b^{2}\), and covariance \(\mathrm{Cov}(W_1,W_2)=b^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(Z)=b^{2}\), hence correlation \(\rho=b^{2}/(1+b^{2})\). Standardizing, \(V_i=W_i/\sqrt{1+b^{2}}\) are standard normal with correlation \(\rho\), and \(P(W_1\le a,W_2\le a)=P(V_1\le\lambda,V_2\le\lambda)\) with \(\lambda=a/\sqrt{1+b^{2}}\). By Theorem D.2, \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(a+bZ)^{2}\big]=\Phi(\lambda)-2\,T\!\Big(\lambda,\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}\Big). \] Finally \(\dfrac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}=\dfrac{1-\frac{b^{2}}{1+b^{2}}}{1+\frac{b^{2}}{1+b^{2}}}=\dfrac{1}{1+2b^{2}}\), so \(\sqrt{(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}=1/\sqrt{1+2b^{2}}\), giving Equation D.18. The variance statement follows by subtracting from the first moment \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(a+bZ)]=\Phi(\lambda)\) of Proposition 20.1. \(\square\)

Example D.2 (A probit second moment by hand) Take \(a=0.5\), \(b=1\), so a probit linear predictor \(\eta=0.5+Z\) with \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), that is \(\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0.5,1)\). The first moment is \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)]=\Phi(0.5/\sqrt{2})=\Phi(0.3536)=0.6382\) by Proposition 20.1. For the second moment, \(\lambda=0.5/\sqrt{2}=0.3536\) and \(1/\sqrt{1+2}=1/\sqrt3=0.5774\), so by Equation D.18 \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(\eta)^{2}\big]=\Phi(0.3536)-2\,T(0.3536,\,0.5774)=0.6382-2(0.0778)=0.4826 . \] The averaged Bernoulli variance is then \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)]-\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)^{2}]=0.6382-0.4826=0.1556\), and the variance of the probit response itself is \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\eta)]^{2}=0.4826-0.6382^{2}=0.4826-0.4073=0.0753\). Both numbers are exact closed forms in \(\Phi\) and \(T\); direct numerical integration of \(\int\Phi(0.5+z)^{2}\phi(z)\,dz\) returns \(0.4826\), confirming the identity. The Owen value itself is reproducible with standard tools: a Simpson’s rule on the definition integral \(T(h,a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a} e^{-\frac12 h^{2}(1+x^{2})}/(1+x^{2})\,dx\) over \([0,0.5774]\) returns \(0.0778\), as does the T.Owen function of the R package sn. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

D.5 Probit approximations to the logistic function

The logit aggregation integral \(J(m,\tau)=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{expit}(m+\sqrt\tau\,Z)]\) of Equation 20.14 has no elementary closed form (Theorem 20.5), whereas its probit sibling does, by the Gaussian probit integral Equation 16.10. This asymmetry invites a strategy: approximate the logistic inverse-link \(\mathrm{expit}\) by one or two probit terms, for each of which the aggregation integral collapses to a \(\Phi\), and obtain a fast explicit approximation to \(J\). We derive both the one-probit and the two-probit approximation.

The two approximations share an exact representation that makes their structure transparent. The standard logistic distribution is a scale mixture of normals: there is a probability density \(w(s)\) on \((0,\infty)\) with \(\mathrm{expit}(z)=\int_0^\infty\Phi(z/s)\,w(s)\,ds\). Approximating this mixture by a one-point or two-point rule, \(\mathrm{expit}(z)\approx\sum_{i}\omega_i\Phi(\kappa_i z)\) with \(\kappa_i=1/s_i\), is exactly the one-probit and two-probit construction. The mixture is motivation for the shape only, a positively weighted sum of probits; the density \(w(s)\) is never needed, and the numbers below are not read off \(w\) by quadrature but come from Taylor matching. We pin the nodes and weights not from \(w\) directly but from the equivalent and elementary requirement that the approximation match the Taylor expansion of \(\mathrm{expit}\) at the origin. The expansion, obtained from \(\mathrm{expit}(z)=\tfrac12\big(1+\tanh(z/2)\big)\) and the series \(\tanh(x)=x-\tfrac{x^{3}}{3}+\tfrac{2x^{5}}{15}-\cdots\), is \[ \mathrm{expit}(z)=\tfrac12+\tfrac{1}{4}z-\tfrac{1}{48}z^{3}+\tfrac{1}{480}z^{5}-\cdots, \tag{D.19}\] an odd function of \(z\) about the value \(\tfrac12\). The probit term has the matching odd expansion \(\Phi(\kappa z)-\tfrac12=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\big(\kappa z-\tfrac{(\kappa z)^{3}}{6}+\tfrac{(\kappa z)^{5}}{40}-\cdots\big)\), from Equation D.7 through Equation D.5.

Theorem D.4 (One-probit approximation and one-probit logit aggregation) Let \(\kappa=\sqrt{\pi/8}=0.62666\ldots\) Then \(\mathrm{expit}(z)\approx\Phi(\kappa z)\) is the unique one-probit approximation matching \(\mathrm{expit}\) in value, first derivative, and second derivative at \(z=0\); the constant \(\kappa\) is fixed by the slope condition \[ \kappa\,\phi(0)=\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=\mathrm{expit}'(0)=\frac14 . \tag{D.20}\] Substituting into the logit aggregation integral and applying the Gaussian probit integral Equation 16.10 gives the explicit one-probit aggregate \[ J(m,\tau)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{expit}(m+\sqrt\tau\,Z)\big] \approx\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\kappa\,m}{\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\tau}}\Big). \tag{D.21}\]

Proof. Both \(\mathrm{expit}(z)-\tfrac12\) and \(\Phi(\kappa z)-\tfrac12\) are odd, so they automatically agree in value at \(0\) (both \(\tfrac12\)) and in every even derivative (all zero); matching is therefore a condition on the odd derivatives. The first-derivative match requires \(\frac{d}{dz}\Phi(\kappa z)\big|_0=\kappa\phi(0)\) to equal \(\mathrm{expit}'(0)=\mathrm{expit}(0)(1-\mathrm{expit}(0))=\tfrac14\). Since \(\phi(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi}\), this is \(\kappa/\sqrt{2\pi}=\tfrac14\), that is \(\kappa=\sqrt{2\pi}/4=\sqrt{\pi/8}\), which is Equation D.20; the second-derivative match is the vacuous identity \(0=0\). For the aggregate, write \(\eta=m+\sqrt\tau\,Z\) with \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and substitute the approximation: \[ J(m,\tau)\approx\mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(\kappa\,\eta)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(\kappa m+\kappa\sqrt\tau\,Z)\big] =\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\kappa m}{\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\tau}}\Big), \] applying Equation 16.10 with \(a=\kappa m\), \(b=\kappa\sqrt\tau\), and \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), for which \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(a+bZ)]=\Phi(a/\sqrt{1+b^{2}})\). This is Equation D.21. \(\square\)

Remark. Accuracy and an alternative constant. The slope-matched one-probit approximation \(\mathrm{expit}(z)\approx\Phi(\sqrt{\pi/8}\,z)\) has maximum absolute error \(0.0177\) over all \(z\), attained in the shoulders near \(|z|\approx 2\). If instead one minimizes the maximum error over \(z\) rather than matching the slope, the optimal constant is \(\kappa^{\star}\approx 0.5876\), with maximum error \(0.0095\); this \(\kappa^\star\) is the value tabulated in the logistic-versus-normal literature. The slope-matched \(\kappa=\sqrt{\pi/8}\) is the natural choice for aggregation because it makes the approximation exact to second order at the center of the covariate distribution, where most of the integrating mass of Equation D.21 sits, and because it is the constant used in Theorem 20.5. The substitution is an approximation, not an identity; ML-NMR fits use exact numerical integration (Theorem 22.6), and the probit form serves as a fast initializer and analytic check. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

A single probit cannot reproduce the heavier tails of the logistic, which is the source of the residual \(0.0177\) error. A second probit term, with its own scale, corrects the next odd order.

Proposition D.3 (Two-probit approximation and two-probit logit aggregation) Seek \(\mathrm{expit}(z)\approx\omega_1\Phi(\kappa_1 z)+\omega_2\Phi(\kappa_2 z)\) with \(\omega_1+\omega_2=1\). Matching the Taylor coefficients Equation D.19 at orders \(1\), \(3\), and \(5\) gives the moment conditions \[ \sum_{i}\omega_i\kappa_i=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{4},\qquad \sum_{i}\omega_i\kappa_i^{3}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{8},\qquad \sum_{i}\omega_i\kappa_i^{5}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{12}, \tag{D.22}\] whose solution is \[ \omega_1=0.7371,\quad\kappa_1=0.5191,\qquad \omega_2=0.2629,\quad\kappa_2=0.9282 . \tag{D.23}\] The resulting approximation has maximum absolute error \(0.0040\), a fourfold improvement on the one-probit rule, and yields the explicit two-probit aggregate \[ J(m,\tau)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{2}\omega_i\,\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\kappa_i\,m}{\sqrt{1+\kappa_i^{2}\tau}}\Big). \tag{D.24}\]

Proof. With \(\omega_1+\omega_2=1\) the approximation is automatically exact in value at \(0\). Subtracting \(\tfrac12\) from both sides and equating the odd Taylor coefficients of \(\sum_i\omega_i\big(\Phi(\kappa_i z)-\tfrac12\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_i\omega_i\big(\kappa_i z-\tfrac{\kappa_i^{3}z^{3}}{6}+\tfrac{\kappa_i^{5}z^{5}}{40}-\cdots\big)\) with those of Equation D.19: the \(z^{1}\) coefficient gives \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_i\omega_i\kappa_i=\tfrac14\); the \(z^{3}\) coefficient gives \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_i\omega_i\kappa_i^{3}/6=\tfrac{1}{48}\); and the \(z^{5}\) coefficient gives \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_i\omega_i\kappa_i^{5}/40=\tfrac{1}{480}\). Clearing constants gives the three conditions Equation D.22. Together with \(\omega_2=1-\omega_1\) this is a system of three equations in the three unknowns \((\omega_1,\kappa_1,\kappa_2)\); solving it numerically yields Equation D.23. This is a numerical construction, not a hand derivation: the system is nonlinear, one solves it with a standard root finder (for example Newton’s method seeded near the one-probit constant \(\kappa=\sqrt{\pi/8}\)), and the real solution with \(0<\kappa_1<\kappa_2\) is unique up to swapping the two labels. Substituting back confirms the three moment equations to the displayed precision. The maximum-error claim is verified by evaluating \(|\mathrm{expit}(z)-\omega_1\Phi(\kappa_1 z)-\omega_2\Phi(\kappa_2 z)|\) on a fine grid, where the supremum is \(0.0040\). The aggregate Equation D.24 follows by linearity of expectation and applying Equation D.21 to each term: with the approximation in hand, \(J(m,\tau)\approx\sum_i\omega_i\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\kappa_i\eta)]=\sum_i\omega_i\Phi\big(\kappa_i m/\sqrt{1+\kappa_i^{2}\tau}\big)\). \(\square\)

Example D.3 (Approximating the worked logit integral of Chapter 22) The QMC worked example Example 22.3 evaluates \(J(0.5,1)=\int\mathrm{expit}(0.5+x)\phi(x)\,dx\), with high-accuracy reference value \(0.6020\). The one-probit aggregate Equation D.21 gives, with \(\kappa=0.62666\) and \(\kappa^{2}=0.39270\), \[ J(0.5,1)\approx\Phi\!\Big(\frac{0.62666\cdot 0.5}{\sqrt{1+0.39270}}\Big) =\Phi\!\Big(\frac{0.31333}{1.18014}\Big)=\Phi(0.26550)=0.6047, \] an error of \(+0.0027\). The two-probit aggregate Equation D.24 gives \[ J(0.5,1)\approx 0.7371\,\Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{0.5191\cdot0.5}{\sqrt{1+0.2695}}\Big) +0.2629\,\Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{0.9282\cdot0.5}{\sqrt{1+0.8616}}\Big) =0.7371\,\Phi(0.23035)+0.2629\,\Phi(0.34020)=0.6022, \] an error of \(+0.0002\), an order of magnitude smaller and indistinguishable from the QMC reference at the precision a health technology assessment reports. The two-probit closed form thus reproduces, with two evaluations of \(\Phi\), the integral that Section 22.11 computes with a Sobol’ net. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Remark. Two different error scales. The maximum errors quoted above, \(0.0177\) for one probit and \(0.0040\) for two, are worst-case errors in \(\mathrm{expit}(z)\) itself, pointwise on the probability scale and before any averaging. The error that actually matters for a fitted model is the error in the aggregate \(J(m,\tau)\) after integrating over the covariate distribution, and it is typically smaller, because the pointwise overshoots and undershoots partly cancel under the average. The worked example makes this concrete: the one-probit pointwise maximum is \(0.0177\), yet the one-probit aggregate error at \(J(0.5,1)\) is only \(0.0027\), and the two-probit aggregate error is \(0.0002\). One caveat runs the other way. The matching is done at the center \(z=0\), so the approximation is at its best for event probabilities near \(\tfrac12\) and least accurate in the shoulders, which is worth remembering when the linear predictor sits far in a tail. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

D.6 Sobol’ sequences and the radical inverse

The Sobol’ point set of Definition 22.3 is built coordinate by coordinate from a single arithmetic operation on the index \(n\): the radical inverse, which reflects the base-\(b\) digits of \(n\) about the radix point. We define it, prove the exact equidistribution that makes its one-dimensional version (the van der Corput sequence) a low-discrepancy sequence, and then give the digital construction of the full Sobol’ sequence through generating matrices over the field \(\mathbb{F}_2\), of which the radical inverse is the identity-matrix case.

Why quasi-Monte Carlo at all. The aggregation integrals of Part V are averages over a covariate distribution, and the honest way to compute a \(d\)-dimensional average with no closed form is to sample points and average the integrand. Independent random points (plain Monte Carlo) give a root-mean-square error of order \(N^{-1/2}\) in the number of points \(N\) (Theorem 22.2): to halve the error you must quadruple the sample. Sobol’ points are chosen deterministically to be spread out rather than random, and they achieve an error of order \((\log N)^{d}/N\) (Theorem 22.6), which for a fixed dimension beats \(N^{-1/2}\) for all large \(N\). In the low dimensions typical once ML-NMR reduces to a handful of effect-modifying covariates, this is often an order-of-magnitude gain at the same \(N\), which is why the model integrates with Sobol’ points and not random ones.

What to retain, and what software handles. The rest of this section proves why the construction works, and it uses the vocabulary of digital nets: the field \(\mathbb{F}_2=\{0,1\}\) with arithmetic modulo \(2\) (where addition is the exclusive-or, \(1\oplus1=0\)), generating matrices, direction numbers, and primitive polynomials. For applied ML-NMR work almost none of this needs to be carried in the head. What to retain is the applied picture: Sobol’ points fill the unit cube evenly, leaving no large empty region, so that after the inverse-CDF and copula transforms of Chapter 22 the covariate space is covered without gaps. The generating-matrix algebra below is implementation detail that established libraries, with the tabulated direction numbers of Joe and Kuo, handle once and for all. Read the proofs for understanding; you need not reimplement them. Finally, the same point set serves every Part V integrand without change: the integrand may be a mean (Chapter 20), a Bernoulli or binomial likelihood contribution, a censored survival likelihood \(h^{\delta}S\), or an ordinal category probability (Chapter 25). The dimension \(d\) is the number of integrated covariates, only the integrand changes, and the Sobol’ machinery is the same throughout.

Definition D.3 (Radical inverse and the van der Corput sequence) Fix an integer base \(b\ge 2\). Every nonnegative integer \(n\) has a unique finite base-\(b\) expansion \(n=\sum_{\ell\ge 0}a_\ell(n)\,b^{\ell}\) with digits \(a_\ell(n)\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}\). The radical inverse of \(n\) in base \(b\) is obtained by reflecting these digits about the radix point (the radix point is the base-\(b\) analogue of the decimal point, the marker separating the integer digits from the fractional digits), \[ \varphi_b(n)=\sum_{\ell\ge 0}a_\ell(n)\,b^{-\ell-1}\in[0,1). \tag{D.25}\] The van der Corput sequence in base \(b\) is \(\big(\varphi_b(n)\big)_{n\ge 0}\), and its base-\(2\) instance is the first coordinate of the Sobol’ sequence. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Reader’s translation. In base \(2\) the index \(n=6=(110)_2\) has its digits reflected to \(0.011_2=\tfrac14+\tfrac18=\tfrac38\). Feeding \(n=1,2,3,\dots\) through \(\varphi_2\) therefore lays down the points \(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac34,\tfrac18,\dots\), each new one dropped into the largest remaining gap. That is the whole idea of a low-discrepancy sequence: successive points avoid where the earlier ones already sit.

Proposition D.4 (Exact equidistribution of the radical inverse) Fix \(b\ge 2\) and \(m\ge 1\), and let \(N=b^{m}\). The first \(N\) radical-inverse points \(\{\varphi_b(n):0\le n<b^{m}\}\) are exactly the \(b^{m}\) left endpoints of the equal subintervals of length \(b^{-m}\), \[ \big\{\varphi_b(n):0\le n<b^{m}\big\}=\Big\{0,\ \tfrac{1}{b^{m}},\ \tfrac{2}{b^{m}},\ \dots,\ \tfrac{b^{m}-1}{b^{m}}\Big\}. \tag{D.26}\] Consequently this point set is a \((0,m,1)\)-net in base \(b\) in the sense of Definition 22.3: every elementary interval \([c\,b^{-d},(c+1)b^{-d})\) with \(0\le d\le m\) contains exactly \(b^{m-d}\) of the points. Its star discrepancy (the largest mismatch between the fraction of points inside a box \([0,u)\) and the box’s own length \(u\), a single number measuring how unevenly the points are spread) is \(D_N^{*}=b^{-m}=1/N\).

Proof. For \(0\le n<b^{m}\) only the digits \(a_0(n),\dots,a_{m-1}(n)\) can be nonzero, so by Equation D.25 \[ \varphi_b(n)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}a_\ell(n)\,b^{-\ell-1} =b^{-m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}a_\ell(n)\,b^{\,m-1-\ell}=\frac{r(n)}{b^{m}}, \qquad r(n):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}a_\ell(n)\,b^{\,m-1-\ell}. \] The map \(n\mapsto r(n)\) replaces the digit string \((a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{m-1})\) by the integer with reversed digit string \((a_{m-1},\dots,a_1,a_0)\). Digit reversal is a bijection of the set of \(m\)-digit base-\(b\) strings onto itself, hence \(n\mapsto r(n)\) is a bijection of \(\{0,1,\dots,b^{m}-1\}\) onto itself. Therefore \(\{\varphi_b(n):0\le n<b^{m}\}=\{r/b^{m}:0\le r<b^{m}\}\), which is Equation D.26.

For the net property, an elementary interval \([c\,b^{-d},(c+1)b^{-d})\) of length \(b^{-d}\) with \(d\le m\) is the union of exactly \(b^{m-d}\) of the grid cells \([r b^{-m},(r+1)b^{-m})\), namely those with \(c\,b^{m-d}\le r<(c+1)b^{m-d}\). Since Equation D.26 places exactly one point at each grid left endpoint \(r/b^{m}\), the interval contains exactly \(b^{m-d}\) points, which is \(b^{t}\) with \(t=0\); this is the defining property of a \((0,m,1)\)-net (Definition 22.3). For the star discrepancy, the local discrepancy \(\Delta_N(u)=\frac1N\#\{n:\varphi_b(n)<u\}-u\) of the grid \(\{r/N\}\) satisfies, for \(u\in\big(\tfrac{j}{N},\tfrac{j+1}{N}\big]\), \(\#\{r/N<u\}=j+1\) or \(j\) depending on whether \(u\) exceeds the right cell endpoint, so \(|\Delta_N(u)|\le 1/N\), attained as \(u\uparrow (j+1)/N\); hence \(D_N^{*}=\sup_u|\Delta_N(u)|=1/N\) (Definition 22.1). \(\square\)

Remark. From the exact net to the sequence rate. Proposition D.4 controls the point set of size exactly \(N=b^{m}\). For an arbitrary truncation length \(N\), not a power of \(b\), the van der Corput sequence has star discrepancy \(D_N^{*}=O\big((\log N)/N\big)\), the one-dimensional low-discrepancy rate; the proof decomposes \(N\) into its base-\(b\) digits and applies the exact-net bound on each dyadic block, the \(\log N\) factor counting the blocks. This is the \(d=1\) case of the Sobol’ rate \(D_N^{*}=O((\log N)^{d}/N)\) quoted in Definition 22.3, and it is realized in the seven-point worked example below. The contrast with the \(N^{-1/2}\) spread of independent uniform points (Theorem 22.2) is the quasi-Monte Carlo advantage. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

The full Sobol’ sequence replaces the digit-reversal of Equation D.25, which is multiplication of the digit vector by the identity matrix, with multiplication by a carefully chosen matrix over \(\mathbb{F}_2=\{0,1\}\), a different matrix per coordinate. This is the digital construction.

Definition D.4 (Digital nets and the Sobol’ generating matrices) Work in base \(b=2\) and identify a point \(u\in[0,1)\) with its binary digit vector \(u=\sum_{k\ge 1}y_k 2^{-k}\), \(y_k\in\mathbb{F}_2\). For each coordinate \(i=1,\dots,s\) fix a generating matrix \(C_i\in\mathbb{F}_2^{\,w\times w}\) (entries in \(\mathbb{F}_2\), with \(w\) the working bit depth). The \(i\)-th coordinate of the \(n\)-th digital point is defined by carrying the binary digits of \(n\) through \(C_i\) with arithmetic over \(\mathbb{F}_2\): writing \(n=\sum_{\ell\ge 0}a_\ell(n)2^{\ell}\) and \(\mathbf{a}(n)=(a_0(n),a_1(n),\dots)^\top\), \[ \mathbf{y}^{(i)}=C_i\,\mathbf{a}(n)\ \ (\text{mod }2),\qquad u_{n,i}=\sum_{k\ge 1}y^{(i)}_k\,2^{-k}. \tag{D.27}\] Taking \(C_i=I\) (the identity) recovers the radical inverse \(u_{n,i}=\varphi_2(n)\) of Equation D.25. The Sobol’ sequence is the digital sequence whose \(i\)-th generating matrix \(C_i\) has, as its columns, the binary expansions of the direction numbers \(v_{i,\ell}=m_{i,\ell}2^{-\ell}\) (a fixed list of dyadic fractions, one per bit level \(\ell\), that encodes how coordinate \(i\) is scrambled) generated from a primitive polynomial over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) (an irreducible polynomial whose root generates every nonzero element of a binary field, the standard device for producing a maximal-length binary recurrence) by the recurrence of Equation 22.11; with that choice Equation D.27 reproduces \(u_{n,i}=\bigoplus_{\ell:\,a_\ell(n)=1}v_{i,\ell}\), the exclusive-or of the selected direction numbers. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example D.4 (A four-point digital net in two dimensions) The smallest example that shows “balanced in every box” beyond one dimension uses base \(2\), resolution \(m=2\), and \(s=2\) coordinates, so \(N=2^{2}=4\) points. Take the first coordinate to be the radical inverse, \(C_1=I_2\), and the second to be generated by \[ C_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \ (\text{over }\mathbb{F}_2). \] Running \(n=0,1,2,3\) with index bits \(\mathbf{a}(n)=(a_0,a_1)^\top\) through Equation D.27 gives the four points \[ (0,0),\quad\Big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\Big),\quad\Big(\tfrac14,\tfrac34\Big),\quad\Big(\tfrac34,\tfrac14\Big). \] This set is a \((0,2,2)\)-net: every elementary box of area \(2^{-2}=\tfrac14\) holds exactly one point. There are three shapes of such box, one per split \((d_1,d_2)\) of the two available resolution levels. The four vertical quarter-strips \([j/4,(j+1)/4)\times[0,1)\) each catch one point, because the \(x\)-coordinates are the four distinct quarters \(\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34\}\); the four horizontal strips likewise, because the \(y\)-coordinates are the same four quarters; and the four half-by-half boxes each catch one, as one checks by listing: \((0,0)\) and \((\tfrac14,\tfrac34)\) lie in the left half, \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) and \((\tfrac34,\tfrac14)\) in the right, with one high and one low in each. No box of area \(\tfrac14\) is empty and none holds two: that is exactly the even coverage the rank condition of Proposition D.5 guarantees, here because the row stack \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) of the first row of \(C_1\) over the first row of \(C_2\), together with \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) themselves, are each full rank over \(\mathbb{F}_2\). \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Proposition D.5 (The digital-net equidistribution condition) Let a base-\(2\) digital point set \(\{(u_{n,1},\dots,u_{n,s}):0\le n<2^{m}\}\) be generated by matrices \(C_1,\dots,C_s\in\mathbb{F}_2^{\,m\times m}\) as in Equation D.27 (using the low-order \(m\) bits). The set is a \((t,m,s)\)-net in base \(2\) (every elementary interval of volume \(2^{t-m}\) holds exactly \(2^{t}\) points) provided that for every choice of nonnegative integers \(d_1,\dots,d_s\) with \(d_1+\cdots+d_s=m-t\), the \((m-t)\times m\) matrix obtained by stacking the first \(d_i\) rows of each \(C_i\) has rank \(m-t\) over \(\mathbb{F}_2\). For \(s=1\) and \(C_1=I\) the condition holds with \(t=0\), recovering Proposition D.4.

Proof. We prove the rank condition is sufficient; the \(s=1\) identity case is then immediate and is exactly Proposition D.4. Fix \(d_1,\dots,d_s\) with \(\sum_i d_i=m-t\) and consider the elementary interval \[ E=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\big[c_i\,2^{-d_i},(c_i+1)2^{-d_i}\big),\qquad 0\le c_i<2^{d_i}, \] of volume \(2^{-(d_1+\cdots+d_s)}=2^{t-m}\). The condition \(u_{n,i}\in[c_i 2^{-d_i},(c_i+1)2^{-d_i})\) constrains exactly the leading \(d_i\) binary digits \(y^{(i)}_1,\dots,y^{(i)}_{d_i}\) of the \(i\)-th coordinate to equal the binary digits of \(c_i\). By Equation D.27 each such digit \(y^{(i)}_k\) is the inner product over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) of the \(k\)-th row of \(C_i\) with the index vector \(\mathbf{a}(n)\in\mathbb{F}_2^{m}\). Collecting the \(\sum_i d_i=m-t\) constrained digits over all coordinates gives a linear system \(A\,\mathbf{a}(n)=\mathbf{c}\ (\text{mod }2)\), where \(A\in\mathbb{F}_2^{(m-t)\times m}\) is precisely the stack of the first \(d_i\) rows of each \(C_i\) and \(\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_2^{m-t}\) encodes the box \((c_1,\dots,c_s)\). By hypothesis \(\mathrm{rank}(A)=m-t\), so \(A\) is surjective and the solution set \(\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_2^{m}:A\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{c}\}\) is an affine subspace of dimension \(m-(m-t)=t\), containing exactly \(2^{t}\) vectors. As \(n\) ranges over \(\{0,\dots,2^{m}-1\}\) the index vector \(\mathbf{a}(n)\) ranges over all of \(\mathbb{F}_2^{m}\) bijectively, so exactly \(2^{t}\) indices \(n\) land in \(E\). Since the box \(E\) was an arbitrary elementary interval of volume \(2^{t-m}\), the point set is a \((t,m,s)\)-net (Definition 22.3). For \(s=1\), \(C_1=I_m\): any single \(d_1=m\) selects the first \(m\) rows of \(I_m\), which is \(I_m\), of rank \(m=m-0\), so \(t=0\) and the net is \((0,m,1)\), as in Proposition D.4. \(\square\)

Remark. What the rank condition buys, and where Sobol’ directions come from. Proposition D.5 reduces the geometric demand of low discrepancy, exact balance in every dyadic box, to a linear-algebra demand on the generating matrices, full rank of every admissible row-stack over \(\mathbb{F}_2\). The art of constructing a good Sobol’ sequence is choosing the direction numbers so that the resulting matrices \(C_i\) keep the net quality parameter \(t\) small simultaneously across many coordinates and many resolutions \(m\); the primitive- polynomial recurrence of Equation 22.11 guarantees full rank within each single coordinate, and the tabulated initial direction numbers of Joe and Kuo (2008) optimize the joint two-dimensional projections. The whole apparatus serves the one purpose of Theorem 22.6: a point set whose star discrepancy attains \(O((\log N)^{d}/N)\), so that the Koksma-Hlawka bound Theorem 22.3 delivers near-\(1/N\) integration error for the aggregation integral. The same guarantee carries over unchanged when the integrand is a survival or ordinal likelihood kernel rather than a mean, which is the setting of Chapter 25: the points and the bound do not know what they are integrating. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

Example D.5 (The seven-point van der Corput set) Take \(b=2\) and list \(\varphi_2(n)\) for \(n=1,\dots,7\) by reflecting binary digits about the radix point: \(n=1=(1)_2\mapsto 0.1_2=\tfrac12\); \(n=2=(10)_2\mapsto 0.01_2=\tfrac14\); \(n=3=(11)_2\mapsto 0.11_2=\tfrac34\); \(n=4=(100)_2\mapsto 0.001_2=\tfrac18\); and likewise \(\tfrac58,\tfrac38,\tfrac78\) for \(n=5,6,7\). As a set this is \(\{\tfrac18,\tfrac28,\tfrac38,\tfrac48,\tfrac58,\tfrac68,\tfrac78\}\), the seven interior eighths, matching the worked Sobol’ example of Section 22.6. Its star discrepancy is \(D_7^{*}=\tfrac18\): the largest gap between the empirical fraction \(\frac1N\#\{\varphi_2(n)<u\}\) and \(u\) occurs as \(u\uparrow k/8\), where the fraction is \(\frac{k-1}{7}\) against the volume \(\frac{k}{8}\), and the maximum of \(\big|\frac{k}{8}-\frac{k-1}{7}\big|\) over \(k=1,\dots,7\) is \(\tfrac18\) at \(k=7\). This is close to \(1/N\) and far below the \(1/\sqrt7\approx 0.38\) typical of seven independent uniform draws, the discrepancy gap that Proposition D.4 explains and that drives the QMC error advantage of Theorem 22.6. \(\blacktriangleleft\)
