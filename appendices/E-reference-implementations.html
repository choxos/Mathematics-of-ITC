Appendix E — Reference Implementations: multinma, mlumr, and uitc

E.1 Purpose and scope

The preceding thirty chapters built the mathematics of indirect treatment comparison from linear algebra to machine-checked proof. This appendix is the bridge to computation. It maps the objects the book defines and proves onto the functions, arguments, and Stan model files that evaluate them in three R packages: multinma for multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) on connected networks (Phillippo et al. 2020), mlumr for multilevel unanchored meta-regression (ML-UMR) on disconnected two-treatment evidence (Chandler and Ishak 2026), and uitc, an umbrella for unanchored population-adjusted indirect comparison (PAIC) that bundles matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC), simulated treatment comparison (STC), a doubly-robust estimator, and quantitative bias analysis (QBA). All three estimate their Bayesian models in Stan (Carpenter et al. 2017).

This is a map, not a tutorial. Code is kept to the minimum needed to locate each mathematical object in the software; the package vignettes are the place to learn the workflows end to end. Symbols follow Notation throughout: \(\eta_{ijk}\) is the linear predictor, \(g^{-1}\) the inverse link, \(\theta_{ijk}=g^{-1}(\eta_{ijk})\) the conditional mean on the outcome scale, \(f_j\) the covariate density in study \(j\), \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\) its mean and covariance, and \(d_{ab(\mathcal{P})}\) the marginal relative effect of \(b\) versus \(a\) in target population \(\mathcal{P}\).

Remark. The correspondence in this appendix is at the level of mathematical object, not source line. Package versions move; the function signatures shown were read from multinma, mlumr, and uitc at the time of writing and may drift. What does not drift is the underlying mathematics: each computational object named below implements a specific definition, equation, or theorem proved in the chapters, and that linkage is what the tables record. Where a formula is reproduced here it is reproduced exactly; verify argument names against the installed help pages before relying on them.

E.2 Orientation for the first-time reader

Read this appendix alongside the chapters, not instead of them. Each chapter answers why a method is built the way it is and proves that it works; this appendix answers where that mathematics lives in the software and which function evaluates it. The intended use is lookup and verification: when you have read the aggregation theorem of Chapter 20 and want to see the line of Stan that computes it, or when you are about to call nma() and want to know which equation each argument controls, this is the page to open. A few subsections (the Stan-file inventories, the adjusted-binomial reparametrization) are there mainly for readers checking that the code faithfully implements the proofs; a first-time user can skim them and return later. Everything else is meant to be read on a first pass.

If you are new to these methods, a good order is: read this orientation, choose your package from the decision guide in Section E.2.1, read that package’s pipeline table, and follow the prose pointers back into the chapters whenever a term or symbol is unfamiliar. The three object-to-code maps (Section E.3.5, Section E.4.5, Section E.5.5) are the reference cards to return to once you know the workflow.

Remark. A running example, used throughout. To keep the symbols attached to something concrete, we carry one small scenario through the appendix. The outcome is a binary treatment response (PASI 75, meaning at least a 75 percent reduction in a psoriasis severity score at week 12: 1 if achieved, 0 if not). The covariates are patient age in years (continuous) and sex (binary, coded 1 for female). Three treatments appear: drug A, our new treatment, for which we hold individual patient data (IPD, one row per patient); drug B, a competitor, for which we have only a published aggregate summary (mean age, percent female, and responders out of total); and placebo P, a possible common comparator. The goal is the relative effect of A versus B in a decision target population with mean age 48 and 40 percent female. Two data situations recur:

  • Connected (anchored) network: trial 1 reports IPD on A versus P; trial 2 reports aggregate data (AgD) on B versus P. They share the placebo arm P, so the network is connected and the A versus B contrast is anchored through P. This is the multinma setting.
  • Disconnected (unanchored) network: trial 1 reports IPD on A, trial 2 reports AgD on B, and the two share no common arm. The network is disconnected, the contrast is unanchored, and it is identified only by adjusting the IPD to the comparator population. This is the mlumr and uitc setting.

Two words recur and decide everything below, so fix them here. An anchored comparison estimates A versus B indirectly through a shared reference arm that both trials contain (in the example, placebo P); the shared arm cancels study-level nuisance terms, which is why a connected network can be analyzed with the trial-level contrasts of Chapter 14 and Chapter 15. An unanchored comparison has no shared arm, so nothing cancels; the entire between-study difference in outcomes must be explained by adjusting for covariates, a much stronger assumption (Chapter 12, Chapter 26). Whenever the appendix says “anchored” or “unanchored” it means exactly this.

E.2.1 Which package answers which question

Choose the package from the shape of your evidence, not from the method name. The rule is the network structure, summarized here and repeated as a routing table in Section E.6.

Your situation (running example) Network Method Package
IPD on A vs P, AgD on B vs P, shared arm P connected, anchored ML-NMR multinma
IPD on A, AgD on B, no shared arm disconnected, unanchored ML-UMR mlumr
Same unanchored data, but you also want MAIC, STC, doubly-robust, and a bias analysis side by side unanchored PAIC and QBA uitc

In health-technology-assessment (HTA) terms, the three packages answer three versions of the same submission question, “what is the effect of our drug A versus competitor B in the population our decision is about?”

  • multinma (ML-NMR). You have at least one IPD trial and the network of trials is connected through shared treatments. You want the anchored A versus B effect, transported to a chosen target population. Reach for multinma. This is the statistically strongest situation, because the shared arm protects you.
  • mlumr (ML-UMR). Your IPD trial and the competitor’s trial share no arm, so the network is broken. You must lean entirely on covariate adjustment to bridge the gap. Reach for mlumr, and expect to defend the adjustment assumptions carefully, because there is no anchor to fall back on.
  • uitc (PAIC plus QBA). The same broken network, but you want to run the established population-adjustment methods (MAIC, STC), a doubly-robust combination, and a quantitative bias analysis on one data object, so you can report an estimate next to its sensitivity analysis. Reach for uitc.

E.3 multinma: multilevel network meta-regression

multinma fits the ML-NMR model of Chapters 20 to 25: one individual-level regression \[ g\big(\theta_{ijk}\big)=\eta_{ijk}=\mu_j+\boldsymbol{\beta}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_{ij}+\big(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}^\top\mathbf{x}_{ij}+\gamma_k\big) \tag{E.1}\] shared across a connected network, fitted to individual patient data (IPD) directly and to aggregate data (AgD) through the aggregation integral. The package is the reference implementation accompanying Phillippo et al. (2020) and its survival extension Phillippo et al. (2025).

Read Equation E.1 one term at a time; it is the whole model, and every multinma argument sets one of its pieces. The left side \(g(\theta_{ijk})\) is the response of patient \(i\) in study \(j\) on treatment \(k\), mapped to the linear-predictor scale by the link \(g\) (for a binary outcome \(g\) is the logit, so \(\eta_{ijk}\) is a log-odds). The right side builds that log-odds from four parts.

Term Name What it does (running example) multinma
\(\mu_j\) study baseline the outcome level in study \(j\)’s control arm; a separate intercept per trial fitted per study automatically
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_{ij}\) prognostic effects how age and sex shift outcome regardless of treatment main effects added via regression =
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}^\top\mathbf{x}_{ij}\) effect modification how age and sex shift the treatment effect of arm \(k\) the :.trt interaction in regression =
\(\gamma_k\) treatment effect the effect of treatment \(k\) versus the network reference, at the reference covariate values the treatment coefficients

In words: a patient’s modeled log-odds of response is their study’s baseline, plus a prognostic shift for being older or female, plus (only on an active arm) an effect-modifying shift and the drug’s own effect. The single most important feature is that the same \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1\), \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}\), and \(\gamma_k\) are shared across every study in the network; that sharing is what lets an IPD trial and an AgD trial inform one regression, and it is licensed by the shared-effect-modifier assumption (SEMA) of Chapter 17. When a covariate modifies the effect (its \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}\) is nonzero), the A versus B contrast genuinely depends on the population’s age and sex mix, which is the entire reason population adjustment is needed.

E.3.1 The pipeline

Six functions carry an analysis from data to fitted model. Each one realizes a construction from the book.

Function Mathematical role
set_ipd() Register an IPD study: rows are individuals, with outcome and covariates entering Equation E.1 directly.
set_agd_arm() Register an arm-based AgD study: summary outcomes per arm, to be matched by the aggregation integral (Theorem 20.1).
set_agd_contrast() Register a contrast-based AgD study: relative effects with a covariance, the meta-analytic likelihood of Chapter 13.
set_agd_surv() Register AgD with reconstructed survival data (Kaplan-Meier curves digitized to pseudo-IPD), the general-likelihood survival branch of Chapter 25.
combine_network() Assemble the registered studies into one network object; checks connectivity (the precondition removed only in Chapter 26).
add_integration() Attach quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) integration points to every AgD study (Chapter 22).
nma() Build the Stan data, choose the likelihood and link, and sample the posterior (Chapter 24).

Before any of this runs, the data must be in the shape those functions expect: IPD is one row per patient, arm-based AgD is one row per arm. For the running example the two inputs look like this (a few rows shown; the column names are what matter).

# ipd_data: one row per patient in the IPD trial of A vs P
  study    trt   r  age  sex
  Trial1     P   0   52    0
  Trial1     A   1   47    1
  Trial1     A   0   61    0
# ... one row per patient ...

# agd_data: one row per arm in the AgD trial of B vs P
  study    trt    r    n  age_mean  age_sd  sex_prop
  Trial2     P   58  200      49.1    11.8      0.42
  Trial2     B   96  201      48.6    12.1      0.39

Here r is the outcome column (a responder indicator 1/0 per patient in IPD, a responder count per arm in AgD), n is the arm size, study and trt are the study and treatment labels, and the age_* and sex_prop columns are the covariate summaries the AgD trial publishes. The canonical call sequence is

net <- combine_network(
  set_ipd(ipd_data, study, trt, r = r),
  set_agd_arm(agd_data, study, trt, r = r, n = n)
)
net <- add_integration(net, age = distr(qnorm, mean = age_mean, sd = age_sd),
                            sex = distr(qbern, prob = sex_prop), n_int = 64L)
fit <- nma(net, regression = ~ (age + sex):.trt, likelihood = "bernoulli2",
           link = "logit", trt_effects = "fixed")

The regression formula ~ (age + sex):.trt encodes the effect-modifier interactions \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k}\); the special term .trt is the treatment factor. Prognostic main effects \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1\) are added automatically with center = TRUE.

Each argument of that call controls one modeling choice. In ordinary trial language:

Argument Plain meaning
study, trt the columns naming the study and the treatment arm
r = r set the model’s responder input to the data column named r; the left r is the fixed argument name, the right r is your column name
n = n the arm size column (AgD only; IPD has one patient per row)
regression = ~ (age + sex):.trt which covariates are effect modifiers; .trt is the placeholder for the treatment factor, so (age + sex):.trt means “let age and sex modify the treatment effect”
likelihood = "bernoulli2" the outcome family; bernoulli2 is the two-parameter binary likelihood of Section E.3.2
link = "logit" the link \(g\); logit makes \(\eta\) a log-odds
trt_effects = "fixed" fixed-effect (common-effect) treatment effects, one \(\gamma_k\) shared across studies, as opposed to "random" for treatment effects that vary by study

The note that main effects are added “with center = TRUE” means the covariates are centered before entering the model, so that the treatment coefficients \(\gamma_k\) read as effects at the average covariate values of the network, not at age zero. Centering does not change the fitted contrasts in any target population; it only fixes what the raw \(\gamma_k\) mean and improves the sampler’s geometry. Which population the final estimand refers to is set later, by relative_effects() and marginal_effects() (Section E.3.4), not by centering.

E.3.2 The aggregation integral

Start with the reason this section exists. The obvious way to predict an aggregate arm’s response rate is to plug the arm’s mean covariates into the fitted model: evaluate \(g^{-1}\) at mean age and percent female. For any nonlinear link (a logit, a log) this is wrong, and Chapter 20 proves it is wrong; the error is Theorem 20.2. Averaging a curved function is not the same as the function of the average. So multinma never does that; instead it averages patient-level predictions, which is the content of Theorem 20.1.

Concretely, the correct aggregate-level mean is the average of the inverse-linked individual means, not the inverse link of the average linear predictor. For an arm \(k\) in AgD study \(j\), \[ \bar\theta_{jk}=\int_{\mathcal{X}} g^{-1}\!\big(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,f_j(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} \;\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N} g^{-1}\!\big(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}^{(m)})\big), \qquad \mathbf{x}^{(m)}\sim f_j , \tag{E.2}\] which is Equation 20.2 evaluated at the \(N\) integration points of Section E.3.3. Read the right side as a recipe: generate \(N\) synthetic patients whose covariates follow the AgD study’s reported distribution \(f_j\), predict each one’s response probability from the shared regression, then average those probabilities. That average, not the prediction at the average patient, is what the arm’s observed responder count is matched to. In inst/stan/binomial_1par.stan this is the single line (the comments are ours)

// theta_agd_arm_ii stacks every arm's N per-patient predicted probabilities end to end.
// This slice pulls out the block belonging to AgD arm i, then averages it: exactly the integral above.
theta_agd_arm_bar[i] = mean(theta_agd_arm_ii[(1 + (i-1)*nint):(i*nint)]);
// "~" is Stan's sampling statement (a likelihood contribution, not an assignment):
// the observed responder count is binomial with size n and the integrated probability.
agd_arm_r ~ binomial(agd_arm_n, theta_agd_arm_bar);

where theta_agd_arm_ii holds the \(N\) values \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}))\) and nint is \(N\). (The index arithmetic (1 + (i-1)*nint):(i*nint) is just “rows \((i-1)N+1\) through \(iN\),” the contiguous block for the \(i\)-th arm in a stacked array.)

The two-parameter file binomial_2par.stan refines the likelihood, and the reason is a variance mismatch worth spelling out. The aggregate arm is a mixture: the model assigns patients with different covariates different response probabilities \(\theta_m\), so summarizing the arm by the single number \(\bar\theta_{jk}\) discards the spread of those probabilities. A plain binomial with probability \(\bar\theta_{jk}\) ignores that spread and can understate the dispersion. The first two moments of the per-patient probabilities are \[ \bar\theta_{jk}=\frac1N\sum_m\theta_m,\qquad \overline{\theta^2}_{jk}=\frac1N\sum_m\theta_m^2, \] and their gap \(\overline{\theta^2}_{jk}-\bar\theta_{jk}^{\,2}\ge 0\) is precisely the variance of those probabilities (zero only if every patient has the same \(\theta\)). To carry that extra dispersion while keeping the same mean, binomial_2par.stan matches both moments with an adjusted binomial, \[ n'_{jk}=n_{jk}\,\frac{\bar\theta_{jk}^{\,2}}{\overline{\theta^2}_{jk}}, \qquad p'_{jk}=\frac{\overline{\theta^2}_{jk}}{\bar\theta_{jk}}, \qquad\text{so that } n'_{jk}\,p'_{jk}=n_{jk}\,\bar\theta_{jk}, \tag{E.3}\] which preserves the integrated mean \(n_{jk}\bar\theta_{jk}\) (the product \(n'p'\) is unchanged) while using \(p'_{jk}=\overline{\theta^2}_{jk}/\bar\theta_{jk}\ge\bar\theta_{jk}\) to widen the variance toward the mixture’s. This is the implementation of the aggregate likelihood Equation 20.5. Contrast all of this with the naive plug-in \(g^{-1}(\eta_{jk}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_j))\) of Equation 20.6, whose bias Theorem 20.2 quantifies; multinma never forms the naive quantity for a nonlinear link.

E.3.3 Quasi-Monte Carlo integration

Here is the practical problem this machinery solves. A published trial almost never releases its patients’ covariates; it reports summaries, a mean age, a standard deviation, a percent female, sometimes a correlation. To evaluate the aggregation integral Equation E.2 we nonetheless need a cloud of covariate profiles that reproduces those summaries. add_integration() manufactures exactly that: a grid of \(N\) synthetic covariate vectors whose marginal distributions and correlations match the reported summaries, so that averaging model predictions across the grid approximates averaging over the real (but unpublished) patient distribution. “Quasi-Monte Carlo” (QMC) just means the grid is a cleverly spread low-discrepancy set rather than random draws, which makes the average converge faster.

add_integration() builds the points \(\{\mathbf{x}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^N\) that turn Equation E.2 into a finite sum. It implements the pipeline of Chapter 22: a Sobol’ net (Definition 22.3) pushed through a Gaussian copula (Definition 22.5) and the marginal quantile functions (Theorem 22.4), \[ \mathbf{x}^{(m)}=\Big(F_{j,1}^{-1}\big(u_1^{(m)}\big),\dots,F_{j,d}^{-1}\big(u_d^{(m)}\big)\Big), \qquad u_q^{(m)}=\Phi\!\big((L\,\mathbf{z}^{(m)})_q\big),\quad \mathbf{z}^{(m)}=\Phi^{-1}\!\big(\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\big), \tag{E.4}\] where \(\{\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\}\subset[0,1)^d\) is the Sobol’ sequence and \(LL^\top=\Omega\) is the Cholesky factor of the copula correlation matrix (this is Equation 22.17). Written as nested transformations the display is dense, so read it as a four-step pipeline, each step feeding the next in the order the data flows.

Step Transformation Produces Set by
1. spread \(\mathbf{a}^{(m)}\in[0,1)^d\), a Sobol’ low-discrepancy point an evenly spread point in the unit cube n_int (how many points \(N\))
2. to normal scores \(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}=\Phi^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{(m)})\) independent standard-normal coordinates (internal)
3. correlate, back to uniform \(u_q^{(m)}=\Phi\big((L\mathbf{z}^{(m)})_q\big)\), with \(LL^\top=\Omega\) correlated uniforms with the target dependence cor, cor_adjust (build \(\Omega\), then \(L\))
4. to covariate scale \(x_q^{(m)}=F_{j,q}^{-1}(u_q^{(m)})\) a synthetic patient’s covariate values <cov> = distr(qfun, ...) (the marginal \(F_{j,q}^{-1}\))

The Gaussian copula (step 3) is the device that lets us glue arbitrary marginal distributions (age maybe normal, a biomarker maybe skewed) together with a chosen correlation: it imposes the dependence in a normal latent space, then maps each coordinate back to its own marginal in step 4. If the copula is new to you, read it as “a correlation structure applied to uniforms, so any marginals can be attached afterward,” and see the picture in Chapter 22. The pieces map to arguments as follows.

Argument of add_integration() Mathematical object
<cov> = distr(qfun, ...) the marginal quantile function \(F_{j,q}^{-1}\) of covariate \(q\) (distr wraps a base-R q* function).
n_int the number of integration points \(N\); error decays like \(N^{-1}(\log N)^d\) by Koksma-Hlawka (Theorem 22.3), beating the \(N^{-1/2}\) Monte Carlo rate (Corollary 22.1).
cor the input covariate correlation matrix used to build \(\Omega\).
cor_adjust the rule converting cor (Spearman or Pearson) to the Gaussian-copula correlation \(\Omega\) (Sklar’s theorem, Theorem 22.5).

The distr() wrapper deserves a word, since it appears with no introduction in the call above. distr(qfun, ...) bundles a base-R quantile function with its parameters into the marginal \(F_{j,q}^{-1}\) that step 4 needs. For instance distr(qnorm, mean = 48, sd = 12) represents an age marginal that is normal with mean 48 and standard deviation 12: given a uniform \(u\) it returns qnorm(u, mean = 48, sd = 12), the age at quantile \(u\). Likewise distr(qbern, prob = 0.40) encodes a binary covariate that is female with probability 0.40. You supply one distr() per covariate, using the summaries the AgD trial reported.

How many points? n_int is \(N\), and 64 is a common default. Because the Koksma-Hlawka rate (Theorem 22.3) is roughly \(N^{-1}\) up to a log factor that grows with the covariate dimension \(d\), more covariates need more points; the honest way to choose \(N\) is empirical. Fit with n_int = 64L, refit with it doubled, and confirm the estimand you care about moves by less than your tolerance; plot_integration_error() automates this check as the empirical analogue of the Koksma-Hlawka bound. Cost grows linearly in \(N\), so doubling the points doubles the integration work, not more.

E.3.4 The target-population estimand

A fitted stan_nma object stores the conditional parameters \((\mu_j,\boldsymbol{\beta}_1,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2,k},\gamma_k)\). The estimand machinery of Chapter 23 turns these into a contrast in any target population, via the target-population integral Theorem 23.1.

Two kinds of answer come out of the same fit, and confusing them is a common error, so fix the distinction first (if it is unclear, reread Chapter 23 before calling these functions). A conditional effect is for patients with given covariates: the A versus B log-odds ratio for a 55-year-old woman. A marginal effect is averaged over a whole population: the A versus B effect seen across the entire target population’s age and sex mix. On a nonlinear scale such as the odds ratio these differ, because the odds ratio is non-collapsible (Chapter 11); the population-average effect is not the average of subgroup effects. For an HTA submission the reported number is almost always the marginal effect in the decision population, since that is the effect a payer would experience in practice. So relative_effects() gives the conditional building blocks, and marginal_effects() gives the reportable population summary.

Function Returns Book object
relative_effects() conditional contrasts \(\delta_{j,\,t_1 t_k}\) in a study or newdata population Equation 23.6, the conditional estimand of Definition 23.1

In the running example, relative_effects() might report a conditional log-odds ratio for A versus B of, say, \(-0.82\) (95 percent credible interval \(-1.21\) to \(-0.43\)) at the network’s average covariates, predict(type = "response") the absolute response probabilities in each arm of the target population, and marginal_effects() the population-averaged A versus B contrast to put in the submission. (These numbers are illustrative, not from a real fit.)

Supplying newdata (with its own add_integration() points) is exactly the change of target population \(\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathcal{P}^{*}\) that Theorem 23.1 licenses; with no newdata the estimand defaults to each study’s own population. So the same fitted model produces different reported effects for different target populations, and choosing that population is a substantive decision, not a default.

Remark. Sanity check: ML-NMR contains NMA. When every study is AgD and no covariates enter the regression, the aggregation integral is trivial and the model collapses to standard network meta-analysis; a call like nma(net, trt_effects = "fixed") with no regression = reproduces the Chapter 15 estimator. This is the computational witness of Theorem 23.4, and it is a good first check that a network is set up correctly: turn off the regression and confirm you recover the familiar NMA result.

E.4 mlumr: multilevel unanchored meta-regression

mlumr implements ML-UMR (Chapter 26): the unanchored, two-treatment specialization of ML-NMR for a disconnected network (Definition 26.1), IPD on one index treatment \(A\), AgD on one comparator \(B\), and no common arm. It reuses the QMC and copula machinery of multinma and adds Stan likelihoods for the unanchored problem (Chandler and Ishak 2026).

Recall from Section E.2 what unanchored means here: the index IPD trial (drug A) and the comparator AgD trial (drug B) share no arm, so there is no reference treatment to difference away the study-level baselines. Everything separating the two trials’ outcomes, prognosis and effect modification alike, must be explained by covariate adjustment. That is a strong assumption, and it is why ML-UMR needs both the shared-prognostic-factor machinery below and the honest caveats of Chapter 26. The add_integration() step is the same construction as in multinma (Section E.3.3), but its job is narrower here: rather than integrating several AgD studies across a connected network, it builds the single covariate cloud that represents the comparator population \(f_B\), the population the index data must be bridged to.

E.4.2 SPFA and relaxed models

The two model strings select the identification assumption of Chapter 26. The assumption is not a technical switch; it encodes what you are willing to believe about how covariates act across treatments, so decide it deliberately. The shared prognostic factor assumption (SPFA) says that a covariate affects outcome the same way under both treatments: if age raises risk, it raises it by the same amount under A and under B. Under SPFA a single coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) serves both arms. The relaxed model drops that and lets prognosis differ by treatment, carrying separate \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_A\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_B\); the gap \(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_A-\boldsymbol{\beta}_B\) is exactly effect modification (a covariate whose influence differs by treatment is, by definition, an effect modifier). Concretely: if age predicts response similarly whether a patient took A or B, the SPFA is reasonable and model = "spfa" is the primary analysis; if you believe age helps under A but not under B, that difference is what model = "relaxed" is trying to estimate.

model = Assumption Coefficients Book object
"spfa" shared prognostic factors, no effect modification one \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) for both treatments Definition 26.2, Equation 26.3
"relaxed" treatment-specific prognostic effects \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_A,\boldsymbol{\beta}_B\), with \(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_A-\boldsymbol{\beta}_B\) Theorem 26.2, Equation 26.10

In inst/stan/mlumr_binary_spfa.stan the comparator AgD likelihood is the aggregation integral Equation E.2 written for one comparator arm,

// Xq_int[k] is the N-by-p matrix of integration points for comparator arm k, and beta_tilde the shared
// coefficient vector, so Xq_int[k] * beta_tilde is the N linear predictors; inv_link_binary_vec applies
// g^{-1} (here the inverse logit) to each, giving the N per-patient probabilities p_int.
vector[n_int] p_int = inv_link_binary_vec(Xq_int[k] * beta_tilde, link);
// "~" is a sampling statement: the comparator responder count is binomial with size n_agd[k] and the
// integrated (averaged) probability mean(p_int), i.e. the aggregation integral for one arm.
r_agd[k] ~ binomial(n_agd[k], mean(p_int));

which is Equation 26.8 and the joint likelihood of Theorem 26.6 (the IPD Bernoulli term times this integrated binomial term). The relaxed file carries a second coefficient block and reports delta_beta = beta_index - beta_comparator, the estimated effect modification of Theorem 26.2; its prior is set with prior_beta_comparator, since \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_B\) is identified only through the AgD. Both files compute, in their generated quantities, the response-scale population means in both target populations, the index (IPD covariates) and the comparator (integration points weighted by AgD sample size), and from them the marginal log odds ratio, risk difference, and risk ratio. This is Equation 26.1 and the target functionals Equation 26.21; the choice is exposed through marginal_effects(fit, population = , effect = ). conditional_effects() returns the covariate-conditional contrasts instead.

What to look at after a fit: marginal_effects(fit, population = , effect = ) is the headline, the marginal A versus B contrast (as a log odds ratio, risk difference, or risk ratio) in whichever population you name; conditional_effects() gives the covariate-conditional version if you need it. Fit under model = "spfa" for the primary estimate, and if you also fit "relaxed", inspect delta_beta: a credible interval comfortably covering zero supports the SPFA, while one clearly away from zero warns that effect modification is present and the SPFA estimate may be biased.

E.4.3 The benchmarks: naive and stc

Function Method Book object
naive() unadjusted comparison of crude outcome summaries the naive estimator Equation 20.6 (and the bias it incurs, Theorem 20.2)
stc() frequentist parametric G-computation with delta-method standard errors STC (Chapter 19); Proposition 19.1, the marginal estimand Equation 19.14

“G-computation,” the phrase attached to stc(), is causal-inference shorthand for a two-step recipe: fit the outcome model on the IPD, then average its predictions over the target (here, comparator) covariate distribution. It is the frequentist sibling of what the Bayesian model does inside its generated quantities block.

stc() fits the outcome regression on the IPD and integrates the fitted model over the comparator covariate distribution, the G-computation form Proposition 19.1; Theorem 26.8 shows that ML-UMR under SPFA and STC target the same estimand, so the two should agree up to Monte Carlo and posterior error when the SPFA holds. Always run naive() alongside the adjusted methods as the unadjusted reference point.

How to read the three together. Treat naive() as the unadjusted baseline, stc() and mlumr(model = "spfa") as two routes to the same adjusted estimand (Theorem 26.8), and mlumr(model = "relaxed") as the stress test. Reassuring pattern: stc() and mlumr("spfa") agree (they should, when the SPFA holds), and both differ from naive() in the direction the covariate imbalance predicts, showing the adjustment did something. Concerning pattern: stc() and mlumr("spfa") disagree materially, which points to a modeling or convergence problem since they target one estimand; or the "relaxed" fit moves the estimate a lot and its delta_beta excludes zero, signaling effect modification the SPFA ignores. None of these is a hypothesis test; they are consistency checks that tell you whether to trust the adjusted number or investigate further.

E.4.4 Survival outcomes

This subsection locates the survival functions; it is dense because each piece is a chapter of its own. If you are implementing a survival ITC for the first time, read Chapter 25 (general likelihoods and survival) and the mlumr survival vignette before relying on the calls below. The shape of the workflow is as follows.

The input problem is that a published survival trial reports a Kaplan-Meier curve (a step plot of the fraction still event-free over time), not patient records. set_agd_surv() reconstructs approximate patient-level data from it: you supply points read off the curve (time and survival-probability pairs, typically extracted with a plot digitizer) together with the numbers at risk printed under the axis, and the function inverts the curve into individual event and censoring times. This reconstruction is Theorem 26.5, the inversion Equation 26.19; more digitized points and reported at-risk tables give a more faithful reconstruction. The result is pseudo-IPD for the comparator arm, which the model then treats like real IPD.

The hazard is modeled with one of nine parametric families (distribution = "weibull", "gompertz", accelerated-failure-time variants, and so on) or a flexible baseline ("mspline", an M-spline hazard, or "pexp", a piecewise-exponential hazard), with spline knots placed by make_knots(). As a rule of thumb: a simple parametric family is easiest to interpret and extrapolate but assumes a rigid hazard shape; the flexible "mspline" and "pexp" baselines fit curved or non-monotone hazards at the cost of extra parameters. Choose by checking fit against the reconstructed curve and by how far beyond the observed follow-up you must extrapolate.

The reporting is shaped by collapsibility. The hazard ratio (HR) is non-collapsible (Chapter 11): the population-average HR is not the average of subgroup HRs, and it can drift over time even under a constant treatment effect, so a single number understates the story. Accordingly marginal_effects(fit, effect = "hr") reports the HR at the start of follow-up, and the time-varying curve comes from predict(type = "loghr"). Restricted mean survival time (RMST, the average event-free time up to a horizon) is collapsible and transports more cleanly, which is why it is often the better headline for an HTA: predict(type = "rmst") and the RMST difference and ratio implement the transported restricted-mean functionals Theorem 26.7 and Equation 26.17. When in doubt, report an RMST contrast at a clinically meaningful horizon alongside the HR.

E.5 uitc: unanchored PAIC and bias analysis

uitc is an umbrella over the unanchored PAIC methods, with a single dispatcher and a QBA toolkit. It maps onto Chapter 18 (MAIC), Chapter 19 (STC), the augmented estimators of Chapter 10, and Chapter 28 (QBA).

Note the change of purpose here. multinma and mlumr each fit one Bayesian model; uitc is instead an organizer that runs several unanchored adjustment and sensitivity workflows around the same comparator problem, so you can lay an estimate next to its alternatives and its bias analysis. Its input is an mlumr_data object, the same bundle mlumr::combine_data() produces: it holds the index IPD, the comparator AgD summary, the covariate definitions, and (after add_integration()) the integration points. Because every uitc method reads that one object, a single data setup feeds MAIC, STC, ML-UMR, naive, doubly-robust, and QBA in turn.

E.5.1 The dispatcher

uitc(dat, method = c("maic", "stc", "mlumr", "naive", "dr"))

dat is an mlumr_data object built by mlumr::combine_data(). The stc, mlumr, and naive methods delegate to mlumr (mapped in Section E.4); the maic and dr methods are native to uitc and are described next. All delegated methods return a standardized uitc_result with estimate, se, and an interval.

E.5.2 MAIC (Chapter 18)

MAIC reweights the IPD so that its weighted covariate moments match the comparator’s, then estimates the treatment effect on the reweighted sample.

Function Mathematical role
prepare_maic() / estimate_weights() Solve the entropy-balancing (method-of-moments) problem for the weights.
check_weights() Report the effective sample size and weight diagnostics.
calculate_balance_smd() / balance_table() / love_plot() Standardized mean differences before and after weighting.
maic_unanchored() Fit the weighted outcome model and return the effect with a robust standard error.

The weights are the exponential-tilting solution Theorem 18.1: after centering the IPD covariates on the comparator means, \(w_i=\exp(\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_i^{c})\), where \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) minimizes the strictly convex dual objective \(Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_i \exp(\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_i^{c})\) (Equation 18.6), whose stationary condition is exactly the moment balance Equation 18.1; existence and uniqueness are Theorem 18.2. estimate_weights(method = "BFGS") performs this minimization. In pseudocode the whole solve is short (the package does this internally; you never write it, but seeing it demystifies “call BFGS”):

Xc    <- sweep(X_ipd, 2, comparator_means)              # center IPD covariates on comparator means: x_i^c
Q     <- function(b) sum(exp(Xc %*% b))                 # the convex dual objective Q(beta)
grad  <- function(b) colSums(as.vector(exp(Xc %*% b)) * Xc)  # gradient = weighted covariate imbalance
b_hat <- optim(rep(0, ncol(Xc)), Q, grad, method = "BFGS")$par
w     <- exp(Xc %*% b_hat)                              # the MAIC weights w_i = exp(beta^T x_i^c)

At the optimum the gradient is zero, and the grad line set to zero says \(\sum_i w_i\mathbf{x}_i^{c}=\mathbf 0\): the weighted covariate means equal the comparator means, which is the moment balance Equation 18.1. So minimizing \(Q\) and balancing the covariates are the same act, the content of Theorem 18.1. The reported effective sample size \(\mathrm{ESS}=(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2\) is Definition 18.3 and Equation 18.20, with the upper bound of Theorem 18.6. maic_unanchored() returns the sandwich (robust) standard error of Theorem 18.9, the correct accounting for the estimated weights. The unanchored point estimate it targets, and the conditional-constancy assumption it needs, are Theorem 18.5.

Two diagnostics decide whether to trust a MAIC. The effective sample size (ESS) is the equivalent number of unweighted patients the weighted sample is worth, \((\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2\); it falls below the actual sample size whenever the weights are uneven, and a small ESS (a large drop) warns that a few patients dominate and the estimate is fragile. check_weights() reports it. Covariate balance is the other: balance_table() and love_plot() show the standardized mean difference (SMD) of each covariate between the weighted IPD and the comparator, before and after weighting. A love plot is a dot plot of those SMDs; you want every after-weighting point pulled close to zero, with a common rule of thumb of \(|\mathrm{SMD}|<0.1\). Good balance together with an adequate ESS is the license to report the maic_unanchored() effect; poor balance or a collapsed ESS is a signal to drop a covariate or reconsider the comparison.

E.5.3 STC and the doubly-robust estimator (Chapters 19, 10)

method = "stc" delegates to mlumr::stc() (Chapter 19, mapped in Section E.4.3).

dr_estimator() (also method = "dr") combines the two adjustment strategies into one estimator that is consistent if either the weighting model or the outcome model is correctly specified, the double-robustness property of the augmented inverse-probability-weighting (AIPW) estimator of Chapter 10. Plainly, it gives you two chances to be right: if either the weighting model or the outcome model is correct, the estimate is consistent. A minimal call is

dr <- dr_estimator(dat,
                   weighting    = "maic",           # get weights from MAIC (Chapter 18)
                   augmentation = "weighted_gcomp",  # correct with a G-computation term (Chapter 19)
                   cor_method   = "spearman",        # correlation used by the NORTA comparator simulation
                   n_sim        = 1000,              # NORTA draws of the comparator covariate profile
                   n_boot       = 1000)              # bootstrap resamples for the standard error
Argument Mathematical role
weighting = c("maic", "iow") the propensity-type model: MAIC entropy weights (Chapter 18) or inverse-odds weights.
augmentation = c("residual", "weighted_gcomp") the outcome-regression correction: the G-computation term of Chapter 19.
cor_method, n_sim the NORTA simulation of the comparator covariate profile (the Gaussian-copula construction Theorem 28.2) used to evaluate the augmentation.

The point estimate is the weighting estimate plus a mean-zero (under either model) augmentation; the weighting piece is Theorem 18.1 and the augmentation piece is the G-computation functional Proposition 19.1. Standard errors come from the bootstrap (n_boot).

The two augmentation choices are two ways to write the same correction: "residual" adds the weighted mean IPD residual from the outcome model, while "weighted_gcomp" forms the G-computation prediction over the simulated comparator covariates and corrects toward it; both have mean zero when the outcome model is correct, so they agree in large samples and differ mainly in finite-sample behavior. The two integer knobs serve different stages and should not be confused: n_sim sets how many comparator covariate profiles the NORTA step simulates to evaluate the augmentation, whereas n_int (from add_integration()) sets the QMC grid used inside the Bayesian fits; raising either reduces a different Monte Carlo error.

E.5.4 Quantitative bias analysis (Chapter 28)

Two families of QBA ship with uitc. The ITC-specific functions act on the unanchored estimand and ask how an unmeasured covariate would move it; the classical functions act on a \(2\times2\) table. In practice the QBA functions form a ladder you climb only as far as you need: start with the E-value as a one-number screening calculation (how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to overturn the result?); if that is not reassuring, run an explicit sensitivity analysis with qba_unmeasured_itc() or qba_tipping_itc(); and if you can put priors on the bias, escalate to the probabilistic analysis qba_prob_itc(). The classical \(2\times2\) functions are for the separate case of a single contingency table rather than the unanchored estimand.

Function Method Book object
confounders_evalue() E-value for the minimum unmeasured association that could explain away the effect Definition 28.2, Theorem 28.1, Equation 28.9
qba_unmeasured_itc() simulate an unmeasured confounder jointly with the measured covariates by NORTA, re-estimate Theorem 28.2, Equation 28.12, the correction Equation 28.1
qba_tipping_itc() find the sensitivity-parameter value at which the corrected estimate crosses a threshold Definition 28.3, Equation 28.15, monotone Proposition 28.2
qba_prob_itc() probabilistic bias analysis: priors on the bias parameters, Monte Carlo over the bias-corrected estimand Definition 28.4, Theorem 28.3
probsens(), confounders(), misclass(), selection() classical epidemiologic QBA on a \(2\times2\) table Definition 28.1, the bias factor Equation 28.2

For the E-value, confounders_evalue(case, type = "RR") returns \(E=\mathrm{RR}+\sqrt{\mathrm{RR}(\mathrm{RR}-1)}\) for \(\mathrm{RR}\ge 1\) (and the reciprocal-scale version otherwise), the closed form derived in Theorem 28.1. The NORTA procedure qba_unmeasured_itc() draws an unmeasured covariate with a prescribed correlation to the measured ones through a Gaussian copula, exactly the construction whose validity is Theorem 28.2, then propagates it through the unanchored estimand Equation 28.10. Tipping-point and probabilistic outputs are visualized with plot_tipping_curve(), plot_sensitivity_curve(), and plot_sensitivity_contour().

The distinction between qba_tipping_itc() and qba_prob_itc() is load-bearing and easy to miss. A tipping-point analysis is deterministic root-finding: it varies a single sensitivity parameter (say the strength of the unmeasured confounder) and reports the exact value at which the corrected estimate crosses a threshold, for example loses significance; there is one number, “it tips here” (Definition 28.3, Equation 28.15), and its monotone behavior is Proposition 28.2. A probabilistic bias analysis instead puts a prior distribution on the bias parameters and Monte-Carlos over it, returning a whole distribution of bias-corrected estimates and an interval that folds in bias uncertainty (Definition 28.4, Theorem 28.3). Use tipping when you want a single crisp “how bad would it have to be” answer; use probabilistic when you can express honest uncertainty about the bias and want that propagated.

Comparator selection by covariate distance (Chapter 27) is also available: covariate_distance() and match_comparator() compute the Euclidean, standardized, Mahalanobis, and Gower distances of Definition 27.4, the Mahalanobis whitening of Theorem 27.1 being the scale-invariant choice (Theorem 27.2). As Chapter 27 stresses, these are diagnostics for closeness, not certificates of validity (Proposition 27.4).

E.6 Using the three packages together

The packages partition the problem by network type, and that partition is the decision rule for which to load.

Network Method Package
Connected, anchored, IPD and AgD ML-NMR multinma
Disconnected, unanchored, two treatments ML-UMR mlumr
Unanchored, with MAIC, STC, doubly-robust, and QBA in one umbrella PAIC and QBA uitc (delegating the Bayesian fits to mlumr)

When you run several unanchored estimates on one uitc data object, read them as a panel, not a menu to pick a winner from after the fact. naive() is the unadjusted anchor point; MAIC, STC, ML-UMR under the SPFA, and the doubly-robust estimator are four adjusted routes that should land near one another if their shared assumptions hold, because they target the same marginal estimand by different means (weighting, outcome regression, a Bayesian joint model, and a combination). Broad agreement among the adjusted methods, all shifted away from naive() in the direction the covariate imbalance predicts, is reassuring. Wide disagreement is diagnostic: MAIC drifting from STC points to weight instability (check the ESS and the love plot of Section E.5.2); the doubly-robust estimate sitting apart from both suggests one of its two models is misspecified; and the relaxed ML-UMR fit moving far from spfa flags effect modification the simpler methods assume away. Pre-specify the primary method, use the others as sensitivity analyses, and report the spread.

Because mlumr deliberately reuses multinma’s function names (set_ipd(), set_agd(), add_integration(), distr(), marginal_effects()), the safe practice is one package per session, matched to the network type, or namespacing with multinma:: and mlumr:: when both must be attached. uitc consumes mlumr_data objects directly, so a single combine_data() result feeds MAIC, STC, ML-UMR, naive, doubly-robust, and the QBA sensitivity analyses in turn, which is the recommended way to run the adjusted estimate and its bias analysis side by side.
