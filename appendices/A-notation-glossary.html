Appendix A — Notation Glossary

This appendix is the reference index for the book’s notation. It has two parts. The first repeats every symbol introduced in Notation, grouped by the same topics, and adds for each symbol a one-line gloss together with the chapter that introduces it, naming the defining environment by its slug where one exists. The second is an alphabetical glossary of every formally defined term in the book; each entry gives a one-sentence statement of the term and a pointer to the chapter and the definition slug that fixes it. The conventions themselves, that bold lowercase letters denote vectors and bold uppercase letters denote matrices, that random variables are capitalized when the distinction from a realized value matters, that an unsubscripted probability refers to the population under discussion, and that \(\square\) closes a proof while \(\blacktriangleleft\) closes a definition, example, or remark, are stated in Notation and are not repeated here. Nothing in this appendix is proved; it is a single place to look up a symbol or a definition and to follow the slug to the statement that establishes it.

Throughout, “Ch.” abbreviates chapter, and a slug such as @def-effect-modifier names the cross-referenceable definition environment in the chapter where the term is established; that slug is the exact cross-reference an author would use to cite the result.

A.1 How to use this appendix

There are two ways in. If you have seen a symbol and want to know what it is, use the symbol tables of Section A.6, grouped by role from linear algebra through population adjustment. If you have heard a term and want its meaning, use the alphabetical glossary of Section A.8; the clusters in Section A.7 gather related terms so a whole family can be read at once. In either place the final column names the chapter and, where a formal definition fixes the item, the slug of that definition. That slug is the cross-reference key that takes you to the formal definition in the chapter that establishes it. The chapter legend in Table A.1 turns each “Ch.” number into its subject and part, so that “Ch. 14” reads as “Bucher indirect comparison,” not a bare number.

Four short aids come before the tables: a compact key to the conventions (Section A.2), a worked notation key that maps the symbols onto a single anchored comparison (Section A.3), a table of symbols that carry more than one meaning (Section A.4), and a plain-language note on effect scales (Section A.5).

A.2 A compact key to the conventions

The full conventions live in Notation; the few a reader most often needs are repeated here, since a reader who reaches for a glossary is usually already unsure. Bold lowercase letters (\(\mathbf{x}\), \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)) are vectors; bold uppercase letters (\(\mathbf{X}\), \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)) are matrices; plain letters are scalars. A capital letter is a random variable and the matching lowercase letter is a value it takes: \(X\) is the random covariate, \(x\) a realized covariate value, \(\mathbf{x}\) the covariate vector for one patient, and \(\mathcal{X}\) the set of all possible covariate values. A probability \(P(\cdot)\) with no subscript refers to the population under discussion. The index letters are fixed: \(i\) is a patient, \(j\) a study, \(k\) an arm, and \(a,b,c\) name treatments, so \(\eta_{ijk}\) reads “patient \(i\), study \(j\), arm \(k\).” The symbol \(\square\) closes a proof and \(\blacktriangleleft\) closes a definition, example, or remark.

A.3 A worked notation key: one anchored comparison

To pin the symbols to a familiar picture, read them off a single indirect comparison. Treatments \(A\) and \(B\) were never studied head to head. An \(AC\) trial compares \(A\) against a common comparator \(C\); a separate \(BC\) trial compares \(B\) against the same \(C\). A.5 A note on effect scales

Many entries below turn on two distinctions that run through the whole book, so they are worth stating plainly once. The first is marginal versus conditional. A conditional effect \(\tau^g_{ab}(x)\) compares treatments within a fixed covariate value \(x\); a marginal effect compares treatments after averaging outcomes over a whole population. On a non-collapsible scale, such as the odds ratio, these two differ even when nothing is confounded, which is exactly why the book keeps them apart. The second is outcome scale versus link scale. The outcome scale is the natural scale of the response, a probability, a rate, or a survival time; the link scale, also called the linear-predictor scale, is the scale on which the model is linear, reached by applying the link \(g\) (for a probability, the log-odds). Relative effects such as \(d_{ab(\mathcal{P})}\) live on a stated scale, and odds ratios, risk ratios, risk differences, and hazard ratios are not interchangeable: an assumption true on one scale can fail on another. The book names the scale whenever it matters; the defined terms @def-scale-alignment, @def-collapsibility, and @def-marginal-effect make the point precisely.

A.9 Notes and sources

The source for this appendix is the book itself: the symbol tables reproduce Notation, which is the single source of truth for the symbols and conventions, extended by one table (Bayesian computation and model comparison, Table A.8) that gathers symbols from Chapters 8, 15, and 24 not yet listed there; the term glossary is assembled from the definition environments of Chapters 1 through 30. Where a symbol carries a formal definition, the slug in the “Introduced” column is that definition; where a term is glossed, the slug in the “Defined in” column links to its statement.
